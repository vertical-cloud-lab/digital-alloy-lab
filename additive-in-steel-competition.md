# America Makes Additive in Steel (AIS) competition: past editions and the 2027 brief

Notes for [issue #268](https://github.com/vertical-cloud-lab/digital-alloy-lab/issues/268), compiled
2026-11-18. The live competition page
([americamakes.us/upcoming-events/additive-in-steel-competition](https://www.americamakes.us/upcoming-events/additive-in-steel-competition/))
puts automated clients behind a Cloudflare check, so this is built from Wayback Machine copies of the
America Makes and SFSA pages, the PDFs those pages link, and trade-press coverage (sources at the end).
The newest copy of the America Makes page that could be read is from 2026-05-14, so anything posted
for 2027 since then (judging weights, dates, size limit) is **not** reflected here, except the 2027
testing criteria quoted in the issue.

## Summary

- **What it is.** A student design-and-build competition run by America Makes (the AFRL-funded national
  AM institute, managed by NCDMM). Teams of up to 8 (at least one enrolled university student), with a
  faculty sponsor and ideally an industry partner, additively manufacture a steel tool, write a
  technical report, make a ≤5-minute video, and demo the tool live in a timed test. Prizes in 2025
  and 2026 were **$4,000 / $2,000 / $1,000**.
- **Three editions so far.** 2024: a **Halligan bar**, run by SFSA alongside Cast in Steel and Forged in
  Steel (Colorado School of Mines won). 2025 and 2026: an **everyday-carry (EDC) multi-tool** no larger
  than 3.5 in × 6.5 in when collapsed (University of Louisville won 2025; UTEP won 2026). 2027 keeps
  the multi-tool and changes the test list (see [below](#2027-testing-criteria-compared-with-2025-and-2026)).
- **It grew quickly.** 5 teams from 3 schools in 2025, then 15 teams from 11 schools in 2026. The
  2027 announcement quoted in the issue says 14 teams have already signed up.
- **Both multi-tool winners printed 316-series stainless (316/316L) on an EOS M290** and won on
  design-for-AM, process documentation and a clean live demo, not on metallurgy. Live performance is
  only 20–25 % of the score. The 2026 results sheet lists only 6 of 15 teams as completing every test,
  and the 2nd-place team is not among them.
- **2027 shifts the tests toward pliers, a real cutting edge, an awl and a file:** a spring clamp,
  pulling 16-gauge wire, a 50 lb zip tie, a surprise fruit or vegetable, punching leather, smoothing
  PVC. Soft 316L is a poor fit for the cutting and punching tests, which makes hardenable steels and
  heat treatment (the "Manufacturing & Process" category) a place a materials-focused team could
  stand out.
- **No BYU team** appears in the 2025 or 2026 results.

### Standing rules (2026 text)

- Any steel or stainless steel suitable for AM, by any one or more metal AM processes.
- Heat treatment allowed, but the report must explain why it was chosen.
- Bolts allowed for assembly. In 2025 teams also used off-the-shelf hardware and some non-steel
  material (Louisville's off-the-shelf blade, held in place with DED Inconel), with steel as the
  primary material.
- Report: purpose statement, history of the multi-tool, why this design, design process, compliance
  (dimensions, weight, functions) in a one-page executive summary, plus suggested metallurgy, AM
  parameters, post-processing and inspection. Mandatory cover-page template.
- Optional high-school team member, who then adds a 2-page "what I learned" section.
- Teams are encouraged to interview first responders, military personnel and service technicians.
  The 2025 theme came from "a real-world need to redesign a multi-tool for field servicing in defense
  applications", and the 2025 designs were to be shared with airmen at Tinker AFB.

## Results by year

### 2024: Halligan bar (All in Steel)

- Tested in prying, punching, twisting and striking. Teams could depart from the traditional Halligan
  design to improve utility.
- **1st: Colorado School of Mines, "Bar None"** (six mechanical engineering students and one
  metallurgical and materials engineering student). Other placings were not found. Louisville also
  entered a printed Halligan bar.

### 2025: EDC multi-tool v1 (Forge Fair, Cleveland)

| Place | Team | Design notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1st ($4,000) | **Redbird Tinkerer Tribe**, University of Louisville | **316 stainless, LPBF on an EOS M290**. Two-piece **print-in-place** plier with a dovetail pivot: tool clearance was tuned on test plates and settled at about 350 µm. Every tool sits on the perimeter of a flat shape (hammer, flat-head/pry, hex wrench, wire cutter, socket receiver, seatbelt cutter, bottle opener, Allen key, Phillips/glass breaker). Lattice lightweighting (Materialise), SolidWorks FEA, PLA prototypes first. Off-the-shelf blade captured with **DED (AddUp) Inconel**. Fought warping from residual stress, which distorted the hex and socket receiver; the team thought a stress relief on the plate would have helped. **Sandblasting dulled the blade, so it failed the seatbelt test**, and it still won. Judges cited "minimized moving parts for a simple user experience" and a streamlined AM workflow. |
| 2nd ($2,000) | **ArtiSlicers**, Colorado School of Mines | Slab-style body with an ornate TPMS-like lattice and a hook seatbelt cutter. One tool arm does every screwing task; it takes **printed bits or any standard 1/4" hex bit**. Emphasis on part-count reduction. |
| 3rd ($1,000) | **The Powder Pros**, Grand Valley State | Topology optimization and generative design inside a gyroid lattice. Parts printed by an industry partner (Next Chapter Manufacturing), since GVSU has no metal PBF. **Seatbelt cutter failed.** |
| — | Slicing Units; Jack of All Tools (both GVSU) | Team clips in the Cool Parts Show (not tied to a team name) describe a Leatherman Skeletool-inspired folding plier (minor trouble with the seatbelt cutter, and needed an extension for the Allen head) and a two-pivot fold-out design. One GVSU team built by metal material extrusion and had to simplify its design to suit the machine. One GVSU tool in the event photos has polymer side scales. |

The live test was timed and run by one person per team: remove a screw, a bolt and a socket-head cap
screw; snip wire; cut a seatbelt; pry a paint can; break glass; open a bottle.

- **Listed as completing every test (6 of 15):** UTEP, Mines, Louisville (Aviary), GVSU (Layer By
  Layer), Vermont State, New Haven. New Haven is on that list and still ranked outside the top 10,
  and 2nd-place Cardinal Steelworks is not on it. The report, video and design categories decide most

- **Fastest:** Aviary Manufacturing 1:47; Just Press Print 2:21; M.E.T.A.L. 2:51.

  **Top manufacturing process:** M.E.T.A.L., Borderland Steel, Cardinal Steelworks, Layer By Layer.
- **Winner (UTEP Borderland Steel, W.M. Keck Center for 3D Innovation):** a team of 8 students and
  staff. They interviewed machinists and engineers, had each member sketch a concept, and prototyped
  in PLA. Built in **316L on the Keck Center's own EOS M290**, sliced in Materialise Magics. It
  started as about 20 parts and was consolidated to about 5 using lattices and standardized interfaces.
  Holes printed rough and undersized, so they printed test coupons of hole diameters and 45° internal
  transitions. The first full build failed with a recoater problem that the team traced to the
  supports. Parts were cut off the plate by wire EDM, then sandblasted; supports were removed with drills, pliers and a lathe. The
  tool hammers, breaks glass, drives PH2 and flat-head screws, has an Allen key, a pry, a 1/4" socket,
  a seatbelt cutter, pliers and a wire cutter, plus **a light and a pen** as the creativity feature.
  The team has been invited back for 2027.
- **2nd and 3rd, from the event photos:** Cardinal Steelworks built a plier-style tool with lattice
  handles and a hammer head, in the same line as Louisville's 2025 winner. M.E.T.A.L. built a
  slab-style folding tool with vented scales, in the same line as Mines' 2025 entry.
- **Sponsor and partners:** SFSA sponsored. SSAB, 3D Systems, EOS, Next Chapter Manufacturing and
  Würth Additive directly supported teams.
- **Judges (by organization):** TBGA (AM qualification), Tinker AFB's REACT (Reverse Engineering and
  Critical Tooling) group (two judges), naval aviation (NAVAIR) AM, Siemens Energy, TLG Additive.
  The 2025 panel was TLG, Xact Metal, Additive Manufacturing Media and Siemens Energy. The end users on the panel come from
  DoD sustainment.

### Patterns across the winners

1. **Fewer parts, fewer moving parts.** Both multi-tool winners consolidated parts aggressively (2
   pieces in 2025, about 5 in 2026) and used print-in-place joints or plier action instead of many
   fold-out blades.
2. **Plain 316L on an EOS M290**, which is what the university AM centers already had. Nobody who
   placed is reported to have used a hardenable steel, so there is no evidence yet of a metallurgy-led
   entry.
3. **Prototype in PLA, then print coupons** of the risky features (pivot clearance, hex and hole
   sizes) before the full build. The common LPBF problems were warping, undersized holes and hex
   features, and support removal.
4. **The cutting edge is the usual failure.** Seatbelt cutters failed or struggled for at least three
   of the five 2025 teams, including the winner, whose blade was dulled by sandblasting. Many 2025
   teams used an off-the-shelf blade rather than a printed one.
5. **Documentation counts for most of the score.** Report, video, design and process are 75–80 % of
   it. New Haven finished every test and still ranked outside the top 10.

## 2027 testing criteria compared with 2025 and 2026

The 2025 and 2026 test lists were identical
([`Additive-In-Steel-Competition-Testing-Criteria.pdf`](https://web.archive.org/web/20251217141955/https://www.americamakes.us/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Additive-In-Steel-Competition-Testing-Criteria.pdf)).
The 2027 list comes from the announcement quoted in the issue.

| Test | 2025–26 | 2027 | What it asks of the tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| Remove Phillips #2 screw | ✓ | ✓ | Driver tip or bit. LPBF tips need to be hard enough not to cam out and round off, and finished to size: a flat-head that was too thick failed in 2025. |
| Remove 1/4" flat-head screw | ✓ | ✓ | as above |
| Tighten 1/4" hex-head bolt | ✓ | — | |
| Remove 1/4" socket-head cap screw (Allen) | ✓ | — | |
| Connect and use 1/4" socket extension | ✓ | — | |
| **Receive 1/4" hex-shank bits** | — | **new** | A 1/4" hex (6.35 mm across flats) bit holder, ideally with a magnet or ball detent. Print it undersized and broach or ream to size, since printed hexes came out undersized and distorted. |
| **Squeeze and remove 1/4" spring clamp** | — | **new** | Pliers. A spring clamp's tabs need jaw grip and enough leverage. |
| **Grip and pull 16-gauge wire** | — | **new** | Serrated plier jaws, which can be printed directly. |
| Pry: paint-can lid | ✓ (with staple) | ✓ | Pry tip |
| Pry: bottle cap | ✓ | ✓ | Opener notch |
| Hammer 2" framing nail | ✓ | ✓ | Mass plus a striking face that won't mushroom. In the 2026 event photos, nails go into a wood block held in a vise. |
| **Smooth a rough PVC edge** | — | **new** | A file or rasp. This plays to AM, since teeth can be printed directly, and could also be the creativity feature. |
| **Punch a hole through fabric or leather** | — | **new** | An awl point. Hard and sharp, so 316L will blunt. |
| Snip wire | ✓ | — (now "grip and pull") | |
| Cut seatbelt | ✓ (passenger-safe) | ✓ | Shielded hook blade. This was the most common failure in 2025. |
| **Cut a 50 lb zip tie** | — | **new** | Blade or flush cutter |
| **Cut a surprise item ("fruit or vegetable")** | — | **new** | A real knife edge of some length, or a saw. The heading "Cut/Snip/Saw" suggests any of these counts. |
| Break tempered glass | ✓ | — | |

What changes: the socket, wrench and glass-breaker functions are gone. In their place are **pliers**
(clamp and wire), **three cutting tests**, an **awl** and a **file**. Hard edges and points now matter
far more than in 2025–26, when a soft 316L tool could pass almost everything except the seatbelt.

### Steels worth comparing

These are typical datasheet and literature ranges, to confirm with the print partner.

| Steel | Usual AM route | Hardness after heat treatment | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 316L | LPBF (the default at most university centers) | ~200–250 HV as built, well under 30 HRC | What both multi-tool winners used. Forgiving to print and corrosion-proof, but holds no edge, and a striking face or awl will deform. |
| 17-4PH | LPBF, binder jet | ~40–44 HRC (H900) | Widely available. LPBF 17-4 can hold retained austenite, so the solution and aging steps matter, and they are a good report topic. |
| Maraging 300 (e.g. EOS MS1) | LPBF | ~50–54 HRC aged | Aging at about 490 °C distorts little. Good for jaws, awl, striking face and blade. Contains about 9 % Co; a Co-free maraging steel would tie into the lab's critical-minerals theme. |
| 420 stainless | LPBF, binder jet | ~50+ HRC, hardened and tempered | Knife steel. Needs care against cracking in LPBF. |
| H13 / tool-grade stainless (e.g. EOS StainlessSteel CX) | LPBF (H13 usually with a heated plate), DED | ~48–52 HRC | Tooling grades |

Graded hardness is another route. The rules allow several AM processes and bolted assemblies, so a
tough body could be paired with a harder printed or DED-clad edge and awl. Louisville already used
DED on a second alloy in 2025.

## If a VCL / BYU team entered

- **Eligibility:** up to 8 members with at least one enrolled student, a faculty sponsor, and an
  industry partner (America Makes offers to find one). A capstone or club team would fit.
- **Printing:** BYU's Concept Laser M2 was down as of
  [`alloy-workflow-review.md`](alloy-workflow-review.md), so the build would likely go through an
  industry partner, as GVSU's went through Next Chapter Manufacturing in 2025, or through a
  university partner, such as the University of Utah Aconity MIDI used for FLAIME, if it can run a
  steel build. Expect at least two builds: a coupon plate for pivots, hexes and edges, then the
  tool.
- **Timeline:** if 2027 repeats 2026, the plan is due in mid-February and the tool, report and video
  around 1 April. That matches RAPID + TCT, 12–15 April 2027 in Detroit, but America Makes has not
  confirmed the venue. Past teams did the build in about 2 months, February to April.
- **Where the lab's strengths score:** the 25 % *Manufacturing & Process* category asks for
  metallurgical choices, AM parameters and post-processing. Thermo-Calc and CALPHAD for heat-treatment
  design (aging response, retained austenite, distortion) and data-driven parameter selection would
  give a story no previous entrant has told. A lab-atomized custom steel from the AMAZEMET rePOWDER
  would be unique, but adds a lot of risk on this timeline. "Any steel suitable for AM" seems to allow
  it; confirm with the organizers.
- **Why bother:** the prize money is small, but the judges are DoD sustainment end users (Tinker AFB
  REACT, NAVAIR) and AM industry people, and it builds a relationship with America Makes, which runs
  the powder and alloy calls the lab is already tracking (PADAM 2.0, AIM-4AM in
  [`alloy-workflow-review.md`](alloy-workflow-review.md)).
