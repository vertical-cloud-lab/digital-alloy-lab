## SAASBO from Six Expert Seeds for Aluminium Alloy Design: A Critical Review

### 1. SAASBO Initial Design Sizes and Behavior of the SAAS Prior at Small n

**Initial designs in the SAASBO paper and benchmarks.** The SAASBO algorithm (Eriksson & Jankowiak 2021; arXiv:2103.00349) defaults to a Sobol sequence for initialization (Algorithm 1 explicitly states: "If {x₁:m, y₁:m} is not provided, let x₁:m be a Sobol sequence") (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 3-4). The published benchmarks used m = 10 initial points for the runtime comparison (100D), m = 20 for the 388D SVM problem, and m = 50 for the 124D MOPTA vehicle-design problem (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 13-14, eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 16-17). Follow-up benchmarks by Papenmeier et al. (2023; arXiv:2304.11468) used m = 10 initial samples across all optimizers including SAASBO (papenmeier2023increasingthescope pages 6-8). Hvarfner et al. (2024; arXiv:2402.02229) used m = 30 initial samples across tasks from 12D to 6392D (hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 6-7). Xu et al. (2024; arXiv:2402.02746) used m = 20 for problems up to 388D and m = 50 for the 1003D Humanoid task (xu2402standardgaussianprocess pages 8-9). No published benchmark starts SAASBO from fewer than 10 points, and none uses non-space-filling, expert-chosen initial data.

**SAAS prior behavior when n ≪ d.** The SAAS prior places half-Cauchy distributions on inverse squared length scales: ρᵢ ~ HC(τ), with global shrinkage τ ~ HC(α), where α = 0.1 by default (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 3-4). This causes most ρᵢ to concentrate near zero ("dimension off"), while the heavy tails allow dimensions with sufficient data support to escape shrinkage (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 3-4). In a controlled 100D Branin experiment (2 active dimensions embedded in 100), both NUTS and MAP correctly identified the two active dimensions after approximately 20–30 evaluations (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 5-6). On the 124D MOPTA problem, the effective subspace dimension grew adaptively from approximately 2 to 10 as evaluations accumulated (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 9-10).

With only 6 non-space-filling points in 15 dimensions, the expected behavior is: τ will remain small, most ρᵢ will stay near zero, and only 1–3 dimensions (at most) will be identified as active. The acquisition function (EI averaged over NUTS posterior samples) will then explore primarily along those few identified dimensions, effectively optimizing in a 1–3 dimensional subspace. Eriksson & Jankowiak (2021) noted that NUTS reduces EI's tendency to select domain boundaries relative to MAP, because averaging over multiple posterior modes regularizes the acquisition landscape (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 5-6). However, Hvarfner et al. (2024) proved (Proposition 4.1) that when the GP model is uninformative (K ≈ I), EI concentrates queries near the incumbent rather than at boundaries (hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 4-5, hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 5-6). Thus, the risk with 6 clustered expert points is not boundary-seeking but rather exploitative collapse near the best existing alloy, exploring along perhaps one or two dimensions that happened to show variation in the initial data.

### 2. Failure Modes of SAASBO and Comparison with Alternatives

**Over-shrinkage and missing dimensions.** The SAAS prior is adaptive: τ controls global sparsity, and dimensions can only escape shrinkage if the data provide sufficient evidence (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 3-4). With 6 clustered points where certain elements co-vary (e.g., Mn and Mg always appear together in the literature alloys), the posterior cannot distinguish their individual effects, and the SAAS prior may arbitrarily shrink one while retaining the other. This is exacerbated by collinear or correlated inputs.

**Sensitivity to outcome scaling and noise.** SAASBO was developed primarily for noise-free functions (σ² → 10⁻⁶ default) (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 3-4). Alloy experiments typically have appreciable noise, requiring a weak prior on σ². With only 6 observations, the noise variance and signal variance are poorly identified, which can destabilize NUTS and lead to divergences.

**Computational cost.** SAASBO's NUTS inference costs O(N³D) per posterior sample (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 4-5). While this is negligible at n = 6 and d = 15, Papenmeier et al. (2023) noted that SAASBO's high runtime restricted experiments to 100 evaluations per run on available hardware for higher-dimensional problems (papenmeier2023increasingthescope pages 6-8). For the proposed campaign, computational cost is not a concern.

**Vanilla BO with dimensionality-scaled priors (Hvarfner et al. 2024; arXiv:2402.02229).** This approach scales the LogNormal lengthscale prior by √D: ℓᵢ ~ LN(μ₀ + log(D)/2, σ₀), which shifts the mode and mean by √D (hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 5-6). It outperformed SAASBO on most benchmarks (Lasso-DNA 180D, SVM 388D, Humanoid 6392D), with SAASBO performing better only on MOPTA (124D), which has strong axis-aligned sparsity (hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 8-10, hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 6-7). The DSP is far simpler: it uses MAP estimation rather than full NUTS, runs in a fraction of the time, and does not impose structural assumptions on the objective (hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 6-7). For 15 dimensions, vanilla BO with a Matérn-5/2 ARD kernel and dimension-scaled priors is a strong alternative that does not require the sparsity assumption.

**Standard GP with Matérn kernel (Xu et al. 2024; arXiv:2402.02746).** Using an ARD Matérn-5/2 kernel with robust initialization (ℓ₀ = c√d) achieved top-tier performance across benchmarks from 30D to 1003D, often matching or exceeding SAASBO (xu2402standardgaussianprocess pages 1-2, xu2402standardgaussianprocess pages 9-11). The SE kernel fails due to gradient vanishing at high dimensions, but the Matérn kernel's slower tail decay (threshold τ_Matérn ≈ 21.98 vs. τ_SE ≈ 6.58) makes it trainable in much higher dimensions (xu2402standardgaussianprocess pages 4-6). At d = 15, both kernels should work, but Matérn is more robust.

**TuRBO** (Eriksson et al. 2019; arXiv:1910.01739) uses trust regions that adaptively shrink and expand based on optimization progress. It is well-suited when multiple local optima exist but requires enough initial data to seed the trust region meaningfully. **BAxUS** (Papenmeier et al. 2023; arXiv:2304.11468) adaptively expands nested random subspaces, avoiding the need to pre-specify the effective dimensionality (papenmeier2023increasingthescope pages 1-3).

### 3. Hand-Picked vs. Space-Filling Initial Points

**Expert initialization in materials BO.** Hastings et al. (2026; DOI:10.1039/d5dd00361j) systematically compared initialization strategies for alloy design BO campaigns. They found that initialization quality produced a performance difference equivalent to approximately 100 additional evaluations (hastings2026leveragingdomainknowledge pages 4-5). However, purely geometric diversity in input space (e.g., k-medoids on composition alone) provided little improvement over random sampling for output-space coverage. Only when expert-defined descriptors (subsystem identity, configurational entropy class, predicted microstructural elements) were added did initialization meaningfully improve optimization (hastings2026leveragingdomainknowledge pages 11-13, hastings2026leveragingdomainknowledge pages 3-4). This suggests that the proposed 6 expert alloys may provide good objective-space coverage if they were chosen to span different alloy families, but poor coverage if they are from the same compositional neighborhood.

**Warm-start BO.** Ruža et al. (2025; DOI:10.1021/acs.chemmater.5c01209) used 229 literature polymer-salt formulations to warm-start batch BO for polymer electrolyte discovery, achieving top-performing formulations in five batches over one month (ruza2025autonomousdiscoveryofa pages 1-4). Poloczek et al. (2016; DOI:10.1109/wsc.2016.7822140) showed that warm-starting with related task data allowed wsKG to reach ~95% of the optimum after only 10 samples (poloczek2016warmstartingbayesian pages 12-14). However, Wu et al. (2024; DOI:10.1039/d3dd00234a) cautioned that domain knowledge, if overly restrictive, can bias optimization and reduce solution diversity (wu2024racetothe pages 11-11).

**Risk of clustered expert points.** The critical issue is not whether expert points are informative—they likely are—but whether they provide the GP with sufficient spatial contrast to estimate lengthscales. Six alloys from 2–3 compositional families may provide good coverage of objective values but poor coverage of the input space, leaving the GP unable to distinguish which elements matter. Mixing a few space-filling points into an expert seed set directly addresses this by providing the deconfounding contrasts needed for model calibration.

### 4. Composition Constraints and Sparsity

**Simplex and sum constraints.** Yu et al. (2026; arXiv:2604.01328) described a bounded-simplex reparameterization for alloy BO: for n elements with sum-to-total and per-element bounds, a sequential bijective map transforms the constrained simplex into an (n−1)-dimensional unit hypercube, enabling standard BO methods to operate in an unconstrained space (yu2026efficientandprincipled pages 19-21, yu2026efficientandprincipled pages 21-23). This is directly applicable: with 15 candidate elements summing to ≤15 wt% (balance Al), reparameterize to a 14D hypercube with appropriate bounds.

**Cardinality constraints and element on/off.** When each alloy uses only 2–5 of 15 elements, most composition variables are exactly zero. This creates a naturally sparse input structure that aligns well with the SAAS assumption. However, there is a subtlety: the SAAS prior operates on GP lengthscales (dimension importance), not on input sparsity. An element that is zero in all training data provides no information for its lengthscale, so SAAS will shrink it—correctly, from the data's perspective, but potentially missing an important element that simply was not included in any expert alloy. This is the fundamental risk of combining a sparsity-inducing model with a non-space-filling initial design.

**Alloy BO campaigns.** Hastings et al. (2024; arXiv:2405.08900) used batch BO for multi-objective alloy discovery in multi-principal-element alloy systems with batches of 20 (hastings2026leveragingdomainknowledge pages 3-4). Khatamsaz et al. (2023; DOI:10.1038/s41524-023-01006-7) applied constrained multi-objective BO to the Mo-Nb-Ti-V-W system, identifying 21 Pareto-optimal alloys (khatamsaz2023bayesianoptimizationwith pages 1-2). However, no published alloy campaign using SAASBO specifically with fewer than 30 experiments was found in the literature.

### 5. Batch Selection with Delayed Feedback

With batches of 3 and results arriving weeks later, the standard BoTorch approach uses fantasization: pending points are assigned hallucinated outcomes (typically the GP posterior mean or kriging believer), and the acquisition function conditions on these fantasies when selecting additional batch members (desimpel2026bayesianoptimizationfor pages 12-13). qLogNEI (the log-transformed noisy expected improvement for q points) and qNEHVI (noisy expected hypervolume improvement) in BoTorch implement this natively.

**Batch collapse risk at small n.** With only 6 observations and a SAAS model that identifies ≤3 active dimensions, the EI landscape is extremely low-dimensional. All 3 batch candidates may cluster near the incumbent in those few active dimensions (hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 4-5, hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 5-6). Thompson sampling provides a natural safeguard: each batch member is generated by optimizing an independent posterior sample, and the inherent randomness in the posterior promotes diversity (desimpel2026bayesianoptimizationfor pages 12-13, dai2023batchbayesianoptimization pages 9-10). An alternative is to allocate one batch slot to a coverage-oriented point (e.g., the most distant feasible point from existing data).

**Delayed feedback.** Verma et al. (2022; arXiv:2206.09341) developed BO algorithms with sub-linear regret guarantees under stochastic delayed feedback, treating pending evaluations explicitly in the acquisition function (dai2022samplethenoptimizebatchneural pages 2-4). When an earlier batch is still pending while the next must be chosen, the pending points should be fantasized at their posterior means and included in the conditioning set.

### 6. Literature Data as Extra Training Rows

**Multi-task transfer.** Using literature alloy data as additional training rows in a multi-task GP is appealing but risky when real n = 6. Hvarfner et al. (2026; arXiv:2607.09073) demonstrated that standard multi-task GPs (ICM kernel) can misestimate cross-task correlation, attenuate it, or even flip its sign, causing negative transfer. The textbook MTGP lost to a target-only GP on most tested functions (hvarfner2026pitfallsandremedies pages 1-3). The identified causes are finite-sample standardization error and limited information for correlation estimation, especially with non-overlapping source and target designs. When only 6 real experiments are available, literature data can easily dominate the posterior.

**Safeguards.** Bai et al. (2023; arXiv:2302.05927) reviewed approaches including Envelope-GP (which inflates source noise variance as source-target similarity decreases) and Diff-GP (which models the discrepancy f_target − f_source) (bai2023transferlearningfor pages 13-15). Practical recommendations: (i) learn per-task means and scales rather than relying on standardization; (ii) constrain cross-task correlations to be non-negative when scientifically justified; (iii) co-locate some source and target measurements at the same compositions; (iv) use the literature data as a prior mean function rather than as literal training rows, allowing the GP to learn departures from the literature trend.

**Warm-start success case.** Ruža et al. (2025) successfully used 229 literature data points spanning 41 salts to warm-start polymer electrolyte BO, but their literature data were directly comparable measurements on the same platform type, not extracted from heterogeneous papers (ruza2025autonomousdiscoveryof pages 1-4, ruza2025autonomousdiscoveryofa pages 1-4).

### 7. Concrete Recommendation

**Recommendation: Spend the next batch (3 points) on space-filling coverage, giving 9 seeds before engaging the optimizer.** The evidence strongly supports this:

**Arguments against starting SAASBO at 6 expert seeds:**
- Every published SAASBO benchmark uses ≥10 quasi-random (space-filling) initial points, even in much higher dimensions (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 13-14, eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 16-17, papenmeier2023increasingthescope pages 6-8).
- Six clustered expert alloys cannot provide the spatial contrasts needed for ARD/SAAS lengthscale estimation. The SAAS prior will correctly shrink dimensions that show no variation in the data, but this may reflect data poverty rather than irrelevance.
- In the SAASBO 100D Branin experiment, 20–30 evaluations were needed to identify even 2 active dimensions from Sobol-initialized data (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 5-6). With 6 non-space-filling points, dimension identification will be less reliable.
- EI under a SAAS model at n = 6 will concentrate near the incumbent in 1–2 identified dimensions, producing exploitative batch collapse rather than useful exploration (hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 4-5).
- Hastings et al. (2026) showed that poor initialization quality in alloy BO produces a performance deficit equivalent to ~100 wasted evaluations—catastrophic in a 25-experiment budget (hastings2026leveragingdomainknowledge pages 4-5).

**Arguments for the 6-seed start (and why they are insufficient):**
- The expert alloys may span objective-space diversity, providing useful prior information about the response landscape.
- SAASBO's fully Bayesian treatment via NUTS provides built-in uncertainty quantification, and EI averaged over posterior samples is more robust than point-estimated alternatives (eriksson2021highdimensionalbayesianoptimization pages 5-6).
- At d = 15, SAASBO is computationally cheap, so the overhead of NUTS is minimal.
- These advantages are real but do not compensate for the fundamental lack of input-space coverage.

**Recommended protocol:**
1. Run the 6 expert alloys as planned.
2. For batch 3 (3 points), use a constrained maximin or k-medoids design that maximizes compositional diversity relative to the 6 existing points, targeting underrepresented element families, different cardinalities (e.g., if all 6 experts use 3–4 elements, include a 2-element and a 5-element alloy), and different total addition levels.
3. After receiving results for all 9 points, run the diagnostics below before engaging the optimizer.
4. For the remaining ~16 runs (≈5 batches of 3), consider using either SAASBO or vanilla BO with a Matérn-5/2 kernel and dimension-scaled LogNormal prior (Hvarfner et al. 2024). The latter is simpler, faster, and competitive at d = 15.

**Alternative model consideration.** At d = 15, the problem is not truly high-dimensional by HDBO standards. Vanilla BO with a Matérn-5/2 ARD kernel and dimension-scaled priors (ℓᵢ ~ LN(μ₀ + log(D)/2, σ₀)) is a strong alternative that avoids the sparsity assumption entirely while maintaining calibrated exploration (hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 5-6, hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 6-7). It outperformed SAASBO on most benchmarks except those with strong axis-aligned sparsity (hvarfner2402vanillabayesianoptimization pages 8-10). Run both models in parallel (computationally cheap) and compare LOO predictive performance to select the better one adaptively.
