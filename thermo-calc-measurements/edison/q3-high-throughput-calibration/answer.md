# High-Throughput Characterization and CALPHAD Calibration for a Self-Driving LPBF Aluminium Alloy Discovery Campaign

## 1. Combinatorial and Composition-Graded Metal Samples for Alloy Screening

Several complementary bulk and thin-film sample-fabrication strategies are available for mapping composition–structure–property relationships across the design space relevant to LPBF aluminium alloys. Each method excels in a different subset of measurable quantities.

**Diffusion couples and multiples (J.-C. Zhao).** Multiple pure metals or alloys are joined into a single block and annealed at high temperature (e.g. 1200 °C for 40 h), creating continuous composition gradients via interdiffusion. A single five-component multiple (Cr–Pd–Pt–Rh–Ru) generated 10 ternary isothermal sections from its trijunction regions, replacing the fabrication and characterization of several hundred equilibrated alloys (zhao2005highthroughputdiffusionmultiples pages 1-3). Measurements accessible on diffusion multiples include phase equilibria (tie-line compositions from EPMA profiles), interdiffusion coefficients, hardness and elastic modulus (via nanoindentation), thermal conductivity (time-domain thermoreflectance maps of 100 × 100 points in approximately 1 h at 2–4 µm resolution and ±8 % accuracy), thermal expansion, and specific heat (zhao2014highthroughputexperimentaltools pages 4-6, zhao2014highthroughputexperimentaltools pages 2-4, zhao2005highthroughputdiffusionmultiples pages 8-10, zhao2005highthroughputdiffusionmultiples pages 3-4). They are not suitable for liquidus/solidus determination or bulk tensile/corrosion testing.

**Dual-anneal diffusion multiples (DADMs)** extend the concept to precipitation kinetics and metastability. A first high-temperature anneal creates broad solid-solution composition ranges; after quenching and sectioning, each slice is aged at a different temperature and time, yielding composition–temperature–time datasets spanning precipitate morphology, stable/metastable solvi, and interfacial-energy estimates (zhao2020highthroughputandsystematic pages 1-2). A reported Co–Cr–Fe–Mo–Ni DADM contained 10 binary couples and 8 diffusion triples.

**Thin-film sputtered composition-spread libraries** co-deposit from multiple targets onto one substrate, creating continuous ternary or higher-order compositional spreads with hundreds to thousands of addressable positions. These are valid for automated XRD phase mapping, nanoindentation (with substrate-effect corrections), resistivity/conductivity mapping, and localized corrosion screening (decost2022towardsautomateddesign pages 1-4, decost2022towardsautomateddesign pages 4-7). However, texture, residual stress, substrate constraint, and suppressed nucleation render them less representative of bulk LPBF microstructures.

**Laser-DED compositionally graded samples** use independently metered powder feeders to vary composition along a track, wall, or array. The HT-READ framework demonstrated a 32 mm ring library containing 16 distinct alloy compositions deposited by DED, all characterizable by automated SEM/EDS/EBSD in less than 1 h using 22.5° automated ring indexing (vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 1-4, vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 4-7, vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 7-11, vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 11-13). DED graded samples are valid for rapid-solidification phase selection, cracking/printability assessment, hardness, mapped XRD, segregation analysis, corrosion, and—from larger deposits—tensile testing. The main caveat is that DED cooling rates and melt-pool dimensions differ from LPBF.

**In-situ alloyed LPBF from elemental powder blends** avoids atomizing each candidate composition by blending elemental or master-alloy powders and melting them in the LPBF melt pool. This directly tests LPBF-specific printability, cracking, porosity, rapid-solidification phases, and aging response (martucci2023developmentandcharacterisation pages 15-16, mehta2021highperformancealuminium pages 21-24, martucci2023ongoingchallengesof pages 3-4). Key challenges include powder segregation due to differing density, size, morphology, and absorptivity; incomplete homogenization; Mg or Zn evaporation; and oxygen pickup.

**Arc-melted button arrays** provide gram-scale bulk alloys suitable for DSC solidus/liquidus measurement, annealing/equilibrating for phase-fraction determination, hardness, conductivity, and corrosion. No standardized buttons-per-hour rate has been established, but automated stages can subsequently characterize arrays without individual remounting. The approach is valid for all equilibrium CALPHAD quantities but suffers from volatile-element loss and a solidification history unlike atomized powder.

## 3. Feeding Measurements Back into CALPHAD and Kinetic Models

### 3.1 ESPEI / pycalphad and Bayesian MCMC Fitting

ESPEI (Extensible Self-optimizing Phase Equilibria Infrastructure) is an open-source Python package that performs automated CALPHAD database development with uncertainty quantification. Its workflow has two stages (bocklund2024espeiforefficient pages 1-5, bocklund2024espeiforefficient pages 5-9):

1. **Parameter generation:** Single-phase thermochemical data (enthalpy, entropy, heat capacity, formation energy from DFT) are supplied as JSON files. ESPEI fits Compound Energy Formalism parameters by linear least squares with L2 regularization, selecting complexity via corrected AIC (bocklund2024espeiforefficient pages 5-9).
2. **Bayesian MCMC refinement:** Phase-equilibrium data (tie-line compositions, phase-boundary temperatures from DSC, invariant reactions) are simultaneously fitted using Markov Chain Monte Carlo via an affine-invariant ensemble sampler (emcee). New experimental DSC onset temperatures, EDS tie-line compositions, or thermochemical measurements are added as JSON dataset entries. The MCMC posterior provides parameter distributions for uncertainty quantification (bocklund2024espeiforefficient pages 13-17, bocklund2024espeiforefficient pages 9-13, bocklund2024espeiforefficient pages 1-5).

**Dataset formats** include JSON files for experimental data organized in an input_data folder, YAML configuration files for ESPEI settings, and TDB/XML database files for thermodynamic models. PyCalphad performs all thermodynamic calculations and Gibbs-energy minimization (palma2023thermodynamicmodelingwith pages 26-28, richter2026thermodynamicmodelingof pages 1-6).

**Uncertainty quantification** is derived from converged MCMC chains. Parameter uncertainties are propagated by sampling the 95% highest-probability-density parameter values and recalculating phase diagrams, yielding 95% credible intervals on predicted phase boundaries, Gibbs energies, and phase fractions (bocklund2024espeiforefficient pages 13-17, palma2023thermodynamicmodelingwith pages 10-15, honarmandi2019uncertaintyquantificationand pages 9-10).

### 3.2 Precipitation Model Calibration (KWN / TC-PRISMA / kawin)

Kampmann–Wagner Numerical (KWN) precipitation models treat interfacial energy and nucleation-site density as adjustable parameters that are calibrated by inverse optimization against experimental data. Yu et al. demonstrated that SciPy Nelder–Mead and Powell algorithms can optimize KWN parameters in TC-PRISMA, requiring as few as 3–5 measurements of mean precipitate radius versus aging time (yu2024implementingnumericalalgorithms pages 1-2). For Al–Mg–Si, the optimized interfacial energies were 79.13 mJ/m² (Nelder–Mead) and 95.62 mJ/m² (Powell), with nucleation-site densities differing by two orders of magnitude between the two local minima (yu2024implementingnumericalalgorithms pages 2-4). Kawin is identified as an open-source KWN implementation that could similarly accept these optimization workflows (yu2024implementingnumericalalgorithms pages 1-2).

Alizadeh et al. demonstrated gradient-based calibration of a differentiable KWN model for 6xxx Al alloys, using the ADAM optimizer to fit yield-strength evolution by adjusting trainable coefficients that modify interfacial energy, nucleation barrier, critical radius, growth velocity, and coarsening velocity, with sigmoid smoothing replacing conditional logic to enable backpropagation (alizadeh2025gradientbasedcalibrationof pages 7-9, alizadeh2025gradientbasedcalibrationof pages 11-12, alizadeh2025gradientbasedcalibrationof pages 5-7). Hardness or yield-strength aging curves serve as the primary calibration observable for such models, while SAXS and DSC data can provide independent constraints on precipitate size distributions and transformation enthalpies.

### 3.3 Multi-Fidelity Bayesian Approaches

Akhavan et al. demonstrated a Bayesian multi-fidelity Gaussian-process framework for aluminium alloys (Al–Cu, Al–Cu–Zn), using CALPHAD as a low-fidelity data source (1,672 thermal-conductivity predictions) fused with a small number of high-fidelity experimental measurements to produce improved surrogate models with uncertainty quantification (akhavan2025multifidelityframeworkand pages 1-3). Hu et al. proposed CALPHAD-informed MAP priors for cold-start composition-space partitioning in active alloy design, using thermodynamic phase boundaries as informative priors whose influence diminishes as experimental data accumulate (hu2026calphadinformedmappriors pages 1-3). Olson and Liu described a genomic-materials-design ecosystem in which ESPEI/pycalphad supplies probabilistic thermodynamic models, machine learning and DFT provide additional data, and inverse design connects performance requirements to alloy/processing specifications through iterative Bayesian updating (olson2024genomicmaterialsdesign pages 29-34). Adapa et al. outlined a Bayesian-network approach for metal AM, hierarchically calibrating coupled simulation models (thermal, solidification, microstructure, property) from high-throughput experiments including indentation and small punch tests (adapa2025rapiddevelopmentof pages 19-21).

## 4. Reported CALPHAD Discrepancies

### 4.1 Al–Ce System

Published Al–Ce eutectic coordinates vary substantially: reported eutectic temperatures range from 621 to 645 °C and compositions from 10 to 17.8 wt% Ce. One Thermo-Calc calculation predicted 580 °C and 2.09 at% Ce, compared with the experimental value of 660 °C and 4 at% Ce measured by Czerwinski and Amirkhiz, who determined the eutectic at 644.5 ± 0.6 °C and 10.6 wt% Ce by combined thermal analysis and metallography (czerwinski2020ontheal–al11ce3 pages 20-22). For Al–Ce–Si–Mg alloys, CALPHAD predicted the ternary AlCeSi₂ (s2) intermetallic at approximately 4.5% phase fraction, whereas experimental observation showed more than 8%; moreover, the metastable s4 (Al₂(REE)Si₂) phase—which is not included in CALPHAD databases because its stability is uncertain—was the majority intermetallic after T6 heat treatment (moore2020thermodynamicmodelingof pages 17-20, moore2020thermodynamicmodelingof pages 13-15).

### 4.2 Al–Mg–Sc–Zr System

Conventional Scheil assumptions cannot represent the beneficial effects of primary Al₃(Sc,Zr) precipitates as grain refiners because the model assumes primary precipitates do not further react with the liquid. LPBF's rapid cooling produces supersaturation beyond equilibrium solubility, and metastable Al₃Zr may form instead of stable Al₃Zr_D023. Suppressing the stable phase in Thermo-Calc predictions increases predicted Zr solubility, broadly consistent with experiments but only "to some extent" (mehta2021highperformancealuminium pages 21-24). The general challenge is that thermophysical data for liquid alloys are scarce, and equilibrium calculations are poorly suited to LPBF cooling rates exceeding 10⁵ K/s (martucci2023ongoingchallengesof pages 3-4).

### 4.3 Al–Si–Mg System

The Scheil–Gulliver model generally predicts phase selection better than equilibrium calculations for LPBF, but its assumptions of complete liquid diffusion, zero solid back-diffusion, and interfacial equilibrium remain theoretical limitations. Scheil tends to predict more segregation than actually occurs because some solid back-diffusion does take place during LPBF solidification (martucci2023developmentandcharacterisation pages 15-16, martucci2023ongoingchallengesof pages 3-4).

### 4.4 Al–Mn System

Scheil calculations predict that Al–Mn maintains an approximately 0 K solidification range because Mn precipitates early as Al₄Mn or Al₆Mn and does not become strongly enriched in the final liquid, in contrast to Al–Mg where increasing Mg expands the solidification range to approximately 200 K (mehta2021highperformancealuminium pages 21-24). Under rapid solidification (e.g. gas atomization or LPBF), Mn achieves substantial supersaturation in the α-Al matrix well beyond equilibrium solubility limits, which equilibrium or Scheil CALPHAD calculations do not capture.

### 4.5 Al–Cu and Al–Fe–Ni Systems

For Al–Ce–Cu alloys, Cu dissolved in the FCC-Al matrix was experimentally observed to migrate toward Ce-containing intermetallics rather than forming the predicted Al₂Cu or Q-Al₃Cu₂Mg₉Si₇ phases (moore2020thermodynamicmodelingof pages 17-20). No specific CALPHAD prediction errors for binary Al–Cu solidus/liquidus or Al–Fe–Ni phase equilibria were identified in the retrieved evidence, though general limitations of the TCAL database and COST 507 under rapid solidification conditions apply.

### 4.6 Cu–Al–Ni–Fe (Nickel Aluminium Bronze)

The Cu–Al–Ni–Fe system features complex intermetallic formation (κ-phases) whose phase fractions and morphologies are strongly dependent on cooling rate. While CALPHAD calculations with Thermo-Calc have been used to predict equilibrium phase constitution, the retrieved evidence did not provide specific quantified solidus/liquidus or phase-fraction discrepancies for this system beyond general observations that intermetallic formation under non-equilibrium conditions departs from predictions.
