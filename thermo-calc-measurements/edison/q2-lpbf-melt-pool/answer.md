### 1. Melt-Pool Width, Depth and Length from Single-Track Cross-Sections

**Measurement method.** Single tracks are printed on a substrate (bare plate or with a nominal powder layer) across a matrix of laser power and scan speed. Each track is sectioned transversely (and at least one longitudinally), mounted, ground, polished (to 0.04 µm colloidal silica), and chemically etched. For Al–Si alloys (e.g., AlSi10Mg), Keller's reagent (2.5 mL HNO₃, 1.5 mL HCl, 1.0 mL HF, 95 mL H₂O) or Weck's reagent is standard; for Al–Cu and Al–Mg alloys, modified Keller's or Barker's electrolytic etch can be used. For Cu alloys, aqueous FeCl₃–HCl, ammonium persulfate, or Klemm-type colour etchants reveal the melt-pool boundary. At least 3–5 transverse sections per track are recommended because melt-pool depth varies substantially along a single track, especially near the keyhole threshold (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 15-19).

**Mode classification.** The depth-to-half-width criterion from King et al. classifies melt pools: keyholing occurs when d/(w/2) > 1, or equivalently d/w > 0.5 (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 28-36, navarreteUnknownyearinfluenceofcontour pages 17-20, king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 11-15). Conduction-mode pools are approximately semi-circular with d/w < 0.5. King et al. reported a normalized-enthalpy threshold for the conduction-to-keyhole transition of ΔH/h_s ≈ 30 ± 4 for 316L stainless steel, where ΔH/h_s = (AP)/(h_s √(πDvσ³)), with A the absorptivity, P laser power, h_s the enthalpy at melting, D thermal diffusivity, v scan speed, and σ beam radius (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 15-19, king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 19-28).

**Analytical model calibration.** The Eagar–Tsai model extends Rosenthal's moving point source to a Gaussian beam on a semi-infinite solid (hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 4-5, hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 2-4). The quasi-steady temperature field is given by T(x,y,z) = T₀ + [AP/(ρcₚ(2π)^(3/2))] ∫₀^∞ g(τ′)dτ′, where g incorporates Gaussian spatial spreading. Melt-pool depth and half-width are obtained by finding the liquidus isotherm by root-finding. For calibration, absorptivity A is the primary fitting parameter; the beam radius σ should be measured independently (e.g., by beam profiler at D4σ) and fixed. Hong et al. demonstrated that fixing A independently (e.g., from Ye et al.'s closed-form correlation) converts the model from an object of calibration into one of validation; conduction-regime depths are reproduced to within a few percent, while keyhole tracks demand non-physical absorptivity values above unity (hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 4-5, hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 2-4). For Goldak double-ellipsoid parameters used in FEM/CFD, the front and rear fractions (f_f + f_r = 2) and ellipsoid dimensions are typically optimized by BOBYQA or similar derivative-free methods against measured width, depth, and length (rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsand pages 71-74).

**Thermo-Calc AM Module and open alternatives.** The Thermo-Calc AM Module uses CALPHAD-derived thermophysical properties (from its Property and Equilibrium modules) as inputs to a Scheil or equilibrium solidification path and an Eagar–Tsai-class thermal model. It predicts melt-pool geometry, solidification path, and susceptibility to cracking. The open-source printability framework of Sheikh et al. similarly uses CALPHAD data and calibrated Eagar–Tsai models, combining defect criteria with Bayesian model averaging to generate printability maps without proprietary code (sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 4-5, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 1-2).

**Open-source code.** 3DThesis (github.com/LLNL/ExaCA thermal kernel) provides open Eagar–Tsai melt-pool calculations. AdditiveFOAM is an open-source CFD solver for LPBF. The Honarmandi et al. Bayesian calibration framework for Eagar–Tsai has been described in detail (doi: 10.1016/j.addma.2021.102300).

### 2. Laser Absorptivity at ~1070 nm

**Calorimetric method (Trapp et al.).** Metal discs and ~100 µm powder layers are laser-irradiated while thermally isolated in a porous-alumina holder. The effective absorptivity is A_eff = E_abs/E_L, where E_abs = m∫C_p(T)dT is determined from the thermocouple-measured temperature rise and E_L = PL/v is the incident laser energy (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 1-2, ye2019energycouplingmechanisms pages 2-3). At low power (conduction mode), powder absorptivity is approximately twice that of polished bare discs. After melting and track consolidation, absorptivity drops, then rises sharply at the keyhole transition due to multiple reflections within the vapor cavity (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 1-2).

**Integrating-sphere radiometry (Simonds et al., NIST).** A gold-coated integrating sphere with calibrated InGaAs photodiodes measures dynamic reflectance with sub-microsecond resolution. Absorbed power is P_abs(t) = P₀(t) − [P_head(t) + P_sphere(t)], and dynamic absorptance is A(t) = P_abs(t)/P₀(t) (simonds2018timeresolvedabsorptanceand pages 5-8). This approach can resolve the precise moment of melt and keyhole formation.

**Typical values.** For pure Cu at 1070 nm: flat bulk A ≈ 0.03–0.05; Cu powder bed A ≈ 0.12–0.20 (4–5× bulk); at keyholing Cu powder absorptivity rises toward ~0.53 (gargalis2021determiningprocessingbehaviour pages 2-3, gargalis2021determiningprocessingbehaviour pages 7-8, gargalis2021determiningprocessingbehaviour pages 1-2). For common structural alloys (which may include Al alloys), flat solid absorptivity is ~30% and powder ~60%; clean bulk aluminium at 1070 nm is ~5–9% (gargalis2021determiningprocessingbehaviour pages 1-2). Uncertainty: calorimetry ±5–10% relative; integrating sphere a few percent after calibration (simonds2018timeresolvedabsorptanceand pages 5-8).

### 3. Thermal History, G, R, and Cooling Rate

**Direct methods.** Thermocouples embedded in the substrate near tracks provide indirect cooling-rate data (bandwidth-limited, typically 10³–10⁴ K/s resolution). Two-colour pyrometry and calibrated IR thermography (MWIR/SWIR high-speed cameras) can measure melt-pool surface temperatures with ±2–5% accuracy under grey-body assumptions (rahman2025acomprehensivestudy pages 19-20).

**Post-mortem proxies.** Cell or dendrite arm spacing λ relates to cooling rate Ṫ by a power law λ = A·Ṫ^(−n). The constants A and n must be determined for the specific alloy rather than imported from casting literature. For LPBF AlSi10Mg, cooling rates of 10⁵–10⁷ K/s are typical (tumulu2025laserpowderbed pages 38-42, rahman2025acomprehensivestudy pages 19-20). The local solidification rate at any point on the melt-pool boundary is R = v·cos β, where v is the scan speed and β is the angle between the scan direction and the local interface normal (bertoli2018processstructurepropertiesrelationshipsin pages 103-108). The thermal gradient G is commonly estimated from Rosenthal or Eagar–Tsai solutions at the liquidus isotherm, yielding cooling rate Ṫ = G·R (bertoli2018processstructurepropertiesrelationshipsin pages 103-108, bertoli2018processstructurepropertiesrelationshipsin pages 89-93).

**In-situ synchrotron X-ray imaging.** At facilities such as the APS 32-ID beamline, high-speed X-ray radiography (up to 1 MHz frame rate) directly images the melt pool, keyhole, solidification front, and pore dynamics during LPBF. This enables direct measurement of keyhole depth and fluctuation frequency (2.5–10 kHz), vapor-depression morphology, and solidification-front velocity (guo2024correlativespatterand pages 1-3).

### 4. Columnar-to-Equiaxed Transition, Grain Size and Texture

**EBSD methods.** Sections parallel to the build direction are prepared with final colloidal-silica or low-energy ion-beam polishing and mapped by field-emission SEM with EBSD. For Sc/Zr-modified Al alloys, the bimodal microstructure consists of fine equiaxed grains (FG) at melt-pool boundaries and coarser columnar grains (CG) in the pool centre. Lu et al. measured FG diameter of 0.45 ± 0.19 µm and CG width of 1.07 ± 0.49 µm, with equiaxed area fraction ~65% (lu2023microstructuralevaluationand pages 6-9). Step sizes of 0.05–0.2 µm are needed for submicrometre Sc/Zr-refined grains.

**CET models and G–R maps.** The ratio G/R controls morphology: high G/R favours columnar growth, low G/R favours equiaxed. The critical G/R for CET depends on nucleant density and undercooling; in Sc/Zr systems, primary Al₃(Sc,Zr) particles provide heterogeneous nucleation sites with small lattice misfit to α-Al (lu2023microstructuralevaluationand pages 9-10). Hunt's model defines a critical G below which the columnar front is blocked by equiaxed grains ahead of it, while the KGT (Kurz–Giovanola–Trivedi) model provides the dendrite-tip undercooling as a function of R. Plotted on G–R maps, CET boundaries delineate columnar, mixed, and equiaxed regions (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 6-7). Platform preheating (reducing G) and increased energy density have been shown to increase equiaxed fraction in Scalmalloy (cattano2019feasibilitystudieson pages 100-103).

### 5. Composition Change by Selective Evaporation

**Measurement.** Virgin powder and printed coupons are dissolved and analyzed by ICP-OES (per ASTM E3061 for Al alloys). At least three independent digestions per condition are recommended (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 1-2). EPMA/WDS provides spatial composition maps; SEM-EDS is inadequate for detecting ~1% relative Mg loss.

**Evaporation modelling.** The Langmuir/Hertz–Knudsen equation gives the evaporative flux for each element: J_i = α_i · a_i · p_i^sat(T) · √[M_i/(2πRT)], integrated over the melt-pool surface area and lifetime, with corrections for inert-gas shielding (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 8-10). Bärtl et al. measured 7.9% total mass loss during electromagnetic levitation of Scalmalloy at ~540 K superheat, dominated by Mg evaporation (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 8-10). For Al–Zn alloys (e.g., 7xxx series), Zn and Mg loss has been documented with ICP-OES by Li et al. (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 1-2).

### 6. Printability Maps

**Construction workflow.** (i) Single-track experiments across 20–30 power–speed combinations identify conduction, transition, and keyhole regimes and balling. (ii) Single-layer pads at candidate hatch spacings test overlap and continuity. (iii) Multi-layer cubes (5–10 mm) validate density. Boundaries: lack of fusion occurs when melt-pool depth d < layer thickness t, or when the overlap criterion (h/W)² + (t/D)² > 1 is violated; keyhole porosity warning at d/w > 0.5; balling when the melt-pool length-to-width ratio L/W ≳ 2 and the track is discontinuous (rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsand pages 51-55, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 4-5, zhang2021aprintabilityassessment pages 2-3).

**Density measurement.** Archimedes method (ASTM B962) provides bulk density but can exceed 100% due to wetting and uncertain theoretical density (zhang2021aprintabilityassessment pages 7-9). Optical image analysis (ImageJ on ≥3 polished planes, per ASTM E1245) gives area-percent porosity; this method was preferred by Zhang et al. for its lower variability (zhang2021aprintabilityassessment pages 7-9). Micro-CT distinguishes irregular LOF pores from rounded keyhole pores.

**Automated frameworks.** The Sheikh et al. framework uses CALPHAD-derived properties, Eagar–Tsai predictions, and Bayesian model averaging across 12 defect-criterion combinations to produce probabilistic printability maps for any alloy with CALPHAD data (sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 4-5, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 1-2).

### 7. Residual Stress in Small LPBF Coupons

**XRD sin²ψ.** A laboratory diffractometer (e.g., Stresstech G3) with appropriate radiation (Cr-Kα for Al (111)/(311), or Mn-Kα for Ni alloys) measures lattice spacing d at multiple ψ tilts (typically 11 angles spanning ±45°). Stress is calculated from the slope of d vs. sin²ψ using X-ray elastic constants (rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsand pages 137-140, mohammadtaheri2025arobustfinite pages 102-106). Typical uncertainty for Al is ±20–50 MPa; texture, coarse grains, and uncertain stress-free d₀ can dominate.

**Bridge/cantilever deflection.** Alligator- or bridge-shaped cantilevers are printed, then partially released by wire EDM. The resulting deformation is measured by DIC or profilometry and compared with thermo-mechanical FE predictions (bertoli2018processstructurepropertiesrelationshipsin pages 117-122). This approach is pragmatic for small desktop-scale coupons.

**Hole drilling.** Incremental drilling with strain gauges or ESPI (per ASTM E837) resolves depth-dependent stress to ~250 µm (rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsand pages 137-140). The contour method (wire EDM cut, surface displacement by CMM or profilometry) provides a 2D stress map but requires larger specimens.

**Neutron diffraction.** Provides through-thickness lattice strain at millimetre spatial resolution (per ISO 21432) but requires beam time at a neutron facility (bertoli2018processstructurepropertiesrelationshipsin pages 113-117).

### 8. Spatter and Vapour Plume

**High-speed imaging.** Side-view cameras at 5–60 kfps (e.g., Phantom V2512, Fastcam 1024 PCI at 6 kHz for AlSi10Mg) with narrow-band illumination (e.g., CAVILUX HF, Lumencor SOLA) capture spatter trajectories, velocities, and counts (li2022areviewof pages 7-8). Schlieren imaging uses a collimated source, knife edge, and high-speed camera to visualize vapour-plume density gradients and morphology (liu2023reviewofvisual pages 23-25, shi2024areviewof pages 8-10).

**Relevance for Al.** In Mg-bearing Al alloys, vigorous Mg vaporization produces increased spatter, denudation, and oxide-rich fume (MgO) that can contaminate the powder bed and attenuate the laser (li2022areviewof pages 13-15, shi2024areviewof pages 6-8). Guo et al. used synchrotron X-ray imaging at ESRF to correlate vapour-depression morphology with spatter dynamics in an Al–Fe–Zr alloy, showing that conduction-mode depressions produce backward spatter while keyhole rear walls redirect spatter vertically (guo2024correlativespatterand pages 1-3).

### 9. Powder Properties

**Particle size distribution.** Laser diffraction (ISO 13320) provides volume-weighted PSD; dynamic image analysis (ISO 13322) gives number-weighted PSD and shape parameters. Report D10, D50, D90, and span (gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 25-26, brika2020influenceofparticle pages 5-8).

**Flowability.** Hall funnel (ASTM B213) and Carney funnel (ASTM B964) measure flow time; many fine Al powders show "no flow" through a Hall funnel. The FT4 powder rheometer provides dynamic flow energy, specific energy, and aeration response under conditions more representative of recoating (brika2020influenceofparticle pages 5-8, gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 14-16). Rotating-drum analysers (GranuDrum, Revolution Analyzer) measure avalanche angle at recoater-relevant speeds (gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 14-16). Combine multiple techniques (lorenzo2021metallicpowderflowability pages 9-10).

**Density and chemistry.** Apparent density (ASTM B212), tap density (ASTM B527), and Hausner ratio HR = ρ_tap/ρ_bulk characterize packing (lorenzo2021metallicpowderflowability pages 9-10). Oxygen and hydrogen content are measured by inert-gas fusion (ASTM E1019 for O/N, ASTM E1447 for H in applicable materials) using a LECO-type analyzer (brika2020influenceofparticle pages 5-8, wadia2023qualifying174ph pages 20-25). Moisture is assessed by Karl Fischer titration or TGA.
