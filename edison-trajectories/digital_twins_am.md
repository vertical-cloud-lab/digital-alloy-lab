# State of the Art in Digital Twins and Physics-Based Simulation for Metal Additive Manufacturing (LPBF): 2022–2026

## 1. Thermal and Melt Pool Simulation

### 1.1 Analytical and Semi-Analytical Models

Analytical heat-source models remain widely used for rapid parameter screening in LPBF. The Rosenthal point/line-source solution provides near-instantaneous temperature estimates (<1 s per track) but neglects fluid flow, temperature-dependent properties, and keyholing, yielding accuracy of only ±30–50% (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 16-18). The Goldak double-ellipsoid volumetric heat source improves fidelity to ±20–30% accuracy at modest cost (~10 min per track), though it still requires empirical calibration and cannot predict defects (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 16-18). These models remain useful for first-pass process mapping but are insufficient for defect-critical applications.

### 1.2 Finite Element and Finite Volume Methods

Continuum FEM/FVM approaches with conduction-based thermal analyses represent the most common simulation framework for LPBF. Modern implementations incorporate temperature-dependent thermophysical properties, dynamic laser absorptance, phase-dependent density/enthalpy, and enhanced thermal conductivity to approximate Marangoni convection (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 18-19, trejostaborda2022finiteelementmodeling pages 5-8). Part-scale scanwise simulation has been enabled through GPU-based matrix-free FEM with adaptive remeshing (teymouri2022simplifiedmultiscalemodeling pages 24-29). Reported melt pool dimensional errors for well-calibrated FEM models range from 2.3% (width) to 7.1% (depth) for 316L stainless steel, with one study achieving a relative error of 2.92% with a standard deviation of ±7.21 µm using a new effective width criterion (trejostaborda2022finiteelementmodeling pages 1-5, trejostaborda2022finiteelementmodeling pages 5-8). Peak temperature predictions have been validated to within ~3% of experimental measurements (trejostaborda2022finiteelementmodeling pages 1-5).

### 1.3 High-Fidelity CFD and Multiphysics Models

High-fidelity thermo-fluid CFD models using Volume of Fluid (VOF) or Level Set interface tracking explicitly resolve Marangoni convection, recoil pressure, evaporation, and phase change. These models achieve approximately ±10–20% accuracy for melt pool predictions and can capture keyhole dynamics, spatter generation, and micro-void formation (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 16-18, teymouri2022simplifiedmultiscalemodeling pages 24-29). However, they remain computationally prohibitive for multi-track, multi-layer simulations; neglecting Marangoni flow alone can underpredict melt pool depth by up to 50% (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 16-18). Tools such as FLOW-3D AM and ALE3D are commonly employed for this class of simulation (leon2025areviewof pages 18-19, mukalay2024selectivelasermelting pages 29-30).

### 1.4 Lattice Boltzmann and Particle-Based Methods

Lattice Boltzmann methods (LBM), often coupled with discrete element methods (DEM), capture powder-scale dynamics, melt pool free-surface behavior, and mesoscale porosity formation (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 64-66, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 28-29). DEM-LBM and DEM-SPH couplings improve predictive capability for powder–fluid and vapor–melt interactions. GPU acceleration and adaptive refinement enable higher-resolution mesoscale simulations, but scalability to part-scale remains a fundamental limitation (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 28-29, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 26-28).

## 2. Residual Stress and Distortion Prediction

### 2.1 Thermo-Mechanical FEA

Full thermo-mechanical FEA uses sequentially coupled thermal–mechanical analyses with element-activation techniques, nonlinear thermoelastoplastic constitutive laws, and adaptive meshing to resolve layerwise heating/cooling cycles (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 21-23). These simulations can predict detailed residual stress distributions and deformation fields but are extremely computationally demanding—complex parts may require years of compute time with conventional approaches (mohammadtaheri2022inherentstrainapproach pages 1-2). Commercial packages such as ANSYS Additive, Simufact Additive, Abaqus, and COMSOL implement these workflows (johnson2024monitoringmodelingand pages 27-29, leon2025areviewof pages 17-18).

### 2.2 Inherent Strain Methods

The inherent strain (IS) method, originally developed for welding, has been extensively adapted for LPBF. It extracts effective plastic strains from high-fidelity simulations or experiments and applies them at part scale through elastic analyses, delivering 6–100× speedups while maintaining prediction accuracy within ~10–15% for residual stress and distortion (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 21-23). Enhanced variants account for temperature-dependent mechanical properties, anisotropic scan-strategy effects, and shear-strain components (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 21-23, pourabdollah2024anewvariant pages 1-2). A recent variant by Pourabdollah et al. (2024) explicitly accounts for both compressive plastic strain adjacent to the melt pool and the changing macroscale thermal field, reporting in-plane compressive strains on the order of 10⁻³, while noting that some simplified models have underestimated distortion with average errors around 22% (pourabdollah2024anewvariant pages 1-2). Commercial tools including MSC Simufact, Autodesk Netfabb, and Amphyon implement IS-based workflows (park2022anewapproach pages 1-4).

### 2.3 Calibration and Validation

Both thermo-mechanical FEA and IS methods are routinely validated against synchrotron and neutron diffraction, X-ray diffraction, digital image correlation, and optical 3D scanning, with errors typically reported below ~15% for calibrated models (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 21-23, oyedeji2025laserpowderbed pages 258-261, oyedeji2025laserpowderbed pages 63-67). Preheating strategies (e.g., substrate heating to 200–300°C) have been simulated to show substantial reductions in thermal gradients and residual stresses (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 21-23).

## 3. Microstructure Simulation

### 3.1 Cellular Automaton (CA) Models

CA methods are rule-based, grid-efficient approaches well-suited for grain-scale and multi-layer simulations. They are computationally far more efficient than phase-field methods—a benchmark showed CA requiring ~6 hours versus ~255 days for phase-field on a comparable selection map on a desktop computer (zhi2024reviewoncellular pages 4-6). CA models have been tightly coupled with FEM/FD thermal models to predict location-specific grain structures, columnar-to-equiaxed transitions, and texture evolution in alloys including IN625, Ti-6Al-4V, and 316L (zinovieva2023areviewof pages 9-11, zhi2024reviewoncellular pages 7-10). Non-equilibrium CA variants incorporate kinetic undercooling and interface non-equilibrium effects for rapid solidification conditions characteristic of LPBF (zhi2024reviewoncellular pages 7-10). ExaCA provides an open-source, performance-portable exascale CA implementation (johnson2024monitoringmodelingand pages 37-38).

### 3.2 Phase-Field (PF) Models

Phase-field methods offer the highest physics fidelity for mesoscale microstructure simulation, capturing dendritic growth, solid–liquid interface stability, nucleation, solute diffusion, primary dendrite arm spacing (PDAS), microsegregation, and texture evolution (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 7-11). PF is often coupled with CALPHAD thermodynamic databases to model multi-component alloy systems such as Inconel 718 and Ti-6Al-4V, enabling simulation of phase transformations (e.g., β→α/α′) and multiphase behavior (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 7-11). However, PF remains limited to mesoscale volumes (~10–100s µm) due to extreme computational cost (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 7-11, zhi2024reviewoncellular pages 4-6). State-of-the-art practice integrates multi-physics inputs from CFD/FEM, stress coupling, adaptive meshing, GPU parallelization, and ML acceleration to enable larger 3D simulations (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 7-11, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 20-21).

### 3.3 Kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC)

KMC methods, particularly the Potts model implemented in SPPARKS, are used for on-lattice stochastic modeling of grain growth and microstructural evolution (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 30-33, johnson2024monitoringmodelingand pages 37-38). While computationally efficient, KMC implementations often cannot fully capture crystallographic orientations or predict texture and may fail to reproduce certain physical mechanisms (zinovieva2023areviewof pages 9-11).

### 3.4 CALPHAD-Coupled Models

CALPHAD methodology is increasingly integrated with PF and CA to predict equilibrium and non-equilibrium phases under the extreme cooling rates of LPBF. Applications include constructing tailored TTT diagrams for AM-specific conditions and predicting solidification pathways, microsegregation, and secondary phase precipitation in Ni-based superalloys and titanium alloys (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 7-11). ML surrogates trained on PF/CALPHAD data have been reported to achieve 2–3% errors while enabling near-instantaneous inference (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 20-21).

## 4. Digital Twin Frameworks

### 4.1 Architecture and Sensor Integration

State-of-the-art digital twin (DT) architectures for LPBF are layered systems that fuse physics-based simulation, real-time multi-modal sensing, and AI (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 34-35, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 2-5). A dedicated data layer ingests high-rate sensor streams from photodiodes, pyrometers, high-speed cameras, infrared thermal cameras, and acoustic sensors at rates up to 20 kHz, using edge computing with standardized protocols (OPC-UA/MQTT) to reduce latency (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 34-35). Multi-sensor fusion enables high-accuracy defect detection—reported at 98.5% accuracy and 95% probability of detection—supporting closed-loop PBF operation (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 42-43).

### 4.2 Real-Time Model Updating

Bayesian calibration provides probabilistic, layer-wise parameter updating based on in-situ feedback, while hybrid physics–data models (PINNs, reduced-order models, or neural operators) serve as fast surrogates for real-time inference (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 34-35, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 43-44). Surrogate models yield runtime speedups of 10–3900× with ≥97% accuracy, making rapid updates feasible for intra-layer control (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 42-43, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 43-44). The formulation y_e(x) = y_s(x,θ) + δ(x) captures model bias δ(x) and parameter uncertainty θ, with in-situ data used to reduce both (ozel2023areviewon pages 27-29).

### 4.3 Closed-Loop Process Optimization

Closed-loop implementations include model predictive control (MPC) for thermal tracking and laser power modulation, reinforcement learning (RL) for adaptive parameter tuning, and Bayesian optimization for time-series process optimization (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 43-44, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 42-43, karkaria2024towardsadigital pages 2-3). Karkaria et al. (2024) demonstrated a DT framework using LSTM-based Bayesian surrogates with Bayesian Optimization for Time-Series Process Optimization (BOTSPO), dynamically optimizing laser power profiles to achieve desired mechanical properties in DED parts (karkaria2024towardsadigital pages 2-3). Kavas et al. (2025) implemented Bayesian optimization for automated in-layer PI controller tuning in LPBF, significantly reducing overheating in controlled specimens (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 45-47). Most demonstrations remain at laboratory or prototype technology readiness levels, with industrial scalability and latency reporting still limited (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 34-35, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 2-5).

## 5. Reduced-Order Models and ML Surrogates

### 5.1 Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

PINNs embed governing PDEs into neural network loss functions via automatic differentiation, enabling temperature field prediction with limited labeled data (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 19-21). Sequential PINN strategies for multi-track LPBF scenarios maintain MAPE below 2.5% while reducing training time by 8.5× relative to operator-based alternatives (safari2502physicsinformedsurrogatesfor pages 17-19). Ryan et al. (2025) demonstrated that PINNs can reduce computational time by up to 98.6% for wire-arc DED thermal histories while maintaining accuracy and offering "super-resolution" (ryan2025physicsinformedmachinelearning pages 1-3). Transfer learning–based PINNs have achieved average temperature errors below 1.3% for single-track 3D predictions (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 19-21).

### 5.2 Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)

GNN-based surrogates for LPBF thermal prediction achieve a baseline MAPE of 3.77% and can produce thermal distributions near-instantaneously compared to ~4 hours for equivalent FEA (raut2407temperaturedistributionprediction pages 1-3). Critically, GNNs learn from small-scale FEA simulations and transfer knowledge to larger domains: calibration with less than 1% of available data produced 34.3% and 10.2% drops in MAPE for 3 mm × 3 mm and 4 mm × 4 mm domains, respectively (raut2407temperaturedistributionprediction pages 1-3). Multi-laser GNN variants with Bayesian-optimized hyperparameters showed 46.4% improvement over baseline models (raut2407temperaturedistributionprediction pages 1-3).

### 5.3 Operator Learning and POD-Based ROMs

Fourier neural operators (FNO) and Deep Operator Networks (DeepONet) have been applied to build parametric surrogates for AM thermal responses, with FNO generally showing smaller mean prediction errors than DeepONet (yaseen2023fastandaccurate pages 1-3). POD-GPR frameworks for distortion prediction in LPBF achieve accuracy within ±0.001 mm with ~1800× computational speedup (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 32-32). Proper Generalized Decomposition (PGD) has achieved at least one order of magnitude speedup relative to standard FEM techniques (safari2502physicsinformedsurrogatesfor pages 1-3). Data-driven ROMs combining POD with convolutional autoencoders and MLPs have demonstrated strong correlation with full thermo-mechanical FEA results for AM-built parts (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 32-32).

The LPBF simulation ecosystem spans multiple commercial and open-source platforms. The most widely used commercial tools include ANSYS Additive Suite, Simufact Additive (Hexagon/MSC), Autodesk Netfabb, COMSOL Multiphysics, FLOW-3D AM, Dassault Simulia/Abaqus, and Altair Amphyon (mukalay2024selectivelasermelting pages 25-26, leon2025areviewof pages 18-19, mukalay2024selectivelasermelting pages 29-30, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50, leon2025areviewof pages 17-18, mukalay2024selectivelasermelting pages 26-26, mukalay2024selectivelasermelting pages 24-25). On the open-source side, OpenFOAM has been adapted for melt pool CFD with Marangoni effects, MOOSE provides a multiphysics FEM framework for AM thermal/mechanical modeling, ExaCA enables exascale cellular automata grain-structure simulation, and SPPARKS supports kinetic Monte Carlo microstructure modeling (leon2025areviewof pages 18-19, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50, johnson2024monitoringmodelingand pages 37-38, yaseen2023fastandaccurate pages 1-3). ## 7. Simulation Accuracy Compared to Experiments

The accuracy of LPBF simulations varies significantly by method class and target quantity. For melt pool geometry, well-calibrated FEM thermal models achieve 2–7% relative errors in width and depth against optical/cross-section measurements (trejostaborda2022finiteelementmodeling pages 1-5, trejostaborda2022finiteelementmodeling pages 5-8). High-fidelity CFD models targeting defect-critical predictions achieve approximately ±10–20% accuracy but at substantially higher computational cost (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 16-18). For residual stress and distortion, inherent strain methods typically achieve ~10–15% accuracy validated against diffraction measurements, while enhanced thermo-mechanical FEA can produce comparable fidelity at higher cost (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 21-23). ML surrogates report impressive metrics: PINNs achieve <3% error for 2D temperature fields, GNNs reach 3.77% MAPE, and POD-GPR ROMs predict distortions within ±0.001 mm (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 32-32, safari2502physicsinformedsurrogatesfor pages 17-19, raut2407temperaturedistributionprediction pages 1-3). For microstructure, ML surrogates trained on phase-field data achieve 2–3% errors (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 20-21). Key limitations include scarce comprehensive experimental validation datasets, instrumentation constraints (emissivity calibration, temporal/spatial resolution), and the difficulty of subsurface sensing during builds (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 16-18, ozel2023areviewon pages 27-29).

## 8. Digital Twins for Closed-Loop Process Optimization in Production

Digital twins are emerging as the integrative paradigm for achieving "first-time-right" manufacturing in LPBF. They enable closed-loop optimization through several mechanisms: (1) real-time thermal monitoring with fast surrogate models that predict melt pool behavior and trigger laser power adjustments within layer deposition timescales (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 34-35, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 42-43); (2) Bayesian optimization frameworks that automatically tune controller parameters and laser power profiles across layers (karkaria2024towardsadigital pages 2-3); (3) model predictive control using physics-informed surrogates to track thermal states and adapt process parameters layer-by-layer (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 43-44); and (4) multi-sensor fusion architectures that detect defects in-situ and generate corrective toolpaths for material addition or removal (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 45-47). An iterative closed-loop optimization concept exchanges bidirectional feedback between an optimization core and interconnected simulation/sensing components, enabling continuous improvement (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 45-47). Current challenges include high computational demands for frequent model updating, dependence on high-quality continuous sensor data, limited sensor modality fusion in some implementations, and the critical need for standardized validation frameworks to bridge laboratory demonstrations and industrial deployment (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 43-44, łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 2-5). Nearly 70% of reviewed digital twin works include experimental validation, but most demonstrations remain at prototype technology readiness levels, with industrial-scale deployment as a near-term priority (łach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 2-5).

## 9. Summary and Outlook

The period 2022–2026 has witnessed a fundamental shift from isolated physics-based simulations toward integrated digital twin ecosystems for LPBF. Multi-scale simulation chains—from melt pool CFD through part-scale thermo-mechanical analysis to grain-level microstructure prediction—are being accelerated by orders of magnitude through ML surrogates (PINNs, GNNs, operator learning) while maintaining accuracies of a few percent relative to high-fidelity benchmarks. The convergence of reduced-order physics models, multi-modal in-situ sensing, and adaptive control algorithms is enabling the transition from open-loop, trial-and-error manufacturing to closed-loop, data-driven production optimization. Key remaining challenges include computational cost for real-time part-scale digital twins, limited transferability of trained models across machines and alloy systems, data scarcity for comprehensive model validation, and the need for standardized uncertainty quantification and certification frameworks.
