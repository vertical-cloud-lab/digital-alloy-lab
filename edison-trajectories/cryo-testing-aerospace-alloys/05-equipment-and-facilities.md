# Cryogenic mechanical testing of metals: equipment and facilities

**The operating distinction is the specimen environment, not merely the refrigerator rating.** Liquid-nitrogen immersion provides approximately 77 K; liquid-helium immersion provides approximately 4.2 K. Tests near 20 K can instead use temperature-controlled helium gas or direct liquid-hydrogen immersion. A Gifford–McMahon (GM) or pulse-tube cold head rated near 4 K does **not** establish that a loaded metal specimen reaches or remains at 4 K. The cited cryogen-free metal tests demonstrate approximately 20 K; the documented 4 K bulk-metal tension, fatigue and fracture tests principally use liquid helium. ## Cooling arrangements and mechanical measurements

**Immersion cryostats.** A servo-hydraulic or electrodynamic frame applies force through long, aligned pull rods and grips inside a vacuum-insulated dewar. For 77 K tests, specimens and grips are submerged in LN₂; for 4 K tests, systems commonly pre-cool with LN₂, remove it and fill with LHe. ISO 6892-4 and ASTM E1450 describe immersed-helium tensile practice: keep the specimen submerged, use displacement control and limit the nominal strain rate to approximately 10⁻³ s⁻¹ or below. Immersion supplies strong thermal coupling, particularly useful during plastic deformation and prolonged fatigue, but demands cryogen transfers, level monitoring and oxygen-deficiency precautions. NIST’s published protocol used a minimum five-minute immersion soak and independently calibrated its clip gages in each liquid. (perez2022mechanicaltestingof pages 12-15, perez2022mechanicaltestingof pages 15-18, weeks2022tensiletestsat pages 8-12, perez2022mechanicaltestingof pages 21-23)

**Controlled gas and flow-through chambers.** For a selected temperature between bath boiling points, gas can be cooled by passing liquid cryogen through a heat exchanger or by expanding/vaporizing it into an insulated specimen enclosure; a sensor near the specimen, flow control and a heater establish the setpoint. This is *not equivalent* to immersing the specimen. At NASA Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC), liquid helium entered distributed, orificed tubes in an insulated fatigue-crack-growth chamber and had vaporized before leaving them: the specimen experienced **cold gaseous He at −423 °F, approximately 20 K**, not an LHe bath at 4 K. Thermocouples on both specimen faces and in the chamber monitored a reported +5/−10 °F temperature band. The rig required continuous operator monitoring and a 5,000-US-gallon helium tanker for the extended tests; that tanker capacity is **not** the amount consumed per test. KIT describes a distinct, two-chamber LN₂/LHe transfer-cooled arrangement in which the specimen is *not immersed*, specifically avoiding liquid-bubble interference with extensometer signals. (sas2015cryomak–theoverviewof pages 3-8, wells2005fatiguecrackgrowth pages 6-9, perez2022mechanicaltestingof pages 21-23)

**Liquid hydrogen.** MSFC’s Hydrogen Test Facility used a dedicated cryostat with automatic LH₂ filling to keep Inconel 718 crack-growth specimens completely submerged at −423 °F. Tests at 20 Hz followed ASTM E647; front-face clip-gage compliance tracked crack length, checked against optical measurements after testing. Direct LH₂ testing tests both **temperature and hydrogen exposure**, whereas helium gas at the same temperature is an inert thermal surrogate. In that particular alloy/test series, LH₂ and cold-He crack-growth results broadly agreed, but LH₂ testing was expensive enough that an additional desired near-threshold test was not undertaken. The source is Wells, Wright and Hastings, *NASA/TM-2005-213948* (2005); **no DOI was identified**. Precharging a specimen with hydrogen and subsequently testing it in inert helium at 20 or 4 K is likewise not testing *in* liquid hydrogen. (wells2005fatiguecrackgrowth pages 6-9, wells2005fatiguecrackgrowth pages 9-13, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 2-4, damm2025hydrogen–dislocationinteractionsat pages 4-7)

**Cryogen-free, conduction-cooled rigs.** A vacuum jacket and multilayer-insulated radiation shield intercept room-temperature heat; the first cryocooler stage cools the shield, while a copper bridge or strap connects the colder stage to a specimen vessel or grips. Some designs add a small sealed charge of high-purity **helium exchange gas** to couple the specimen thermally: *no liquid helium* does not mean *no helium gas*. Thermometers must characterize the loaded specimen or its immediate fixture, since load-train conduction, contact resistance and deformation heating can leave it considerably warmer than the second-stage cold head. A Chinese two-GM system achieved a **16.8 K specimen** after about eight hours, with up to 0.3 K spatial variation, and tested Ti–6Al–4V at 20 K through quartz windows using digital image correlation (DIC). A Korean ULVAC UHE15 GM system, whose second stage is *rated* at 1.5 W at 4.2 K, took about 13 hours to reach a **20 K specimen** and reported approximately ±0.3 K control around it. The Korean studies performed metal tension and ASTM E1820 compact-specimen, unloading-compliance J-integral fracture testing, rather than demonstrating 4 K specimen tests. DOIs: [10.1088/1757-899X/278/1/012083](https://doi.org/10.1088/1757-899X/278/1/012083), [10.3390/pr10112401](https://doi.org/10.3390/pr10112401), [10.3390/met13101774](https://doi.org/10.3390/met13101774). (zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 6-9, zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 1-3, zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 3-6, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 4-5, kim2023tensileandfracture pages 4-6, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 5-9)

**Pulse tubes:** Washington State University’s HYPER laboratory couples a **Cryomech PT-415** to an MTS Acumen 12 inside a vacuum-insulated rig, using a copper thermal strap and hydrogen liquefaction for about 20 K testing; its reported demonstrations concern **polymer composites**, although its authors say appropriately small specimens of some metals could fit. It reports up to 100 Hz fatigue capability, not a demonstrated 100 Hz metal test at 4 K. A separate Toulouse/Laboratoire National des Champs Magnétiques Intenses (LNCMI) pulse-tube installation uses a copper vessel and He exchange gas, reaches 20 K in about three hours and warms in about 4.5 hours; the reported tests are also **composites**. Its stated ability to cool to 4 K should not be cited as a demonstrated *4 K metal-test temperature*. DOI for CRAFT: [10.1088/1757-899X/1240/1/012080](https://doi.org/10.1088/1757-899X/1240/1/012080); no DOI was identified for the LNCMI abstract. (adams2022cryogenicacceleratedfatigue pages 1-4, narbonneUnknownyearnovelmethodfor pages 1-2, adams2022cryogenicacceleratedfatigue pages 4-7)

## Where the documented capabilities are

- **NIST, United States:** an MTS 55-kip (about 222 kN) servo-hydraulic frame, 100 kN load cell and three Shepic clip gages measured welded-316L tension at **77 K in LN₂ and 4 K in LHe**; the same report situates these measurements within an ASME–NASA–NIST fracture-toughness programme. It specifies three tensile specimens per weld at 77 K and two per weld at 4 K—not an annual throughput figure. DOI: [10.6028/NIST.TN.2229](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.TN.2229). (weeks2022tensiletestsat pages 6-8, weeks2022tensiletestsat pages 8-12)
- **National High Magnetic Field Laboratory, Tallahassee:** LHe-immersed, typically **100 kN servo-hydraulic** systems perform **4 K tension, compact-tension fatigue-crack growth and J-integral fracture toughness** of magnet steels; the methods invoke ASTM E1450, E647 and E1820. DOI: [10.1088/1757-899X/279/1/012001](https://doi.org/10.1088/1757-899X/279/1/012001). (mcrae2017fatigueandfracture pages 1-3, mcrae2017fatigueandfracture pages 3-4)
- **NIMS and its predecessor National Research Institute for Metals, Tsukuba:** published titanium-alloy high-cycle fatigue tests used **LN₂ at 77 K and a recondensing LHe bath at 4 K**, with a ±100 kN dynamic-capacity servo-hydraulic machine; frequencies were reduced to limit specimen heating. The predecessor’s closed-loop helium-recondensing fatigue plant demonstrated **more than 460 hours** of continuous operation. DOIs: [10.1016/j.cryogenics.2005.10.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cryogenics.2005.10.011), [10.2355/isijinternational1966.26.48](https://doi.org/10.2355/isijinternational1966.26.48). (yuri2006notcheffectson pages 1-3, ogata1986anewliquid pages 1-2, ogata1986anewliquid pages 4-5)
- **CERN Mechanical and Materials Engineering, Geneva:** four earlier **18 kN** sub-size cryostats were supplemented by an in-house **100 kN, 300–4 K** immersion cryostat for standard-size metallic tensile specimens. The 2015 account demonstrates 4 K results but presents low-temperature fracture work as an intended future use of the adaptable frame, **not** as a fracture programme demonstrated in that paper. DOI: [10.1088/1757-899X/102/1/012007](https://doi.org/10.1088/1757-899X/102/1/012007). (santillana2015designandfabrication pages 1-4, santillana2015designandfabrication pages 9-11)
- **KIT CryoMaK, Karlsruhe:** published facility ranges are **MTS 25, room temperature–7 K; MTS 50, room temperature–77 K; MTS 100, ATLAS and PHOENIX, room temperature–4.2 K**; listed facilities support combinations of tension, fatigue, fracture toughness and component testing. Its MTS 100 Landmark is rated **100 kN, up to 40 Hz**. The report described integration of that particular new cryostat as planned for 2016, so the table of facility capabilities should not be read as proof that *every* newly listed configuration was commissioned in 2015. DOI: [10.12776/ams.v21i4.650](https://doi.org/10.12776/ams.v21i4.650). (sas2015cryomak–theoverviewof pages 1-3, sas2015cryomak–theoverviewof pages 3-8, sas2015cryomak–theoverviewof media 78b803da)
- **NASA MSFC, Alabama:** the Hydrogen Test Facility demonstrated direct **20 K LH₂** metal crack-growth tests; a separate continuous-flow cold-He chamber produced **20 K** inert comparison tests. **NASA Glenn, Ohio:** its later cryogen-free **LESTR** uses an Instron ElectroPuls E10000 and a Sumitomo closed-cycle cooler for **40–125 K metal-wire** tension and bend/fatigue work—not 20 K or 4 K. DOI for LESTR: [10.64631/lhoi2310](https://doi.org/10.64631/lhoi2310); MSFC report: *NASA/TM-2005-213948*, no DOI identified. (wells2005fatiguecrackgrowth pages 6-9, wells2005fatiguecrackgrowth pages 9-13, ring2026mechanicaldesignand pages 1-6, ring2026mechanicaldesignand pages 8-10, ring2026mechanicaldesignand pages 26-28)
- **UK National Physical Laboratory:** its published **250 kN, ambient-to-4 K** static/dynamic mechanical cryostat is a **capability design/requirements study**, including controlled intermediate temperatures, not evidence in that report of commissioned metal tests. DOI: [10.47120/npl.mat102](https://doi.org/10.47120/npl.mat102). (perez2022mechanicaltestingof pages 18-21, perez2022mechanicaltestingof pages 15-18)

## Strain, crack-opening and temperature instrumentation

Direct cryogenic strain measurement is preferred to inferring specimen strain from actuator travel, which includes load-train compliance and thermal contraction. Reported implementations include NIST’s **three Shepic ball-ended clip gages** on 25.4 mm-gage rings, calibrated separately at 77 and 4 K; NHMFL’s **Shepic clip-on extensometers** for tension and clip-on crack-opening-displacement (COD) gages for compact specimens; CERN’s **CEME C-shaped 50 mm and W-shaped 25 mm extensometers**, calibrated at room temperature, 77 and 4 K; and **Epsilon Model 3442, 10 mm** gages in the Korean 20 K studies. KIT describes in-house clip-on instruments with **350 Ω strain-gage Wheatstone bridges** and temperature-dependent calibration. Fracture tests additionally require measured load-line displacement/COD and validated crack-length tracking, particularly where discontinuous yielding upsets unloading compliance. (sas2015cryomak–theoverviewof pages 3-8, sas2015cryomak–theoverviewof pages 8-9, mcrae2017fatigueandfracture pages 3-4, weeks2022tensiletestsat pages 8-12, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 2-4, kim2023tensileandfracture pages 4-6, santillana2015designandfabrication pages 9-11)

Bonded cryogenic strain gages need temperature-dependent gage-factor and thermal-output correction; opposed gages can help diagnose bending, and the load train must be checked for axial alignment. Optical DIC avoids mounting a contact extensometer on the specimen but requires insulated windows and a stable speckle image; the Chinese study explicitly compared 20 K DIC with two Epsilon extensometers. A cold-stage reading alone is insufficient during serrated yielding or cyclic heating: NIMS measured internal specimen temperature rise with an embedded thermocouple, and the Korean investigators deliberately slowed tension to **5 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹** in their 304L study. (zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 6-9, zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 3-6, ogata1986anewliquid pages 4-5, perez2022mechanicaltestingof pages 21-23, kim2023tensileandfracture pages 4-6, santillana2015designandfabrication pages 9-11)

**Commercial equipment actually named in methods** includes MTS and Instron frames; **Walter + bai** 100 kN-static/80 kN-dynamic frames; **SUNS PLS 100**; **ULVAC UHE15** GM, **Cryomech PT-415** pulse-tube and **Sumitomo CH-110LT** cryocoolers; **Epsilon**, **Shepic** and CEME-developed extensometers; and **Lake Shore Model 335** temperature control. CERN’s 100 kN cryostat and KIT’s described cryogenic hardware are institutionally built/integrated; the cited studies do not establish a commercial maker for those cryostats. KIT also lists Galdabini and INSTRON CEAST impact machines, but those should **not** be mislabelled as its 4 K fatigue frames. (zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 3-6, sas2015cryomak–theoverviewof pages 1-3, mcrae2017fatigueandfracture pages 3-4, weeks2022tensiletestsat pages 8-12, santillana2015designandfabrication pages 1-4, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 4-5, kim2023tensileandfracture pages 4-6, adams2022cryogenicacceleratedfatigue pages 4-7, ring2026mechanicaldesignand pages 26-28)

## Helium, time and cost: what is actually reported

CERN calculated **25–30 L of LHe for one test** in its approximately **15 L** cryostat, with **20–25 minutes of cooldown between tests** at the specified filling pressure; this is a turnaround component, not the duration of every test. The NIMS predecessor reported about **50 L LHe and 300 kg LN₂ at startup**, approximately **15 kg LN₂ per hour** for shielding, and roughly **15 L LHe added after exchanging a specimen**, while requiring **no added LHe during uninterrupted recondensing operation**. Its optimized initial closure took about **six hours**, exchange/reclosure about **two hours**, and continuous operation exceeded **460 hours**. These figures describe different accounting boundaries and must not be compared as though all were LHe *per specimen*. (ogata1986anewliquid pages 1-2, ogata1986anewliquid pages 2-4, ogata1986anewliquid pages 4-5, santillana2015designandfabrication pages 4-6)

NPL’s **25–30 L LHe during an optimized 30-minute cooldown** is a *design estimate* for a proposed roughly 20–25 L dewar, not a measured cost or consumption per completed test. GM-cooled metal rigs avoid LHe purchases but reported **8–13-hour cooldowns** and still use small amounts of exchange He gas, electricity and cryocooler infrastructure. LH₂ requires dedicated storage, venting, detection and safety systems; MSFC explicitly notes the expense of additional LH₂ tests. The retrieved primary studies provide **no comparable currency-denominated capital price, helium price per litre, or fully burdened cost per metal specimen**; deriving one without local supply, recovery, labour and duty-cycle data would be misleading. (perez2022mechanicaltestingof pages 18-21, zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 6-9, wells2005fatiguecrackgrowth pages 6-9, wells2005fatiguecrackgrowth pages 9-13, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 4-5, adams2022cryogenicacceleratedfatigue pages 4-7, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 5-9)
