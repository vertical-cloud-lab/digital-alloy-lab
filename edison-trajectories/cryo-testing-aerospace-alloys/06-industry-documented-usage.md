Question: How have launch-vehicle, spacecraft and aircraft programs documented the use of cryogenic mechanical testing to select and qualify aluminum alloys for cryogenic propellant tanks? Examples: the Space Shuttle external tank and super-lightweight tank (2219 to 2195 Al-Li), the SLS core stage (2219), Delta IV, Atlas V and Vulcan, Falcon 9 (Al-Li with friction stir welding), Ariane 5 and 6, H-IIA and H3, Chinese launch vehicles (2219, 5A06, Al-Li), and liquid-hydrogen aircraft tank studies (NASA, Airbus, DLR). For each, give the test temperatures used (77 K vs 20 K), the properties measured (for example the cryogenic-to-room-temperature fracture toughness ratio, weld strength, leak-before-break), and NASA technical reports or conference papers with citations, DOIs or NTRS numbers.

# Cryogenic aluminum-alloy evidence for propellant tanks

**The distinction is between a material test and a tank’s operating temperature.** Liquid nitrogen testing near **77 K** (−196 °C, −320 °F) is common in alloy screening; liquid-hydrogen testing near **20 K** (−253 °C, −423 °F) addresses the colder service condition. A paper specifying a 20 K hydrogen tank does **not**, by itself, establish that its alloy coupons were tested at 20 K. The public record is detailed for some NASA programs but does not support a uniform, vehicle-by-vehicle list of 77 K and 20 K qualification results. (chen2002cryogenicfracturetoughness pages 12-16, witzell1977evaluationofmechanical pages 1-4, merino2017ariane6tanksand pages 1-2)

| **Space Shuttle external tank and super-lightweight tank (SLWT)** | The original tank used **2219**; **2195 Al–Li** replaced it in substantial portions of the lighter tank. Production acceptance encompassed material allowables, welds, inspection and proof tests, not merely parent-metal strength. Welding difficulties later led some domes and ogives back to 2219. | Chen and colleagues tested 2195 fracture specimens at **ambient and 77 K**, measuring strength and cryogenic fracture toughness; the reported SLWT minimum cryogenic toughness was **30 ksi√in**. Separately, a published lot-acceptance guideline was **fracture-toughness ratio, FTR = cryogenic/ambient toughness >1**. For lot 950M029B, conventional aging gave **25.40/30.04 = 0.85**; two-step aging gave **33.74/30.44 = 1.11**, in ksi√in at 77 K/ambient. The treatment improved the cryogenic value while controlling detrimental subgrain-boundary T₁ precipitates. These are **77 K results, not 20 K toughness results**. Lockheed Martin tested 2195/2195 and 2219/2195 combinations, including surface-crack-tension specimens. | **Room temperature and −423 °F (≈20 K)** tensile tests were reported. The paper describes a **1.6 cryogenic/room-temperature weld-strength enhancement factor**, weld strength approaching parent-metal strength at 20 K, and improved surface-crack-tension performance relative to fusion welding; **1.6 is a strength ratio, not FTR**. Through-thickness variations and intermetallic-particle clustering affected fracture. This is **plate characterization**, not a published 20 K toughness ratio for a completed SLS tank or its FSW seams. **NASA/TM-20230006220**, NESC-RP-21-01721; related Medders *et al.*, **NASA/TM-20230018439**. Parent-metal yield/ultimate rose from approximately **385/460 to 506/684 MPa**; weld values rose from **135/262 to 249/458 MPa**. Aircraft tank design papers and Airbus Cryoplane concepts address structural sizing, thermal insulation and operation, rather than establishing Airbus/DLR alloy-coupon qualification data. | At **75 °F versus −320 °F (≈77 K)**, 2050 conditional toughness **KQJIC** was **38.4→32.4 ksi√in (L–T)**, **24.8→28.2 (T–L)** and **37.7→44.4 (L–S)**: ratios **0.84, 1.14 and 1.18**. **All failed the cited ASTM toughness-validity requirements**; they are comparative, not certified *K*IC values. **NASA/TM-2011-217163**. **Leak-before-break requires separate evidence.** NASA’s aircraft-tank review, **NASA/TM-2006-214346**, presents the material-selection indices *K*IC/σf for yield-before-break and **(*K*IC)²/σf** for leak-before-break. An index is **not** evidence that a particular launcher passed a pressurized, flawed-tank test. Earlier 2219 pressure-vessel work instead explicitly studied proof loads, ligament penetration and residual cyclic life across conditions down to **20 K** (**NASA/CR-135036**); weldment flaw tests at about **78 K and 20 K** observed leakage before fracture in many configurations but also identified thickness- and stress-dependent exceptions. These studies support examining flaws, welds, wall thickness and operating load together rather than declaring an alloy intrinsically “leak-before-break.” (mital2006reviewofcurrent pages 24-27, finger1976prooftestcriteria pages 1-6, masters1974deepflawsin pages 44-48)

**Bottom line:** The most traceable progression is **77 K plate screening and SLWT 2195 lot acceptance → 20 K testing of selected welds, thick 2219 plate and aircraft-tank materials → component proof, flaw-growth and leak assessment**. It is *not* documented as one identical test sequence across the listed launchers. In particular, a 20 K LH₂ service specification, a 77 K alloy paper, and a room-temperature weld result cannot be combined into a claimed program-specific 20 K qualification without a report that actually connects them. (chen1998theeffectsof pages 1-2, kinchen1999mechanicalpropertiesof pages 1-3, hales2023metallurgicalfactorsthat media 31c7836c, merino2017ariane6tanksand pages 1-2, witzell1977evaluationofmechanical pages 1-4)
