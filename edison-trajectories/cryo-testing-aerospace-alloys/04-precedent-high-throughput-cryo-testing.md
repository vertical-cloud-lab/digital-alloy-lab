**Question (PRECEDENT):** Has anyone built or used a high-throughput, automated or multi-specimen cryogenic mechanical testing system for metals, for example a cryostat that tests many tensile specimens per cooldown, miniaturized or small-punch specimens tested at 77 K or 4 K, or a cryocooler-based conduction-cooled tensile rig, to screen alloys faster than one specimen per cooldown?

---

Question: Has anyone built or used a high-throughput, automated or multi-specimen cryogenic mechanical testing system for metals, for example a cryostat that tests many tensile specimens per cooldown, miniaturized or small-punch specimens tested at 77 K or 4 K, or a cryocooler-based conduction-cooled tensile rig, to screen alloys faster than one specimen per cooldown?

Yes. ## Finding

**Yes.** The clearest direct precedent is a **six-specimen liquid-helium tensile cryostat** used to test a metal alloy at **4.2 K without opening the cryostat between specimens**. An earlier, remotely indexed **eight-position** apparatus also performed sequential tensile tests of aluminum specimens inside a low-temperature reactor channel. These are stronger evidence for faster-than-one-specimen-per-cooldown testing than small-punch or cryocooler rigs alone. (ishikawa1977mechanicalpropertiesof pages 1-2, naskidashvili1970tensiletestingdevicefor pages 1-3, naskidashvili1970tensiletestingdevicefor pages 3-4)

The comparison below separates demonstrated multispecimen operation from methods that conserve material or avoid liquid helium but do **not** document specimen exchange during a cooldown.

### Direct multispecimen precedents

- **Ishikawa and Tsuya, *Cryogenics* (1977), DOI: 10.1016/0011-2275(77)90199-0.** Six tensile specimens were installed on a rotating turret in a liquid-helium cryostat. Rotating the disk brought each specimen into the pull-rod/load-cell train; the authors explicitly state that six could be tested at **4.2 K without opening the cryostat to atmosphere**. They used the apparatus to characterize a ferritic **Fe–13%Ni–3%Mo** alloy. Their reported consumption was **12–15 L of helium for six tests**, including transfer losses and boil-off, with up to approximately **three hours** of testing at 4.2 K. This is a documented, working answer to the one-specimen-per-cooldown problem, although indexing was mechanical rather than reported as fully automated. (ishikawa1977mechanicalpropertiesof pages 1-2, ishikawa1977mechanicalpropertiesof pages 2-3)
- **Naskidashvili and colleagues, *Soviet Atomic Energy* (1970), DOI: 10.1007/bf01235494.** Their miniature in-reactor tensile apparatus held **eight specimens**. After a specimen fractured, electrical pulses actuated electromagnets to rotate the holder and feed the next specimen into a pneumatic grip; load–extension curves were recorded automatically. They report testing technical-grade aluminum specimens before and during irradiation in the low-temperature channel. The eight positions are **capacity**, not an explicit report that all eight were tested in one run; it is also a specialized irradiation system, not a general-purpose 4 K alloy screener. (naskidashvili1970tensiletestingdevicefor pages 1-3, naskidashvili1970tensiletestingdevicefor pages 3-4)

### Related approaches, with an important throughput distinction

**Miniature small-punch tests are established at both liquid-nitrogen and liquid-helium temperatures.** Misawa and colleagues tested **10 × 10 × 0.5 mm** coupons from five cryogenic structural steels at **77 and 4.2 K**, using a compact cryostat and correlating punch-derived fracture measures with conventional fracture toughness. Shindo and colleagues subsequently used comparably sized coupons to evaluate structural alloys and weldments at **4 K** and SUS304/SUS316 in **0 and 6 T** fields at **4 K**. Ju and colleagues tested notched SA508 steel coupons at **77 K**. These studies demonstrate useful small-material-volume screening or comparative characterization—not an automatically indexed, multi-coupon punch cryostat or a documented number tested per cooldown. Moreover, punch-derived toughness requires an appropriate correlation to conventional specimens; it is not intrinsically a direct tensile test. (misawa1989fracturetoughnessevaluation pages 1-2, misawa1989fracturetoughnessevaluation pages 2-3, shindo1999cryogenicfracturetoughness pages 1-4, shindo2004smallpunchtesting pages 1-2, shindo2004smallpunchtesting pages 2-4, ju2003evaluationoffracture pages 2-4)

**Cryocooler-based metal testing is also demonstrated, but coolant-free does not mean multispecimen.** Zhang and colleagues tested **Ti–6Al–4V at 20 K** using Gifford–McMahon cryocoolers: copper links cool the chamber, while **helium exchange gas is the principal heat-transfer route from specimen to chamber**. Kim and colleagues used a G–M/helium-gas tensile cryostat for stainless steels and aluminum alloys at **20 K**, reporting approximately **13 hours** to cool a specimen. Neither paper describes indexed specimens or same-cooldown exchange. A more directly **conduction-cooled** microsample concept was reported by Chen and colleagues: its holder mounts on a pulse-tube cold head, reaching approximately **16 K** after **45 minutes**, with controlled operation from **20 K** upward. It does **not** reach 4 K or demonstrate multiple specimens per cooldown; its TEM-disk prototype is also not a validated standard metal tensile-coupon geometry. (zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 1-3, zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 3-6, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 2-4, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 4-5, chen2015acryogenictensile pages 4-7, chen2015acryogenictensile pages 1-4)

**Bottom line:** A rotating six-specimen **4.2 K tensile turret** and an electrically indexed eight-position low-temperature tensile device establish that the central concept has been built and used. The retrieved small-punch and cryocooler studies offer complementary ways to reduce sample requirements or liquid-helium use, but should not be cited as demonstrations of automated, multi-alloy-per-cooldown screening. The sources establish apparatus capability and reported operating details, **not** a measured head-to-head specimens-per-day advantage over a conventional single-specimen system. (ishikawa1977mechanicalpropertiesof pages 1-2, naskidashvili1970tensiletestingdevicefor pages 3-4, misawa1989fracturetoughnessevaluation pages 2-3, zhang2017liquidheliumfree pages 3-6, kim2022metallicmaterialevaluation pages 4-5)
