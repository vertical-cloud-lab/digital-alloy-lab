## 1) How AMMTO/DOE define “critical minerals/materials,” and relation to USGS/DOI lists

### 1.1 DOE’s statutory and strategy framing

DOE’s 2023 Critical Materials Strategy states that DOE’s Critical Materials RDD&CA Program was established under the Energy Act of 2020 and “allows DOE to invest across the entire research continuum and supply chain,” and lays out a “Vision for Critical Minerals and Materials (CMM)” focused on reliable/resilient/secure domestic supply chains and decarbonization (front matter) (bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 1-9).

A complementary workshop synthesis explicitly cites the Energy Act of 2020 definition of “critical minerals” as a non-fuel mineral or mineral material essential to U.S. economic or national security with a supply chain vulnerable to disruption, and contrasts DOE’s role (application-driven R&D and commercialization to address vulnerabilities) with USGS’s role (resource identification/assessment and maintenance of the U.S. Critical Mineral List) (iams2025materialchallengesin pages 7-11).

### 1.2 DOE’s “critical materials” vs. other federal lists

DOE’s 2023 Critical Materials Strategy explains that DOE’s assessment differs from other federal assessments (e.g., DOI/USGS) because of differences in scope/approach/purpose; DOE broadened its scope beyond raw minerals to include engineered materials such as electrical steel and silicon carbide (SiC) (bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 16-18). This is relevant to AMMTO because AMMTO personnel are explicitly listed as authors/affiliations on the DOE strategy (“Advanced Materials & Manufacturing Technologies Office, U.S. Department of Energy”), indicating AMMTO participation in defining the DOE framing (bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 1-9).

### 1.3 DOE criticality methodology and the “pillar” structure

DOE’s 2023 Critical Materials Strategy summarizes five priority pillars: (1) Diversify & Expand Supply; (2) Develop Alternatives; (3) Materials and Manufacturing Efficiency; (4) Circular Economy; and (5) Enabling Activities (bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 1-9). A separate workshop report also summarizes DOE “priority actions” in essentially the same spirit—diversify supply, develop substitutes, and enable circular material use—while distinguishing DOE’s remit from USGS (iams2025materialchallengesin pages 7-11).

DOE defines criticality via an “importance to energy” vs “supply risk” scoring framework, with critical materials being those scoring ≥3 on both axes and near-critical materials scoring 3+ on one axis and 2 on the other, and enumerates seven short-term (2020–2025) critical materials (cobalt, dysprosium, gallium, natural graphite, iridium, neodymium, terbium) (bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 133-136).

## 3) CMI / Critical Materials Innovation Hub: scope, (available) budget evidence, outcomes, and status

### 3.1 Scope and technical pillars

The CMI Year 10 report describes a three-pillar structure aligned to DOE’s criticality logic: diversifying supply, developing substitutes, and driving reuse/recycling, with crosscutting supply-chain/economic analysis (lograsso2024criticalmaterialsinstitute pages 9-11). It provides concrete substitution examples: La/Co-substituted NdFeB magnets, low-temperature sintering of SmFeN magnets, and rare-earth-free phosphors (lograsso2024criticalmaterialsinstitute pages 9-11).

A concise definition from CMI’s report: “A material becomes critical when it is essential for a technology, vulnerable to supply disruption, and difficult to substitute” (lograsso2024criticalmaterialsinstitute pages 4-6).

### 3.2 Outcomes and measurable impacts

CMI’s decade-of-operation outcomes include: 12 R&D 100 awards; 202 invention disclosures; 50 U.S. patents; 20 technologies licensed/optioned; 635 peer-reviewed publications; and more than $80M in follow-on funding (lograsso2024criticalmaterialsinstitute pages 27-29). Additional publication/citation metrics (e.g., >635 publications; multiple highly cited papers) appear elsewhere in the Year 10 report excerpt (lograsso2024criticalmaterialsinstitute pages 31-34).

A detailed technology-transition example is the Al–Ce alloy family: licensed (Year 4) and used to produce as-cast hydroelectric turbine blades (Year 5), reaching TRL 9; the alloy family served at least 12 customers across eight applications; and CMI oversight yielded disclosed inventions and patents tied to the alloy work (lograsso2024criticalmaterialsinstitute pages 29-31). This provides a concrete precedent for AMMTO-relevant “materials discovery → manufacturing → deployment” translation in a critical-materials context.

### 3.3 Budget and current status

Within the retrieved excerpts, CMI provides strong evidence of outcomes and follow-on funding, but an explicit “total DOE hub budget” number was not located. The report does, however, explicitly state “More than $80 million in follow-on funding” (lograsso2024criticalmaterialsinstitute pages 27-29).

On status and naming: the patent-portfolio evaluation explicitly states the program is the “Critical Materials Innovation Hub (CMI Hub, formerly the Critical Materials Institute)” (thomas2024anevaluationof pages 4-6). The Year 10 report describes CMI as active through Year 10 with ongoing workforce/outreach and also states it is “entering a third phase of operations” (lograsso2024criticalmaterialsinstitute pages 37-39).
