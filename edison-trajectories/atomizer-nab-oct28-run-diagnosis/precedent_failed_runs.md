No. ## Documented cases

The clearest published *metal-run interruption* I found is for 316L stainless steel. The closest copper-alloy report documents incomplete powder recovery and sonotrode contamination, but **not** a witnessed nozzle blockage or melt running off the plate. The distinction matters when diagnosing an individual failed run. **Copper-alloy interpretation.** Müller and colleagues atomized nickel–aluminium bronze propeller-grinding chips on an AMAZEMET rePowder system. They attribute the non-powder portion of their 77% recovery mainly to organic and ceramic contamination collected as slag; they do not identify it as metal that escaped the sonotrode. Their 1 mm melt-feed nozzle was chosen *to avoid possible* blockage by residual ceramic abrasive, not to repair a blockage they observed. They did observe carbon fibres in the product and attributed them to hot melt detaching fibres from the woven carbon-fibre sonotrode. Their proposed change to another sonotrode material, ideally similar to the alloy, was **not tested** in that report. The paper says the *chips* received no cleaning or purification before or after atomization; that statement is **not** a procedure for cleaning the sonotrode. (muller2023usingultrasonicatomization pages 3-6, muller2023usingultrasonicatomization pages 6-8)

**Metal accumulation and reduced yield.** Bałasz and colleagues stopped five of sixteen planned 316L ATO Lab arc-and-wire atomizations—S5, S9, S10, S13 and S14—after unstable operation formed a mass of liquid metal on the sonotrode that interfered with the ultrasonic system. They considered excessive wire feed and/or insufficient arc current the likely parameter mismatch. Selecting an appropriate feed/current combination was their operating remedy: S6, at **100 A and 6.83 mm/s**, returned **80.6%** powder; S3, at 130 A and 18.82 mm/s, returned 32.5%. These are **different parameter settings**, not evidence that a second run on a reused plate yielded less. Nor do the authors establish that resonance itself was lost. The paper’s conclusion rounds the preferred feed to approximately 9 mm/s, but its methods identify S6 precisely as 6.83 mm/s; the separate 100 A, 9.47 mm/s run S2 returned 66.0%. (bałasz2024aninvestigationof pages 2-4, bałasz2024aninvestigationof pages 9-11, bałasz2024aninvestigationof media d6621dff)

**Useful diagnostic analog, not a metal report.** In a laboratory study intended to inform future liquid-metal experiments, Szostak-Staropiętka and colleagues atomized *nonmetal surrogate liquids* on a 160 × 30 × 1 mm adjustable stainless-steel plate. Partial atomization initially left a patterned liquid buildup: measured vibration amplitude reached only about **2.8 µm**, despite higher generator settings drawing more power. Rearranging the acoustic-amplifier configuration produced **8 µm** and smooth atomization of the ordinary surrogate. A much more viscous surrogate still built up instead of atomizing; turning ultrasound off let it drain down the plate. These observations support checking **measured plate displacement**, rather than assuming a generator percentage proves adequate vibration, but do not establish a cause or fix for an actual metal runoff or lost-resonance incident. The authors say a metal-capable stand remained future work. The publication’s cropped Figures 8, 10 and 11 show the retained-liquid pattern, recovered atomization and high-viscosity buildup, respectively. (szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess pages 8-11, szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess pages 11-13, szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess pages 13-15, szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess media 4b1fed1b, szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess media 591a7a70, szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess media 950c9656)

## Published placement, inclination and maintenance guidance

- **Where the stream lands:** Kustron and colleagues’ *proposed* metal-atomizer design directs the molten stream **onto the vibrating working surface** and sizes that surface to capture as much of the stream as possible while preserving sufficiently high, well-distributed amplitude. For their broad twin-wire-arc-spray stream, they chose a **140 mm** plate as a compromise: smaller plates offered greater central displacement but could miss part of the stream; a 190 mm plate covered it but had lower displacement. This is design guidance, **not** an experimentally validated rule to aim a narrow crucible-fed stream at a particular distance from the centre, rim, node or antinode. Their reported water tests and modelling should not be represented as failed molten-metal trials. (kustron2023developmentofhighpower pages 2-4, kustron2023developmentofhighpower pages 4-7, kustron2023developmentofhighpower pages 7-10)
- **Inclination:** Szostak-Staropiętka and colleagues simulated one droplet at **30°, 45° and 60°** plate inclination. After 0.004 s, respectively **48.2%, 49.7% and 50.9%** of the initial liquid area remained: a 2.7-percentage-point spread. They judged that small for *one simulated droplet*, while cautioning that inclination may matter at greater feed rates and for apparatus geometry and heat transfer. Their physical stand allowed adjustment. These results do **not** prescribe an optimum tilt for molten aluminium bronze. (szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess pages 8-11, szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess pages 5-8, szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess pages 13-15)
- **Between-run cleaning and replacement:** I found **no documented protocol** in these primary reports for scraping, chemically cleaning or replacing a metal-atomization plate between runs, and no controlled clean-versus-reused-plate yield comparison. Müller and colleagues’ suggestion to change sonotrode *material* addresses shed carbon fibres, not a demonstrated between-run replacement schedule. Bałasz and colleagues describe vacuuming and blowing **powder sieves** between sieving portions; this must not be mistaken for sonotrode cleaning. (muller2023usingultrasonicatomization pages 6-8, bałasz2024aninvestigationof pages 4-7, bałasz2024aninvestigationof pages 2-4)

**Evidence gap:** Among the accessible reports examined, I could not verify a first-hand copper- or aluminium-alloy account that explicitly records unatomized melt running from the plate into the collector, a lower-yield second run caused by plate reuse, an actual melt-feed nozzle clog, or measured loss of acoustic resonance. The sources above instead document slag-associated recovery loss, sonotrode-derived contamination, liquid-metal accumulation interrupting 316L runs, and a separate surrogate-liquid amplitude failure; their proposed and demonstrated fixes should not be conflated. (muller2023usingultrasonicatomization pages 3-6, muller2023usingultrasonicatomization pages 6-8, bałasz2024aninvestigationof pages 2-4, bałasz2024aninvestigationof pages 9-11, szostakstaropietka2025influenceofprocess pages 11-13)
