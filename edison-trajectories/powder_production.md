# Metal Powder Production Methods for Additive Manufacturing: Custom Alloy Compositions (2022–2026)

## 1. Conventional Atomization Methods: Process Parameters, Alloy Flexibility, and Powder Characteristics

### 1.1 Gas Atomization (GA/VIGA)

Gas atomization, particularly vacuum induction melting gas atomization (VIGA), remains the dominant industrial method for producing metal powders for additive manufacturing (AM). In this process, a vacuum-induction-melted alloy is atomized by high-pressure inert gas (typically argon at pressures up to ~4–6 MPa) through close-coupled or free-fall nozzles (frolov2025metalpowderproduction pages 2-4, wilkens2025continuousandcostefficient pages 1-3). Key controllable process parameters include atomization gas pressure, melt superheat, gas-to-metal mass ratio, nozzle geometry, and melt flow rate; higher gas pressure generally produces finer, more spherical particles (frolov2025metalpowderproduction pages 2-4, zhang2025researchprogresson pages 13-15). VIGA yields broad particle size distributions (PSD) of 15–150 µm, with sphericity of approximately 85–90% and oxygen content of 200–1400 ppm (zhang2025researchprogresson pages 10-11). Satellite particles are common due to collision of solidifying droplets. For a continuous gas atomization plant, powder quality metrics such as Hall flow rate of 12 s/50 g, Hausner ratio of 1.22, and angle of repose of 18.4° have been reported for Inconel 625 (15–45 µm fraction) (wilkens2025continuousandcostefficient pages 3-6). Gas atomization offers high alloy flexibility, as virtually any alloy that can be melted can be processed, though ceramic crucible contact in VIGA can introduce non-metallic inclusions (ren2025propertiesofelectrode pages 2-5). Industrial batch VIGA plants typically have a maximum batch size of approximately 500 kg, while new continuous gas atomization plants can produce up to 4,000 tons per year with substantially lower per-unit production costs (wilkens2025continuousandcostefficient pages 1-3, wilkens2025continuousandcostefficient pages 3-6).

### 1.2 Plasma Atomization (PA)

Plasma atomization uses plasma torches to melt wire feedstock while high-velocity plasma/gas jets atomize the melt into fine droplets. Critical process parameters include wire feed rate (10–100 g/min), plasma torch power (optimal ratio of 19–32 g/(kW·h)), and inert gas flow/pressure (zhang2025researchprogresson pages 13-15). PA produces powders with PSD typically in the 0–150 µm range, with an 80% fraction achievable within 0–106 µm through optimized nozzle design, and median particle size reducible from 70 µm to 40 µm (zhang2025researchprogresson pages 8-10). PA powders exhibit very high sphericity (~95–99%), smooth surfaces, compact cross-sections, narrow size distributions, low impurity content, and excellent flowability (zhang2025researchprogresson pages 8-10, zhang2025researchprogresson pages 10-11). Oxygen content is generally <1000 ppm. PA is particularly well-suited for reactive metals such as titanium and tungsten alloys. However, PA requires expensive wire feedstock, has limited single-equipment throughput, and incurs high energy and equipment costs compared to GA (zhang2025researchprogresson pages 8-10).

### 1.3 Ultrasonic Atomization (UA)

Ultrasonic atomization has emerged as a promising method for small-batch, custom alloy powder production. The process operates by vibrating a sonotrode at ultrasonic frequencies (typically 20–70 kHz) while an electric arc or induction source melts feedstock on the sonotrode surface; capillary standing waves eject molten droplets that solidify in an inert gas stream into spherical particles (halapi2023ultrasonicpowderatomization pages 1-4, zach2023sphericalpowderscontrol pages 4-4, grzelak2022acomparativestudy pages 2-4). Particle size is frequency-dependent per Lang's relation—higher frequency yields finer droplets—with D50 of approximately 45–50 µm at 35 kHz for steel (grzelak2022acomparativestudy pages 2-4). Studies on AlSi12 showed that arc manipulator angle, electrical current (greatest impact), and vibration amplitude significantly influenced PSD, with span variations up to 37.5% (yankin2025effectofultrasonic pages 16-16). UA powders demonstrate high sphericity and narrow PSD, with suitability factors reported as 21% higher than commercial gas-atomized powders in one benchmark study (yankin2025effectofultrasonic pages 16-16). Sieve analysis of iron-based alloys showed >60% of 17-4PH powder in the <32 µm fraction and ~64% of ATO 1.4551 powder in the 45–63 µm bin (halapi2023ultrasonicpowderatomization pages 1-4). UA accepts diverse feedstock types including wire, rod, sheets, chips, and scrap, providing high alloy flexibility (ciftcli2025alloydevelopmentfrom pages 1-3).

## 2. Novel and Emerging Atomization Techniques

### 2.1 Amazemet Ultrasonic Atomization (rePowder System)

The Amazemet rePowder system represents a particularly significant development for custom alloy production. It uses a water-cooled, ultrasonically vibrated sonotrode at ~20 kHz with an 80–400 A arc to melt feedstock, producing spherical, free-flowing powders with high uniformity in a low-pressure argon stream (zach2023sphericalpowderscontrol pages 4-4). The system's critical advantage is its ability to produce custom powders "from even one gram up to a few kilograms to verify new alloys," enabling in-house powder production at research labs and universities without the large-scale infrastructure of conventional atomization (ciftcli2025alloydevelopmentfrom pages 1-3). The system has been demonstrated for a range of materials including Ti-6Al-4V, Al-Cu alloys, NiTi, copper from recycled scrap, iron aluminides, and enriched stable isotopes, highlighting its versatility for alloy development from sustainable/recycled feedstock (zach2023sphericalpowderscontrol pages 4-4, ciftcli2025alloydevelopmentfrom pages 1-3). The absence of a high-velocity fluid stream allows the atomization chamber to be compact and scalable (ciftcli2025alloydevelopmentfrom pages 1-3).

### 2.2 Electrode Induction Melting Gas Atomization (EIGA)

EIGA is a crucible-free process where a pre-alloyed rod electrode is inductively melted at its tip and the resulting melt stream is gravity-fed into a high-pressure gas atomization nozzle (zhang2025researchprogresson pages 4-6, ren2025propertiesofelectrode pages 2-5). This eliminates ceramic crucible contamination, producing high-purity powders with minimal non-metallic inclusions (ren2025propertiesofelectrode pages 2-5). EIGA powders of FGH4096 superalloy showed finer particle size (D50/D90 = 45.9/82.8 µm vs. 63.2/121 µm for VIGA), higher sphericity, and reduced satellite particles (ren2025propertiesofelectrode pages 2-5). For NiTi, EIGA produced powders with D50 ~75 µm, PSD range of 10–180 µm, shape factor ≥95% for small particles, and remarkably low oxygen pickup of only 50 ppm during atomization (total ~0.04 wt%) (sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 8-9). Flow rates for sieved NiTi fractions were 19.3 and 17.5 s/50 g for 15–63 µm and 63–150 µm fractions, respectively (sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 8-9). EIGA is particularly advantageous for reactive alloys (Ti, Zr, superalloys) where crucible contamination is a concern.

### 2.3 Plasma Rotating Electrode Process (PREP)

PREP uses centrifugal force from a rapidly rotating electrode (up to ~15,000 RPM) melted at one end by plasma or arc in an inert chamber. Molten droplets are flung off by centrifugal force and solidify into almost perfectly spherical particles (sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 7-8). PREP produces the highest-quality powders among conventional methods: sphericity >98%, smooth surfaces with minimal satellites, very low oxygen (0.02–0.04 wt% for NiTi), and excellent flowability (sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 8-9, sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 7-8). However, PREP powders are predominantly coarse (>70 µm), with limited yield of fine particles suitable for LPBF (<15% below 50 µm), and the process involves high equipment costs and requires pre-cast electrodes (zhang2025researchprogresson pages 10-11, sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 8-9). PREP is particularly advantageous for directed energy deposition (DED) applications where coarser powders are acceptable (zhang2025researchprogresson pages 10-11).

### 2.4 Close-Coupled Gas Atomization (CCGA)

CCGA positions the gas nozzle in close proximity to the melt delivery tube, achieving higher gas-to-metal momentum ratios and finer powders than free-fall configurations. Mathematical models based on the Lubanska equation have been evaluated for predicting D50, though backpressure phenomena and tip diameter effects require refinement (frolov2025metalpowderproduction pages 2-4). CCGA typically produces PSD of 10–500 µm, with the AM-usable fraction (10–105 µm) requiring sieving and classification.

### 2.5 Additional Emerging Methods

Cold mechanically derived (CMD) powder represents a non-atomization alternative where solid rod stock is mechanically converted to powder at room temperature using a rotating multi-tooth tool. CMD achieves >95% material yield (vs. <20–40% for GA), energy consumption of only ~3.4 kWh/kg (vs. ~11.5 kWh/kg for GA), and produces powders with chemical consistency superior to GA due to avoidance of liquid-state volatilization (12% Mg loss for CMD vs. 26% for GA in Al7075) (martin2023additivemanufacturingof pages 1-2, martin2023additivemanufacturingof pages 2-4, martin2023additivemanufacturingof pages 4-5). CMD powders are non-spherical (sphericity ~0.8–0.85) and require adapted recoating parameters and thicker layers (45 µm vs. 30 µm), but achieved LPBF densities comparable to GA powders (2.77 vs. 2.79 g/cc) and mechanical properties equivalent to wrought material (martin2023additivemanufacturingof pages 2-4, martin2023additivemanufacturingof pages 4-5).

Pulsed shock/detonation wave atomization has also been proposed, using detonation waves (Mach 4–7) to atomize free-falling melt streams, producing particles from 0.1 µm to >400 µm with the finest fractions (0–30 µm) being close to perfectly spherical (frolov2025metalpowderproduction pages 2-4).

## 3. Powder Blending and Mechanical Alloying as Alternatives

### 3.1 In-Situ Alloying via LPBF

Powder blending for in-situ alloying during LPBF involves mechanically mixing elemental or component powders (e.g., via fluidized bed, tumbler, or planetary mixer) and processing the blend directly. This approach enables rapid, low-volume production of custom alloys, potentially reducing feedstock costs and bypassing the atomization step (hassanin2023insitualloying pages 1-2). Demonstrated systems include 60% IN718 + 40% Cu blends achieving >96% relative density, and Ti10Mo from 1 µm Mo particles in Ti powder achieving complete melting and distribution in the alloy matrix (hassanin2023insitualloying pages 1-2). However, challenges include achieving microstructural and chemical homogeneity, with measured composition deviations up to ~8% due to differences in particle size, density, and melting point among constituents, leading to segregation, partial melting, and melt pool instability (hassanin2023insitualloying pages 1-2).

### 3.2 DED Multi-Powder Feeder Systems

Directed energy deposition (DED) with multiple powder feeders represents the most versatile approach for high-throughput alloy screening. Systems with 4–16 feeders enable real-time, continuously variable composition deposition, printing compositional libraries and gradients within single tracks (thoma2023highthroughputsynthesisand pages 2-3, bandyopadhyay2022alloydesignvia pages 7-9, li2025highthroughputphasescreening pages 1-2, li2025highthroughputphasescreening pages 2-5). Using this approach, approximately 1,000 discrete compositions can be synthesized per day via single-bead libraries, making the process at least three orders of magnitude faster than conventional arc-melting or melt-spinning techniques (thoma2023highthroughputsynthesisand pages 2-3). Multi-powder mixing systems with dedicated nozzles and dual-argon shielding minimize composition fluctuations and oxidation (li2025highthroughputphasescreening pages 2-5). Demonstrated applications include Ti-Ni-Nb gradient alloys, high-entropy alloys, metallic glass screening, and high-manganese steels with 0–10 wt% Al addition (li2025highthroughputphasescreening pages 1-2, thoma2023highthroughputsynthesisand pages 2-3, hariharan2025high‐speed3dprinting pages 1-2). Challenges include feed-rate control accuracy (composition variation of 0–1.7% reported), powder waste from cross-contamination of unused powders, and flowability differences among elemental powders of different morphologies (bandyopadhyay2022alloydesignvia pages 7-9, thoma2023highthroughputsynthesisand pages 2-3).

### 3.3 High-Speed DED/EHLA Approaches

Extreme high-speed laser material application (EHLA) combined with in-situ powder mixing enables fabrication of miniature alloy specimens with varied process parameters and compositions simultaneously. This approach produces 20 individual alloy combinations per experimental campaign and has been coupled with machine learning for accelerated process-structure-property relationship discovery (hariharan2025high‐speed3dprinting pages 1-2).

Comprehensive powder characterization is essential for qualifying AM feedstock and understanding processability. Key standards identified in the recent literature include ISO/ASTM 52907 for general powder requirements, ASTM F3049 for characterization guidance, ASTM B213 for Hall flow rate, ASTM B214 for sieve analysis, ASTM B330 for average particle size, and ASTM B527 for tap density (gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 7-8, johnson2024monitoringmodelingand pages 33-34, lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 37-40). PSD is commonly measured by laser diffraction or dynamic image analysis (e.g., Microtrac CAMSIZER) and reported as d10/d50/d90 (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 34-37). Chemical composition verification uses ICP-OES/ICP-MS for elemental analysis and LECO inert gas fusion for oxygen/nitrogen content (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 21-24, johnson2024monitoringmodelingand pages 33-34). SEM provides morphological assessment including sphericity, satellite fraction, and surface quality (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 21-24). Advanced rheological characterization using FT4 powder rheometers provides basic flow energy (BFE), specific energy (SE), conditioned bulk density (CBD), aeration energy (AE), and compressibility indices that correlate with dynamic powder-bed behavior during recoating (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 37-40). Multiple complementary methods are recommended because powders can behave inconsistently across different tests (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 21-24).

## 5. Relationships Between Powder Characteristics and LPBF Processability

### 5.1 Particle Size Distribution

PSD strongly influences spreading, packing, thermal conductivity, laser absorption, and melt pool dynamics in LPBF. The commonly used PSD for LPBF is 15–53 µm, with optimal layer thickness approximately equal to the powder d50 (xue2023effectofparticle pages 1-2, lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 37-40). Fine powders (<10 µm) improve packing density and surface quality but suffer from cohesion, agglomeration, and increased oxidation (xue2023effectofparticle pages 1-2). Coarser powders decrease packing density and laser absorption, narrowing the volumetric energy density (VED) window for full densification, though with proper parameter adjustment coarse powders can still yield fully dense parts (xue2023effectofparticle pages 1-2). Surface roughness increases with increasing coarse fraction—up to ~48% increase observed with high coarse (>60 µm) content—while mechanical properties show modest reductions within approximately ±50 MPa and ~40 HV of benchmarks (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 31-34, lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 50-54).

### 5.2 Morphology and Flowability

Spherical, uniform powders (gas- and plasma-atomized) provide superior flowability, packing density, and consistent layer formation compared to irregular water-atomized or mechanically derived powders (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 21-24). Dynamic flowability—how powders arrange during recoating—is the most critical flow parameter for LPBF, as it directly affects powder-bed uniformity and consequently part density, roughness, and dimensional accuracy (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 24-27, lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 34-37). Highly spherical plasma-atomized powders consistently produce the best surface finish and mechanical properties (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 34-37).

### 5.3 Oxygen Content

Oxygen content is a critical quality attribute, particularly for reactive alloys. In Ti-6Al-4V, oxygen is highly soluble in the α-phase and acts as an α-stabilizer, with a threshold of ~0.13 wt% distinguishing Grade 5 from Grade 23 (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 24-27). Finer gas-atomized powders pick up more oxygen due to higher surface area (e.g., ~0.045 wt% O for 23 µm median vs. ~0.023 wt% for 70–114 µm coarse powder in NiTi) (sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 7-8). Oxygen content correlates more strongly with part hardness than PSD does, with a notable hardness increase observed between 0.07–0.08 wt% O (lalh2023optimizationofti6al4v pages 50-54). Powder reuse also significantly increases oxygen content—NiTi oxygen roughly doubled to ~752 ppm after repeated LPBF cycles (sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 16-18).

## 6. Batch Sizes, Costs, and Lead Times for Custom Alloy Powder Production

AM powders are generally reported to cost 5–10× the price of equivalent bulk material (sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 16-18). For conventional VIGA, batch sizes are typically in the hundreds of kilograms (maximum ~500 kg per batch), requiring substantial upfront investment and long scheduling lead times with commercial atomization service providers (wilkens2025continuousandcostefficient pages 1-3). Continuous gas atomization plants can produce up to 4,000 t/yr with lower unit costs but are designed for high-volume standard alloys rather than custom compositions (wilkens2025continuousandcostefficient pages 3-6).

Ultrasonic atomization (Amazemet rePowder) fundamentally changes the economics of custom alloy development by enabling production from as little as 1 gram up to a few kilograms per batch, with rapid turnaround measured in hours to days rather than weeks to months (ciftcli2025alloydevelopmentfrom pages 1-3). The compact equipment footprint and ability to accept diverse feedstock (wire, rod, chips, scrap, or elemental mixes) make it accessible to university and research-lab environments (ciftcli2025alloydevelopmentfrom pages 1-3). CMD powder production offers on-demand, distributed manufacturing with >95% yield and energy consumption of ~3.4 kWh/kg, representing approximately 70% energy savings compared to gas atomization (martin2023additivemanufacturingof pages 1-2).

For high-throughput alloy screening programs, the literature identifies a multi-tiered strategy as optimal:

**Tier 1 — Computational screening:** CALPHAD-integrated computational frameworks with machine-learning surrogates can evaluate printability across virtually unlimited composition space at ~1,000× the speed of analytical thermal models, generating printability maps that identify defect-free processing windows with up to ~76% accuracy (sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 1-3, sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 37-40, sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 40-42). This narrows the experimental search space dramatically.

**Tier 2 — DED combinatorial printing:** Multi-feeder DED systems enable experimental synthesis of ~1,000 discrete compositions per day using elemental or pre-alloyed powder blends, requiring only grams of material per composition (thoma2023highthroughputsynthesisand pages 2-3). This approach bypasses the need for custom atomization entirely and has been demonstrated for ternary alloy libraries, high-entropy alloys, and metallic glass screening (thoma2023highthroughputsynthesisand pages 2-3, li2025highthroughputphasescreening pages 1-2).

**Tier 3 — Small-batch atomization for LPBF validation:** Once promising compositions are identified, ultrasonic atomization (Amazemet) enables production of 1 g to few kg batches of pre-alloyed spherical powder for LPBF validation, with rapid turnaround and minimal material waste (ciftcli2025alloydevelopmentfrom pages 1-3, zach2023sphericalpowderscontrol pages 4-4). This is preferable to conventional gas atomization, which requires batch sizes orders of magnitude larger.

**Tier 4 — Powder blending for LPBF in-situ alloying:** As an intermediate approach, blending elemental or master-alloy powders for direct LPBF processing allows composition screening without custom atomization, though homogeneity challenges (composition deviations up to ~8%) and segregation risks must be managed through optimized mixing and laser parameters (hassanin2023insitualloying pages 1-2, hariharan2025high‐speed3dprinting pages 1-2).

The high cost and large minimum batch sizes of conventional gas atomization represent the primary bottleneck for experimental high-throughput screening (sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 1-3, hariharan2025high‐speed3dprinting pages 1-2). The combination of computational pre-screening, DED combinatorial synthesis, and small-batch ultrasonic atomization offers the most efficient pathway for rapidly evaluating custom alloy compositions before committing to full-scale powder production for LPBF qualification.
