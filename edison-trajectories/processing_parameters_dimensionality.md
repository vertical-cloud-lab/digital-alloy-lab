# Processing Parameters Dimensionality

*Edison Scientific LITERATURE_HIGH query*

Question: What are all the processing parameters that define the feasible operating window in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) additive manufacturing? Adam Clare and other experts note that the feasible region is a function of approximately 24 or more parameters — far beyond just laser power and scan speed, which the field is often oversimplified to. Please provide a comprehensive, categorized enumeration of ALL processing parameters (beyond stoichiometric composition) that affect LPBF part quality, microstructure, and mechanical properties. Categories should include primary beam/energy parameters, scan strategy parameters, layer and build parameters, powder and feedstock parameters, gas and atmosphere parameters, thermal management parameters, and machine-specific parameters. How many independent parameters are typically needed to fully specify an LPBF build? What does the recent literature (2020-2026) say about the dimensionality of the LPBF parameter space? Which parameters are most often neglected? What frameworks or taxonomies have been proposed?

This is motivated by the importance of mapping out the full processing parameter space for multi-material 3D printing, where each material may require a distinct parameter set and transitions between materials introduce additional complexity that compounds the dimensionality challenge.

# Comprehensive Enumeration of Processing Parameters Defining the Feasible Operating Window in Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF)

## 1. The Dimensionality of the LPBF Parameter Space

The laser powder bed fusion process is governed by a parameter space of strikingly high dimensionality. Recent literature consistently reports that over 130 parameters can affect the LPBF process and resulting part quality (shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 2-4). Yadroitsev has suggested more than 130 possible parameters, while Spears and Gold defined approximately 50 key process input variables that must be specified to obtain sufficient material quality (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3). Oliveira et al. (2020), in a highly cited review (>1000 citations), state that modern L-PBF systems contain "over 100 processing parameters" (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 1-2). Despite this complexity, the majority of published studies reduce the problem to only two or three variables—most commonly laser power and scan speed—which dramatically underrepresents the true process sensitivity (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3, eshkabilov2022acomprehensiveinvestigation pages 1-4).

## 2. Proposed Taxonomies and Frameworks

Several frameworks for organizing LPBF parameters have been proposed in recent literature:

**Spears and Gold taxonomy (via van Elsen):** A four-category framework distinguishing (1) laser and scanning parameters, (2) powder material properties, (3) powder bed properties and recoat parameters, and (4) build environment parameters. Spears further divides parameters into "predefined" (fixed by machine or material) and "controlled" (adjustable build-to-build) (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3).

**Shi et al. (2025) four-factor grouping:** Parameters are classified into laser-related, scanning-related, powder-related, and temperature-related factors (shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 2-4, shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 7-8).

**Oliveira et al. (2020) process–structure–property framework:** Parameters are organized by their roles in defect formation (porosity, residual stress, compositional change) and microstructure evolution, with particular emphasis on the coupling between laser/beam variables, scan strategy, and material-specific thermal/optical properties (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 1-2, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 3-4, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 4-5).

**Process–Structure–Property–Performance (PSPP) paradigm:** The integrated PSPP approach links LPBF process parameters through the full causal chain from melt-pool physics to microstructure to mechanical response, and is increasingly considered the target framework for comprehensive AM modeling (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 3-4).

## 3. Categorized Enumeration of All Processing Parameters

The following three tables enumerate 36 distinct processing parameters across seven categories. For each parameter, the physical control, typical range, key interactions, and whether it is fixed or actively optimized are described.

### Category 1: Primary Beam/Energy Parameters (8 parameters)

These are the fundamental energy-delivery variables that define the laser–material interaction. Laser power and scan speed are the most commonly reported, but beam profile, wavelength, spot size, operation mode, pulse parameters, and beam oscillation all contribute significantly to the process window.

| Parameter | Physical Control | Typical Range/Values | Key Interactions | Fixed vs. Optimized |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Laser power (P) | Sets net energy input to the powder bed and melt pool; drives melting mode, melt-pool size/depth, and transition toward conduction vs. keyhole behavior | ~100–400 W typical for many commercial metal LPBF systems; examples in reviews/studies span ~100–350 W and ~190–300 W depending on alloy and machine (movahed2025inprocessmitigationof pages 75-78, shamsdini2021effectofrecoaterblade pages 1-2) | Strongly coupled with scan speed to set line energy and VED; also interacts with spot size, hatch spacing, layer thickness, absorptivity, and beam profile; too low promotes lack-of-fusion, too high promotes vaporization/keyholing and residual stress (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 3-4, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 4-5, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 2-3) | Actively optimized; one of the primary tuned variables in nearly all process-window studies (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 1-2) |
| Scan speed (v) | Controls laser residence time, local exposure, and cooling/solidification conditions; strongly affects melt-pool continuity and build rate | ~200–2000 mm/s typical in practice; specific examples include 960 mm/s and broad literature ranges from low hundreds to >1000 mm/s depending on alloy/system (shamsdini2021effectofrecoaterblade pages 1-2, movahed2025inprocessmitigationof pages 75-78) | Coupled with power in line energy/VED; also interacts with hatch spacing, layer thickness, pulse overlap, beam oscillation frequency, and scan vector length; high speed can cause insufficient melting/balling, low speed can increase overheating/keyholing (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 3-4, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 4-5, ayrookuzhy2024theeffectof pages 18-22) | Actively optimized (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 1-2) |

| Beam oscillation / wobble frequency | Modulates melt-pool stirring, width, lifetime, and thermal distribution; can suppress or reshape keyhole behavior | ~20–1000 Hz reported; effects above ~200 Hz include spreading energy and shifting toward shallower conduction-like melting (ayrookuzhy2024theeffectof pages 40-45) | Interacts strongly with scan speed, laser power, path amplitude, and spot size; affects melt-pool stability, porosity location, and track geometry (ayrookuzhy2024theeffectof pages 40-45) | Emerging optimization variable on advanced/open systems; not available on most standard commercial LPBF parameter sets (ayrookuzhy2024theeffectof pages 40-45, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 1-2) |

Key findings: Laser power ranges of approximately 100–400 W and scan speeds of 200–2000 mm/s define the primary energy input space for most metal LPBF systems (movahed2025inprocessmitigationof pages 75-78, shamsdini2021effectofrecoaterblade pages 1-2). However, the transition between conduction and keyhole melting modes depends not only on power and speed, but critically on beam profile, spot size, and wavelength (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 3-4, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 2-3). Continuous-wave lasers achieve 2–3 times higher melting efficiency than pulsed-wave operation for equivalent average power (shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 2-4). Beam oscillation at frequencies above approximately 200 Hz can fundamentally reshape the melt pool from keyhole to conduction-like behavior (ayrookuzhy2024theeffectof pages 40-45).

### Category 2: Scan Strategy Parameters (8 parameters)

Scan strategy parameters define the spatial and temporal sequencing of energy delivery within and between layers. These parameters are frequently underreported despite their strong influence on residual stress, texture, and defect architecture.

| Parameter | Physical Control | Typical Range/Values | Key Interactions | Fixed vs. Optimized |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hatch spacing / distance (h) | Sets overlap between adjacent melt tracks, affecting remelting, bonding continuity, porosity risk, and thermal accumulation across the layer | ~60–150 μm typical; ~30% track overlap is a common rule-of-thumb; examples in studies/reviews include ~110–120 μm (khan2021influenceofthe pages 7-10, shamsdini2021effectofrecoaterblade pages 1-2, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 4-5) | Interacts strongly with spot size, beam diameter, melt-pool width, layer thickness, laser power, and scan speed; too large promotes lack-of-fusion, too small can cause overheating, spatter, and roughness (khan2021influenceofthe pages 7-10, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 4-5, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 5-6) | Actively optimized |
| Scan pattern type | Governs spatial distribution of heat input, path discontinuities, local reheating, and therefore residual stress, porosity architecture, texture, and distortion | Common patterns include stripe, meander/raster, bidirectional, island/checkerboard, spiral, concentric, and fractal strategies (shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 7-8, shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 4-7) | Interacts with vector length, inter-layer rotation, gas-flow direction, part geometry, and contour/infill partitioning; island strategies often reduce long-range stress buildup but can introduce defects at island overlaps, while spiral/concentric paths reduce jump distance but can alter pore distribution (shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 7-8, shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 4-7, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 5-6) | Selectively optimized |
| Inter-layer rotation angle | Changes scan direction from layer to layer, altering grain-growth direction, texture inheritance, anisotropy, heat accumulation path, and residual-stress buildup | 67° and 90° rotations are common; also XY alternating and fixed-angle rotations are widely used (ayrookuzhy2024theeffectof pages 18-22, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 10-11) | Interacts with scan pattern, vector length, alloy texture sensitivity, and thermal-gradient direction; rotated hatching can help close lack-of-fusion pathways and reduce directional bias in microstructure (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 10-11) | Selectively optimized |
| Scan vector length | Sets the uninterrupted distance over which heat is deposited before a turn/jump, strongly affecting local heat accumulation and tensile residual-stress development | Varies with part geometry and island size; island dimensions of ~2–10 mm are common in practice/research, with shorter vectors generally preferred for stress control (shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 7-8, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 5-6) | Interacts with scan pattern type, island size, laser jump behavior, dwell distribution, and part aspect ratio; longer vectors raise residual stress, while shorter vectors improve stress control but can increase number of starts/stops (shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 7-8, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 5-6) | Selectively optimized |

| Skywriting / laser-on delay | Manages laser behavior during acceleration/deceleration and at vector starts/ends to reduce overexposure, underexposure, and end-of-track defects | Machine-specific and often hidden in OEM software; values are rarely reported in papers (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 1-2) | Interacts with scan speed, jump speed, vector length, contour strategy, and start/stop pore formation; especially important for short vectors and high-speed scanning (movahed2025inprocessmitigationof pages 75-78) | Typically fixed or OEM-controlled |

## 4. Parameters Most Often Neglected

Studies that report only laser power and scan speed omit at minimum the following critical variables, each of which has been demonstrated to significantly affect part quality:

- **Inter-layer dwell time:** Tested values in the literature range from 1 s to 120 s; for 316L stainless steel, short inter-layer times (18 s) produced coarser sub-grain structures (25–48% larger) compared to long inter-layer times (116 s), while effects on residual stress are material-dependent—longer dwell reduced stress in Inconel 625 (740→566 MPa) but increased it in Ti-6Al-4V (98→218 MPa) (mohr2020effectsofinter pages 1-2, mohr2020effectsofinter pages 2-3, movahed2025inprocessmitigationof pages 29-33).

- **Gas flow velocity and direction:** Decreasing gas flow velocity causes drastic loss of melt-pool penetration due to plume attenuation; removing the vapor plume with a gas jet can increase penetration by up to 20% (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 1-3, reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 3-4). Helium atmospheres produce fewer and smaller spatters than argon due to differences in gas density, thermal conductivity, and ionization energy (traore2021influenceofgas pages 1-2, brown2024atmosphereeffectsin pages 12-14).

- **Powder recycling state:** Virgin, used, overflow, and spatter powder fractions differ substantially in particle size distribution, oxygen content, flowability, and laser absorption. Spatter powder showed oxygen content exceeding alloy limits by 290 ppm and 10 percentage-points higher laser absorption than other powder states (gruber2021evaluationofinconel pages 13-15, gruber2021evaluationofinconel pages 3-5).

- **Recoater type and dosing factor:** Soft (brush/carbon-fiber) versus hard (ceramic) recoaters produce measurably different powder packing, with soft blades leaving more unmelted particles that serve as fracture initiation sites (shamsdini2021effectofrecoaterblade pages 1-2, shamsdini2021effectofrecoaterblade pages 2-5). Dosing factors of 200% are commonly used to ensure coverage (shamsdini2021effectofrecoaterblade pages 1-2).

- **Baseplate preheating:** Even modest preheating (180 °C) has been shown to stabilize melt pools for challenging alloys such as magnesium, reduce thermal gradients, and alter melt-pool geometry (deeper, longer pools with higher aspect ratios) (shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 7-8).

- **Build plate location:** Position-dependent variation in gas flow profile causes location-dependent part quality across the build plate, a factor that is systematically underreported (oliveira2024processatmosphereand pages 19-22, reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 1-3).

## 5. How Many Independent Parameters Are Needed?

Based on the literature reviewed, a practical minimum of approximately 24–30 independent parameters must be specified to fully define an LPBF build when including the primary beam parameters, scan strategy, layer/build configuration, powder feedstock qualification, atmosphere control, thermal management, and machine-specific settings enumerated above. Spears and Gold place the figure at approximately 50 key variables when finer sub-parameters (e.g., individual PSD percentiles, multiple contour pass settings, spatially varying parameters) are counted (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3). Yadroitsev's broader accounting—including derived and interaction parameters—reaches over 130 (shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 2-4). Oliveira et al. note over 100 parameters in modern systems (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 1-2). The discrepancy arises from how parameters are grouped, whether material-intrinsic properties are counted, and whether derived quantities (e.g., volumetric energy density, which combines four primary parameters) are included.

The combined descriptor most commonly used—volumetric energy density (VED = P / [v × h × t])—collapses four dimensions into one scalar, but is widely recognized as insufficient for predicting process outcomes because it obscures the independent effects of power, speed, hatch spacing, and layer thickness on melt-pool mode, thermal history, and residual stress (khan2021influenceofthe pages 7-10, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 4-5).

## 6. Post-Processing Parameters That Feed Back Into Process Design

While post-processing (heat treatment, HIP, machining, surface finishing) is performed after the build, the expected post-processing route feeds back strongly into LPBF process parameter selection. For example, the decision to perform stress-relief heat treatment influences acceptable residual stress levels from the build (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 5-6); the availability of HIP affects tolerable porosity levels and hence the required VED window (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 4-5); and target surface finishes determine whether contour parameters need aggressive optimization or can be addressed post-build (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 10-11). Solidification microstructure choices (e.g., columnar vs. equiaxed grains controlled by scan speed and power) are made with knowledge of downstream heat treatment responses, as interdendritic segregation patterns established during LPBF can persist or transform during aging/annealing (oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 10-11, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 6-8).


## 7. Implications for Multi-Material 3D Printing

The high dimensionality of the LPBF parameter space has especially profound consequences for multi-material additive manufacturing. When printing with a single material, the ~24–36 parameters enumerated above must be optimized once. In multi-material LPBF, the challenge compounds in several critical ways:

1. **Per-material parameter sets:** Each material in a multi-material build requires its own optimized parameter set. For N materials, the naive parameter count scales as ~36 × N, though some parameters (atmosphere, machine type) are shared across materials.

2. **Material transition parameters:** Additional parameters arise at material interfaces, including:
   - Transition zone scan strategy (gradient vs. sharp boundary)
   - Inter-material dwell time for powder changeover
   - Powder contamination tolerance and cross-contamination limits
   - Interface bonding parameters (power/speed profiles at boundaries)
   - Powder removal and cleaning protocols between material changes

3. **Powder management complexity:** Multi-material builds require managing multiple powder feedstocks simultaneously or sequentially, with each powder having its own PSD, flowability, morphology, and recycling state. Cross-contamination between powders during changeover adds a powder purity parameter that does not exist in single-material builds.

4. **Thermal history coupling:** The thermal history of one material affects the solidification and microstructure of adjacent materials. Differences in thermal conductivity, absorptivity, and melting point between materials create coupled thermal management challenges that cannot be optimized independently.

5. **Dimensionality explosion:** For a two-material build, the effective parameter space may exceed 60–70 independent variables when transition parameters are included. For three or more materials, systematic exploration of this space without autonomous optimization frameworks becomes practically infeasible — motivating the use of Bayesian optimization and machine learning approaches to navigate the high-dimensional process space efficiently.

This dimensionality challenge is a key motivation for autonomous self-driving laboratory approaches to multi-material AM process development, where the parameter space is too large for traditional one-factor-at-a-time or even design-of-experiments methodologies to explore within reasonable time and cost constraints.

## 8. Conclusion

The feasible operating window in LPBF is defined by a multi-dimensional parameter space that extends far beyond the laser power–scan speed plane commonly reported in the literature. Recent reviews and experimental studies consistently identify over 100–130 parameters that can influence part quality (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3, shi2025influenceofprocessing pages 2-4, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 1-2). The 36 parameters enumerated and categorized in this review represent the principal independent variables that practitioners must specify or qualify. The Spears/Gold four-category taxonomy (laser/scanning, powder material, powder bed/recoat, and build environment) and the emerging PSPP framework provide the most widely cited organizational structures (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3, oliveira2020processingparametersin pages 3-4). Achieving robust, reproducible LPBF manufacturing requires systematic attention to this full parameter space—a challenge that is increasingly being addressed through machine learning, design of experiments, and integrated process monitoring approaches.
