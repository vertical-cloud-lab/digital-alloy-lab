# Infrared laser deposition of aluminum bronzes: evidence and limits

**Bottom line.** A published InssTek powder-DED experiment deposited *nickel-free* Cu–10Al–1Fe using **500 W average power**, but its controller could deliver **750 W peak power**. It is therefore **not** proof that a laser limited to 500 W can make a dense aluminum-bronze deposit. Neither that experiment nor the other studies examined here demonstrates a dense, single-stream C95300, C63000, or C95800 powder deposit fused to **thick cast C95800 plate at ≤500 W maximum power**. No verified thickness of such a fused NAB substrate can be given. A separate **300 W** NAB result is *laser powder-bed fusion (LPBF)*, not blown-powder DED or repair. (makarenko2020analyticalevaluationof pages 3-4, makarenko2020analyticalevaluationof pages 4-8, doyle2022additivemanufacturingof pages 47-53, huang2026densificationbehaviorand pages 4-6)

## Near-infrared absorption and thermal transport

**[CITE] [NUMBERS]** R. Webb, “Absolute Absorptance Measurements on Copper-Based Alloys at Infrared Wavelengths,” *International Journal of Thermophysics* **10** (1989) 513–525. DOI: **10.1007/BF01133547**. Direct calorimetric measurement at **1.06 µm**, near-normal incidence, approximately **20 °C**, on *polished solid coupons*, gave: **4.85%** for C101 pure Cu; **11.1%** for C608 (**Cu–5.5Al**); **8.90%** for C614 (**Cu–6.3Al–3.68Fe**); **18.8%** for C706 (**Cu–10.9Ni–1.59Fe**); and **29.4%** for C715 (**Cu–30Ni–0.48Fe–0.30Mn**). These are **measured, composition-specific** values—not measurements of C95300, C63000 or C95800. **Thermal-conductivity comparison—what can actually be substantiated.** Hatala’s **34.1 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹** is a **23 °C wire-built NAB measurement**, and Makarenko used **75 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹** for its Cu–10Al–1Fe bronze in a heat-flow *calculation*; neither is a verified room-temperature datasheet measurement of **C95300**. A retrieved secondary source gives approximately **29 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ for C71500**, but the search did **not** verify a consistent, traceable, like-for-like set of values for **C95300, cast C95800, wrought C63000, C70600 and C71500**, measured at the same temperature and condition. Consequently, an exact five-alloy ranking, especially a numerical C95300-versus-C95800 comparison, **cannot responsibly be supplied from the inspected evidence**. The defensible qualitative point is that these alloyed bronzes and Cu–Ni alloys are less copper-like heat conductors than pure Cu, while substrate geometry and temperature remain decisive. (hatala2024aninvestigationof pages 4-5, makarenko2020analyticalevaluationof pages 10-16)

## Implications for a 500 W, 0.4 mm fiber-laser machine

At a nominal **0.4 mm diameter**, 500 W corresponds to roughly **4.0 MW/cm² incident average intensity** and **75 J/mm incident line energy at 0.4 m/min**, before reflection, powder interception and heat conduction. These are *calculations*, **not a qualified process setting**. Webb’s polished-solid alloy results suggest aluminum bronzes need not couple infrared light as poorly as pure copper, but their Cu–Al–Fe/Ni coupling **at the actual powder-stream and molten-plate interfaces remains unmeasured**. Moreover, the first layer on cold, thick cast NAB must melt *both powder and sufficient substrate*: its fusion requirement is different from reheating a small existing bronze track or depositing onto steel. (makarenko2020analyticalevaluationof pages 4-8, hatala2024aninvestigationof pages 4-5, webb1989absoluteabsorptancemeasurements pages 7-11, brandau2022absorbancestudyof pages 6-8)

A cautious experimental ordering is **C95300/Cu–Al–Fe on itself first**, then the **C63000 powder** on itself and copper-alloy substrate, and finally **C95800 on thick cast C95800**, with each *first-layer fusion boundary* sectioned independently. This is a **test priority, not an established ranking of achievable density or corrosion**. C95300’s nickel-free chemistry does not make it a validated as-deposited seawater substitute for 70–30 Cu–Ni. C63000’s approximate **10% Al** also exceeds the stated **9.5%** design ceiling; using the proposed C63000-bar atomization trial as a powder-production and fusion benchmark must not be mistaken for qualifying its retained-β′ microstructure or its compliance with that ceiling. Particularly for high-Al material, measure deposited composition, α/β′/κ/γ₂ populations, and attack at the clad, dilution zone and parent NAB—not merely density and surface hardness. (zhao2022microstructureandmechanical pages 2-4, huang2026densificationbehaviorand pages 2-4, huo2025modulatingheatinput pages 10-13, fayanas2025nickelaluminiumbronze pages 2-5)

The most important **[GAP]** is a controlled, *as-deposited* experiment comparing pre-alloyed C95300-family and compliant NAB-family blown powders, deposited on themselves **and on measured-thickness cast C95800**, against **wrought C71500 tested concurrently** under the *same stated* natural or ASTM D1141 substitute seawater, temperature, oxygenation, flow and duration. Report actual laser **peak/average power and measured spot**, delivered powder mass flow/size/oxygen, substrate preheat and thickness, fusion penetration and cross-sectional dilution, volumetric density and pore distribution, cracking, direction-resolved tensile results, **i₍corr₎ with reference electrode, gravimetric rate and deepest selective attack**. None of the cited low-power, LPBF, steel-cladding or arc-wire results alone closes that gap. (makarenko2020analyticalevaluationof pages 4-8, doyle2022additivemanufacturingof pages 47-53, huo2025modulatingheatinput pages 4-5, fayanas2025nickelaluminiumbronze pages 5-7)

## Top 5 things the PI should know

1. **500 W average is not 500 W maximum.** The closest InssTek Cu–Al–Fe powder result permits **750 W peaks** and does not qualify a thick NAB repair. (makarenko2020analyticalevaluationof pages 4-8)
2. **The first-layer interface is the decisive demonstration.** Report the actual NAB plate thickness and sectioned fusion/dilution; the reviewed sources provide no verified ≤500 W aluminum-bronze powder fusion into thick cast C95800. (zhao2022microstructureandmechanical pages 2-4, makarenko2020analyticalevaluationof pages 4-8)
3. **Steel cladding proves a useful process exists, not that it transfers unchanged:** CuAl10Fe1 on steel used **1.5–4 kW**, and dilution shifted from **3–9%** to **21%** as powder feed fell. (freisse2016investigationsondry pages 3-5, freisse2016investigationsondry pages 1-2)
4. **Measure coupling rather than assign pure-Cu constants:** polished Cu–Al alloys absorbed **8.9–11.1%** at 1.06 µm versus pure Cu **4.85%** and C715 **29.4%**; no verified corresponding C95300/C63000/C95800 stream-and-melt measurements were found. (webb1989absoluteabsorptancemeasurements pages 4-7, webb1989absoluteabsorptancemeasurements pages 7-11)
5. **Density and strength do not establish as-deposited seawater equivalence.** LPBF’s **300 W** dense NAB retained β′ and showed **9.03% elongation**; as-deposited wire-laser NAB reached **>99.5% density** but its cited study explicitly lacked corrosion qualification. Concurrent, sufficiently long C71500-controlled marine tests remain necessary. (doyle2022additivemanufacturingof pages 47-53, doyle2022additivemanufacturingof pages 53-58, huang2026densificationbehaviorand pages 10-12, fayanas2025nickelaluminiumbronze pages 5-7)
