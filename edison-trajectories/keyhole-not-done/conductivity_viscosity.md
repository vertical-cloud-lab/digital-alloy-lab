## Thermophysical Properties for LPBF Keyhole and Melt-Pool Modelling of Al-Si Alloys

### (a) Thermal Conductivity of As-Built LPBF AlSi10Mg

The thermal conductivity of as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg is anisotropic and process-dependent. Azizi et al. (2023) measured samples by flash diffusivity on graphite-coated 8 × 8 × 3.5 mm coupons and found a maximum through-plane conductivity of 136 ± 2 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ (at 59 J/mm³ energy density) and a maximum in-plane conductivity of 117 ± 2 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ (at 50 J/mm³), all at room temperature (azizi2023processdependentanisotropicthermal pages 1-2, azizi2023processdependentanisotropicthermal pages 2-3). Sélo et al. (2020) reported comparable as-built values of 119 ± 5 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ in the build direction and 103 ± 5 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ in the transverse direction (selo2020onthethermal pages 1-2, selo2020onthethermal pages 2-3). The reduced conductivity relative to pure aluminium (~237 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹) is attributed to the supersaturated Si in the α-Al matrix and the fine Si cellular network that acts as an electron–phonon interface resistance (azizi2023processdependentanisotropicthermal pages 2-3). Butler et al. (2021) used a Wiedemann–Franz approach (four-wire electrical resistivity at 20 °C, Lorenz number L = 2.1 × 10⁻⁸ WΩ/K², phonon term k_g ≈ 10.5–13.6 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹) and extrapolated the zero-porosity dense-material conductivity to 143.9 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ as-built and 191.5 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ after annealing (butler2021effectsofprocessing pages 4-5, butler2021effectsofprocessing pages 7-9).

**Effect of heat treatment.** All post-processing heat treatments increase the thermal conductivity by precipitating Si from the supersaturated matrix and coarsening the cellular Si network. Butler et al. found a 18–41% increase after annealing at 300–500 °C for 1–5 h, with 300 °C for 2 h being nearly sufficient for maximum improvement; porosity was unchanged (butler2021effectsofprocessing pages 1-2, butler2021effectsofprocessing pages 7-9). Sélo et al. reported that a 300 °C/2 h anneal raised conductivity to 173 ± 10 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ in both directions (anisotropy eliminated), a solution treatment at 520 °C gave +36%, and a T6-like cycle (520 °C/1 h + WQ + 160 °C/6 h) gave the largest increase at +44% (selo2020onthethermal pages 1-2, selo2020onthethermal pages 2-3). Liu et al. (2026) demonstrated that direct aging at 200 °C for 10 h (measured by LFA 447 laser flash) achieved 173.70 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹, a 44.3% improvement over the as-built state (liu2026synergisticenhancementof pages 2-5).

**Temperature-dependent data (RT to solidus).** A notable gap in the literature is that existing studies on LPBF AlSi10Mg report thermal conductivity predominantly at room temperature. Temperature-dependent laser-flash data from room temperature up to the solidus (~555–570 °C for AlSi10Mg) specifically for LPBF-processed material were not found in the accessible literature. For modelling purposes, the temperature dependence of cast AlSi10Mg or AlSi12 may be used as a proxy, recognising that the LPBF microstructure will evolve (Si precipitation) during heating toward the solidus.

### (b) Thermal Conductivity of Hypereutectic Al-Si Alloys and Pure Silicon

**Pure silicon.** Fulkerson et al. (1968) measured the thermal conductivity, electrical resistivity, and Seebeck coefficient of 99.99+% pure silicon from 100 to 1300 K using an absolute radial heat-flow method above 387 K, with uncertainties of approximately ±2% (fulkerson1968thermalconductivityelectrical pages 10-11, fulkerson1968thermalconductivityelectrical pages 1-2). The thermal conductivity decreases monotonically with temperature above room temperature; the lattice thermal resistivity follows approximately linear segments with an abrupt change in slope near 670 K (45% increase in slope above 670 K). The electronic contribution becomes significant only above ~900 K. Representative values from Glassbrenner and Slack (1964, cited therein) and Fulkerson et al. are approximately: 148 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ at 300 K, ~80 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ at 500 K, ~43 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ at 800 K, and ~25 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ at 1200 K (DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.167.765) (fulkerson1968thermalconductivityelectrical pages 14-15).

**Hypereutectic Al-Si alloys.** The key reference for A390 (Al–17Si–4Cu) thermal diffusivity in both the solid and liquid states is Kaschnitz and Ebner (2007), "Thermal diffusivity of the aluminum alloy Al–17Si–4Cu (A390) in the solid and liquid states," *Int. J. Thermophysics* 28, 711–722, DOI: 10.1007/s10765-007-0172-4. This paper uses laser flash analysis (LFA) and reports thermal diffusivity from room temperature through the solidus and into the liquid phase. Unfortunately, the full text was not accessible during this search, but it is widely cited as the primary source for temperature-dependent thermal transport data for hypereutectic Al-Si casting alloys.

General literature data indicate that the room-temperature thermal conductivity of Al-Si alloys decreases with increasing Si content from pure Al (~237 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹). For alloys containing 17–25 wt% Si, typical room-temperature values range from approximately 110 to 130 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹, depending strongly on microstructure (Si morphology, Cu content, heat treatment) (alberoniUnknownyearanalysisofmicrostructure pages 35-41). The thermal conductivity of these alloys is expected to decrease moderately with temperature up to the solidus, qualitatively following the behaviour of pure Al and of the Si phase.

### (c) Dynamic Viscosity of Liquid Al and Al-Si Alloys

**Reference correlation for eutectic Al+Si (12.0 wt% Si).** Assael, Mihailidou, and Brillo (2012) critically evaluated all available oscillating-cup viscosity measurements for eutectic liquid Al+Si and established the following reference correlations (assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 6-7, assael2012referencecorrelationfora pages 6-7, assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 3-4, assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 4-6):

**Density:** ρ = 2603 − 0.241T (kg·m⁻³), valid 858–1700 K, 2.0% uncertainty (2σ), Table 2 (assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 4-6).

**Viscosity:** log₁₀(η / 1 mPa·s) = 0.8022 + 658.34/T (K), valid 860–1275 K, 7.7% uncertainty (2σ), Table 5 (assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 6-7, assael2012referencecorrelationfora pages 6-7). This can be rewritten as η = 10^(0.8022 + 658.34/T) mPa·s. This form is equivalent to an Arrhenius-type expression.

The earlier reference by Assael et al. (2006), "Reference data for the density and viscosity of liquid Aluminum and liquid Iron," *J. Phys. Chem. Ref. Data* 35, 285–300, DOI: 10.1063/1.2149380, provides the benchmark values for pure liquid aluminium. It was not directly accessed here, but it is cited in the 2012 paper and in Turchi (2018) as the standard reference for pure Al viscosity (turchi2018viscosityandsurface pages 18-22).

**Arrhenius parameters for Al-Si alloys (rotational rheometer).** Betzou, Auinger, and Srirangam (2025) measured viscosity using a rotational rheometer at a shear rate of 10 s⁻¹ and fitted the Arrhenius form η = η₀ exp(E_A/RT) (betzou2021thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 64-70, betzou2025effectofmelt pages 2-4, betzou2025effectofmelt pages 1-2):
- **Al–7 wt% Si:** η₀ = 0.19151 ± 0.130, E_A = 1476.6 ± 639 J·mol⁻¹; viscosity 1.01 Pa·s at 15 K superheat.
- **Al–20 wt% Si:** η₀ = 0.10085 ± 0.035, E_A = 1410.0 ± 348 J·mol⁻¹; viscosity 0.436 Pa·s at 10 K superheat, measured through 100 K superheat.

These rotational-rheometer values are orders of magnitude higher than the oscillating-cup values (sub-mPa·s range), which the authors attribute to oxide contamination and alumina crucible reactions (betzou2021thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 60-64, betzou2025effectofmelt pages 2-4). The oscillating-cup measurements should be preferred for clean-melt modelling. Silicon addition lowers the viscosity of liquid Al alloys (betzou2021thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 64-70).

### (d) Temperature Coefficient of Surface Tension for Al-Si and AlSi10Mg Melts

**Containerless measurements (EML, oxygen-minimised).** Seimiya, Yoneyama, and Ozawa (2026) measured surface tension of pure Al, pure Si, and Al–Si alloys (5–80 at% Si) using the oscillating-drop method with electromagnetic levitation. Only data from oxide-free droplet surfaces (confirmed by high-speed video) were analysed (seimiya2026surfacetensionof pages 1-3). For all compositions, the surface tension decreased linearly with temperature. The temperature coefficient dσ/dT becomes slightly less negative with increasing Si content, from −0.105 × 10⁻³ N·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ for pure Al to −0.084 × 10⁻³ N·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ for pure Si (seimiya2026surfacetensionof pages 3-6, seimiya2026surfacetensionof pages 6-8). For the composition closest to AlSi10Mg (Al–20 at% Si ≈ 11.3 wt% Si), σ_L = 855.5 ± 12.9 × 10⁻³ N·m⁻¹ at T_L = 964 K, with dσ/dT = −0.099 ± 0.003 × 10⁻³ N·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ (seimiya2026surfacetensionof pages 6-8). For the hypereutectic Al–24.3 wt% Si blend, the nearest data point is Al–40 at% Si (≈25.3 wt% Si): σ_L = 821.2 × 10⁻³ N·m⁻¹ at T_L = 1210 K, dσ/dT = −0.095 × 10⁻³ N·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ (seimiya2026surfacetensionof pages 6-8). The Butler equation with an ideal-solution approximation reproduced these composition-dependent temperature coefficients (seimiya2026surfacetensionof pages 11-13).

**Effect of oxygen.** Literature reviews compiled by Turchi (2018) and by Mills and Su report markedly different values for nominally "pure" Al versus oxygen-saturated Al (flood2020thermophysicalpropertiesmeasurement pages 104-106, turchi2018viscosityandsurface pages 70-72, turchi2018viscosityandsurface pages 72-75):
- **Nominally pure Al:** σ = 1050 − 0.25(T − 933) mN·m⁻¹, so dσ/dT = −0.25 × 10⁻³ N·m⁻¹·K⁻¹.
- **Oxygen-saturated Al:** σ = 875 − 0.18(T − 933) mN·m⁻¹, so dσ/dT = −0.18 × 10⁻³ N·m⁻¹·K⁻¹.

Oxygen adsorption lowers the absolute surface tension at the melting point (from ~1050 to ~875 mN·m⁻¹) and makes the temperature coefficient less negative. This range is consistent with the spread of −dσ/dT values in the literature for pure Al: 0.93 × 10⁻⁴ to 3.88 × 10⁻⁴ N·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ depending on the method and oxygen control, as compiled by Flood (2020) (flood2020thermophysicalpropertiesmeasurement pages 104-106, flood2020thermophysicalpropertiesmeasurement pages 161-163).

**Positive dσ/dT (Marangoni sign reversal).** Giuranno et al. (2006) demonstrated using dynamic sessile-drop measurements of the Al–O system (873–1323 K) that in certain temperature–oxygen partial pressure windows, the sign of dσ/dT inverts from negative to positive (giuranno2006dynamicsurfacetension pages 2-3, giuranno2006dynamicsurfacetension pages 3-4). This inversion occurs when oxygen segregation at the surface dominates the temperature dependence of adsorption—analogous to the well-known effect of sulphur/oxygen in steel weld pools. Under the Knudsen regime (P_tot = 10⁻³ Pa, P_O₂ = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴ Pa), sharp inversion points were observed; at atmospheric pressure with low Ar flow rates (0.05 L/min, inlet P_O₂ = 0.4 Pa), the sample remained permanently in the oxidation regime with no inversions, while at higher flow rates (0.7 L/min), inversions reappeared (giuranno2006dynamicsurfacetension pages 3-4, giuranno2006dynamicsurfacetension pages 4-5). This sign reversal of dσ/dT has critical implications for the direction of Marangoni flow in the LPBF melt pool: with a negative coefficient, flow is directed outward from the hot centre of the melt pool, whereas a positive coefficient (oxygen-induced) reverses the flow inward, deepening the melt pool.
