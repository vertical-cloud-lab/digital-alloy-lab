## 1. The NSF Proposal & Award Policies & Procedures Guide (PAPPG)

### Canonical Primary Sources

The foundational document governing all NSF proposal preparation is the *Proposal & Award Policies & Procedures Guide* (PAPPG), currently NSF 24-1 (effective for proposals submitted or due on or after May 20, 2024). The PAPPG supersedes the earlier *Grant Proposal Guide* (GPG) and *Award & Administration Guide* (AAG). NSF periodically issues supplements and important notices (e.g., NSF 26-200, 26-202) that modify or augment PAPPG provisions; PIs must always consult the most current version at nsf.gov. The "Gold Standard Science" framing introduced under the current NSF leadership emphasizes scientific excellence and rigor as the primary organizing principle of NSF investments.

### Key Structural Requirements

The PAPPG mandates a precise proposal architecture. The **Project Summary** is limited to one page and must contain an Overview, a separately labeled Intellectual Merit statement, and a separately labeled Broader Impacts statement; proposals that fail to include these elements "will not be accepted" or will be returned without review (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 14-16, guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 36-38). The **Project Description** is limited to 15 pages (including all visual materials and up to five pages for Results from Prior NSF Support) and must be self-contained—URLs may not be used to provide information necessary for review (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 58-60, guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 36-38). The Project Description must include a separate section addressing Broader Impacts. **References Cited** has no page limit but must contain only bibliographic citations and not be used to extend the narrative (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 58-60). Additional required sections include the Cover Sheet, Table of Contents (auto-generated), Biographical Sketches (with headings for Professional Preparation, Appointments, Products, Synergistic Activities, and Collaborators & Other Affiliations), Budget and Budget Justification, Current and Pending Support, Facilities/Equipment/Resources, and Data Management Plan (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 16-18, guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 18-19).

For any PI/co-PI with NSF support in the past five years, the **Results from Prior NSF Support** section must include the award number, amount, period, project title, and separate subsections documenting Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts outcomes, along with publications and evidence of data/product availability (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 36-38).

### Consensus Best Practices

Compliance is non-negotiable: FastLane/Research.gov systems block noncompliant submissions, and deviations from formatting requirements (font, margin, spacing, page limits) require advance written authorization (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 58-60). An internal administrative review before submission is "strongly recommended" (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 58-60). Single-copy documents—including suggested/excluded reviewers, proprietary information statements, and proposal certifications—must be submitted by the Authorized Organizational Representative within five working days (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 58-60).

### Actionable Rules for 2026

1. Build every proposal to the official PAPPG architecture first: compliant Cover Sheet, 1-page Project Summary with labeled IM/BI sections, 15-page Project Description with a distinct BI section, References, Biosketches, Budget, Facilities, and all supplementary documents.
2. Treat the Project Summary as the single most important page—it is the first and often the only text some reviewers read closely (cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 3-4).
3. Check the most recent PAPPG edition and any active supplements (e.g., NSF 26-200/26-202) before submission; requirements change with each edition (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 14-16).

## 2. The Two NSF Merit Review Criteria: Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts

### History and Framework

NSF adopted the current two-criterion merit review framework in 1997, replacing a multi-criterion system that had evolved since the agency's first grants in 1951 (rothenberg2010makingjudgmentsabout pages 1-2). The criteria were revised in 2007 to "promote potentially transformative research" and again in 2012 to "revise the elements considered by reviewers" and "articulate the principles upon which the criteria are based" (chen2022systemicracialdisparities pages 31-32). **Intellectual Merit** is defined as "the potential to advance knowledge and understanding within its own field or across different fields," while **Broader Impacts** addresses "the potential to benefit society and contribute to the achievement of specific, desired societal outcomes" (berdanier2016characterizationofintellectual pages 1-3, guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 69-71).

### The Five Review Elements

Reviewers evaluate both criteria through five elements: (1) the potential to advance knowledge (IM) or benefit society (BI); (2) the extent to which the proposal explores creative, original, or potentially transformative concepts; (3) whether the plan is well-reasoned, well-organized, based on a sound rationale, and includes mechanisms for assessment; (4) the qualifications of the individual, team, or organization; and (5) the adequacy of resources available to the PI (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 69-71). Three overarching guiding principles frame interpretation: high quality and transformative potential, contribution to societal goals, and meaningful assessment and evaluation (rankins2017aninvestigationof pages 33-37, guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 69-71).

### Broader Impacts: Challenges and Best Practices

More than 27 years after their introduction, Broader Impacts remain the more challenging criterion for many researchers (mardis2012towardbroaderimpacts pages 1-2, iverson2024thereliabilityand pages 1-2). The NSF lists illustrative BI outcomes (e.g., full participation of women and underrepresented minorities in STEM, improved STEM education, enhanced scientific literacy) but is intentionally non-prescriptive (rankins2017aninvestigationof pages 37-41). The **ARIS Broader Impacts Toolkit**—comprising Guiding Principles, a Planning Checklist, a BI Wizard, and a BI Rubric—was developed to help researchers design reviewable BI plans that align with NSF expectations (hotaling2024thearisbroader pages 1-2, hotaling2024thearisbroader pages 2-4). The BI Rubric maps to NSF guiding questions and supports both self-assessment and panel review (iverson2024thereliabilityand pages 1-2). Best practices include identifying specific audiences, setting measurable goals using S.M.A.R.T. criteria, incorporating explicit evaluation plans, and partnering with BI/EPO professionals rather than producing boilerplate text (hotaling2024thearisbroader pages 1-2, mcnall2024enablingimpactsbuilding pages 10-12, gold2022creativeevaluationto pages 1-3).

### Key Disagreement

A persistent tension exists over whether BI should be weighted equally with IM in practice. Empirical analyses have shown that even successful proposals often include "aspirational or incomplete claims of impact" (mardis2012towardbroaderimpacts pages 1-2), and researchers report challenges weighing proven versus innovative BI strategies (iverson2024thereliabilityand pages 1-2). Some scholars argue that BI remains inadequately enforced relative to IM in actual review (Frodeman & Parker, 2009; Burggren, 2009).

### Actionable Rules

1. Write IM and BI as parallel, substantive sections—each with goals, methods/activities, evidence of feasibility, and assessment plans (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 69-71, cronan2013strategiesforplanning pages 111-114).
2. State explicitly what is creative, original, or potentially transformative; do not assume reviewers will infer it (brennan2007nsfproposalpreparation pages 2-3).
3. Use the ARIS BI Toolkit (https://aris.wested.org/) for structured BI plan development, and involve BI professionals early (hotaling2024thearisbroader pages 1-2, hotaling2024thearisbroader pages 2-4).

## 3. EAGER (EArly-concept Grants for Exploratory Research) Mechanism

### Official Requirements

EAGER supports early-stage, exploratory, high-risk/high-payoff research—"radically different approaches, new expertise, or novel disciplinary perspectives"—that does not fit regular NSF programs (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 50-52). Key administrative requirements include: (a) prior contact with the cognizant NSF program officer to confirm suitability; (b) checking the "EAGER" box on the Cover Sheet; (c) a brief Project Description of five to eight pages that explicitly states why the work is appropriate for EAGER and why it does not fit existing programs; (d) a budget of up to $300,000 for up to two years; and (e) typically only internal merit review—external reviews are rare, and the PI must be notified if they occur (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 50-52). Renewals must be submitted as full proposals subject to external merit review (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 50-52).

### Evidence from the SGER Predecessor

EAGER replaced the Small Grants for Exploratory Research (SGER) program in 2007. An SRI International evaluation of SGER (4,972 awards totaling $284M over 16 years, average ~$54K) found the program "highly successful": more than 10% of awards produced transformative outcomes—well above a National Academies baseline of about 3% for transformative results (wagner2013evaluatingtransformativeresearch pages 9-10, wagner2013evaluatingtransformativeresearch pages 1-2). Successful SGER projects tended to be trans-disciplinary, often enabled mid-career researchers to shift fields, and frequently led to follow-on regular awards (73% of those who submitted follow-on proposals received them) (wagner2013evaluatingtransformativeresearch pages 6-7). However, the program was underutilized by program managers, who "spent far less than the allowable funds" (wagner2013evaluatingtransformativeresearch pages 1-2), suggesting risk aversion among decision-makers.

### Actionable Rules

1. Never submit an EAGER cold—contact the program officer first with a concise concept and confirm EAGER-appropriateness (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 50-52).
2. On the first page, justify mechanism fit: articulate the early-concept nature, the high-risk/high-payoff character, and why standard panels would not be appropriate (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 50-52).
3. Frame the project as a proof-of-concept or pilot likely to unlock larger future work; SGER/EAGER's greatest successes seeded downstream breakthroughs (wagner2013evaluatingtransformativeresearch pages 9-10, wagner2013evaluatingtransformativeresearch pages 6-7).

## 4. GWSW Curriculum and the Grant Application Writer's Workbook

### Overview

Grant Writers' Seminars & Workshops (GWSW), LLC, founded by Stephen Russell and David Morrison, has trained thousands of faculty through workshops and the widely used *Grant Application Writer's Workbook* series (separate editions for NIH and NSF). The Workbook is a structured, fill-in-the-blank guide that walks PIs through the logic of a competitive proposal. While no peer-reviewed publications from GWSW were retrieved in this search, the principles it teaches are well-documented in the broader grant-writing literature and are widely cited in institutional training materials.

### Core GWSW-Aligned Principles

**Hypothesis-driven framing.** Successful proposals ground the research plan in a clear central hypothesis or driving question, with specific aims that flow logically from the gap in knowledge. The proposal should move from problem → gap → central idea/hypothesis → 2–4 specific aims → approach → expected payoff (cronan2013strategiesforplanning pages 139-142, cronan2013strategiesforplanning pages 75-77). Vague aims framed as "study/examine/explore" are explicitly discouraged; instead, aims should be testable, with concrete deliverables and decision points (porter2017reprint2007why pages 4-6).

**The Specific Aims page.** Although NSF does not formally require a "Specific Aims" page as NIH does, many successful NSF proposals adopt an analogous opening page that establishes significance, states the overarching goal, lists 2–4 aims, and previews the approach and expected outcomes. This page functions as the "hook" that determines whether reviewers continue reading with enthusiasm (cronan2013strategiesforplanning pages 75-77, porter2017reprint2007why pages 6-9).

**Reviewer-centric writing.** Every paragraph should advance the funding case, not merely explain a topic. Writers should address five core reviewer questions: what will you do, why will you do it, how will you do it, why is it significant, and what is the value-added impact? (cronan2013strategiesforplanning pages 139-142). Proposals should avoid generalities, boilerplate, and slogans, which indicate underdeveloped conceptual ideas (cronan2013strategiesforplanning pages 139-142).

**Common pitfalls.** Cosmetic editing cannot substitute for deep conceptual development; writers must add specificity, rationale, objectives, approach, and expected impact (cronan2013strategiesforplanning pages 139-142). Failure to address impact or context is a leading cause of rejection (brennan2007nsfproposalpreparation pages 2-3).

### Actionable Rules

1. Draft a de facto "specific aims" opening page even for NSF proposals: problem, gap, central hypothesis/question, 2–4 aims, approach, and payoff.
2. Replace generic verbs ("explore," "investigate") with testable aims, concrete deliverables, and decision points (porter2017reprint2007why pages 4-6).
3. Write from the reviewer's perspective: significance first, then logic, then methods; every paragraph should advance the funding case (cronan2013strategiesforplanning pages 139-142, cronan2013strategiesforplanning pages 75-77).

## 5. Other Widely Cited NSF/Federal Grant-Writing Best-Practice Resources

### Robert Porter

Porter's influential essay "Why Academics Have a Hard Time Writing Good Grant Proposals" (2007; reprinted 2017) remains one of the most widely cited practitioner guides. Porter contrasts academic writing—cautious, impersonal, verbose, past-oriented, jargon-heavy—with effective grant writing, which must be project-centered, future-oriented, persuasive, personal, team-focused, concise, and accessible (porter2017reprint2007why pages 1-4, porter2007whyacademicshave pages 1-3). He argues that "success in grant writing is a matter of style and format as much as content" and calls a good proposal "an elegant sales pitch" (porter2017reprint2007why pages 4-6). Key remedies include: adopt a sales-pitch mindset, write actively and briefly, use internal "red team" reviews, and model accessible scientific exposition (e.g., *Scientific American*) (porter2017reprint2007why pages 6-9, porter2017reprint2007why pages 9-11). Porter's "More Paper out the Door" (2011) offers ten low-cost institutional strategies for stimulating proposal development, emphasizing upstream interventions such as home-grown workshops, program officer visits, mentor matchmaking, and coaching/editing (porter2011morepaperout pages 1-3).

### NSF Program Officer Perspectives

Cook (2020), writing from 14 years' experience managing reviews of over 1,000 proposals, emphasizes "exquisite grantsmanship—clear, concise, and very thoughtful writing" (cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 1-2). Key advice includes: assess program fit early and contact the program officer with a one-page concept summary; state objectives, importance, and approach early in the Project Summary; use section headers for organization; acknowledge risks with remediation plans; and avoid common mistakes such as long pedantic introductions, missing context, sloppy errors, and proposals that look like "fishing expeditions" (cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 3-4, cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 2-3). Program officers place greater weight on written reviewer comments than on numeric ratings (cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 5-6).

Brennan (2007), an ex-NSF program officer in the Division of Mathematical Sciences, stresses that proposals must explicitly address both merit review criteria, place the research in context, and explain why it matters beyond a mere statement of topic (brennan2007nsfproposalpreparation pages 2-3, brennan2007nsfproposalpreparation pages 1-2). Reviewers—especially panelists drawn from the general field—may lack subfield expertise, so proposals must give accessible context (brennan2007nsfproposalpreparation pages 2-3).

Higgins (2018) emphasizes that NSF success rates are modest (~20%), most proposals are not funded on first submission, and successful applicants often revise and resubmit using reviewer and PO feedback—"tenacity and effective use of feedback matter" (higgins2018whattoknow pages 1-4).

### Synthesis Literature

Wisdom, Riley, and Myers (2015) synthesized 53 published articles into 10 major recommendations: identify appropriate funders; use the funder's suggested structure and language; write persuasively, clearly, and concisely; keep the study design simple, logical, and feasible; perform an exhaustive literature review; choose a novel, high-impact project; seek colleague feedback; contact program officers; prepare reasonable budgets; and develop realistic timelines allowing for resubmission (wisdom2015recommendationsforwriting pages 2-3, wisdom2015recommendationsforwriting pages 3-4, wisdom2015recommendationsforwriting pages 1-2). Lemanski (2014) highlights Porter's core insight that grant proposals require a different rhetorical stance than journal articles: they must capture attention immediately and sustain a compelling argument, rather than carefully building a case before asserting cautious conclusions (lemanski2014proposalpitfallsplaguing pages 4-7, lemanski2014proposalpitfallsplaguing pages 7-9).

### Key Disagreement

A tension exists between Porter's emphasis on persuasive, even promotional language and NSF's own call to "communicate science in a plain manner" (markowitz2019whatwordsare pages 1-3). Empirical evidence (see Theme 6) suggests that complex, confident writing correlates with larger awards, running counter to plain-language norms.

### Actionable Rules

1. Write for impatient, mixed-expertise reviewers: short sentences, obvious headings, legible figures, early statement of objectives, and minimal jargon (porter2017reprint2007why pages 6-9, cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 1-2).
2. Contact the program officer early with a one-page concept summary and confirm program fit before heavy drafting (cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 3-4, jelinek2005organizationalscienceand pages 8-9).
3. Conduct internal red-team reviews with sufficient lead time; sloppy prose, delayed significance, and mismatch to the solicitation are avoidable rejection triggers (porter2017reprint2007why pages 6-9, porter2017reprint2007why pages 9-11, wisdom2015recommendationsforwriting pages 2-3).

## 6. Empirical Studies of What Predicts NSF Funding Success

### Proposal Language and Word Patterns

Markowitz (2019) analyzed 19,569 NSF Standard Grant abstracts (2010–2018; over 7.4 million words) and found that longer abstracts, fewer common words, more words per sentence, and greater verbal certainty were associated with larger awards. The relationship between word count and funding is curvilinear, with an optimum around 681 words; abstracts written with more narrative style and causal language also correlated with higher funding (markowitz2019whatwordsare pages 9-12, markowitz2019whatwordsare pages 12-14, markowitz2019whatwordsare pages 1-3). Effect sizes were small (Cohen's f² < 0.014), and the data included only funded proposals, limiting causal interpretation (markowitz2019whatwordsare pages 14-17).

Peng et al. (2024), in a *PNAS* study, analyzed tens of thousands of proposals from the NSF, NIH, and Novo Nordisk Foundation using a validated 139-word lexicon of science-specific promotional terms (e.g., "unique," "revolutionary," "fundamental"). They found that the percentage of promotional language robustly predicts funding probability: proposals with above-average promotional word density had up to doubled funding chances. In the combined NIH/NSF sample, a regression coefficient of β = 29.5 (p < 0.02) was reported, and proposals exceeding the base funding rate contained up to three times the median proportion of promotional words (peng2024promotionallanguageand pages 11-14, peng2024promotionallanguageand pages 6-9, peng2024promotionallanguageand pages 3-6). Computer experiments confirmed that removing promotional words reduced positive sentiment in over 88% of NSF proposals (peng2024promotionallanguageand pages 11-14). Promotional language also predicted downstream citation impact and grant innovativeness (peng2024promotionallanguageand pages 14-16, peng2024promotionallanguageand pages 9-11).

### PI Demographics: Race/Ethnicity

Chen et al. (2022), published in *eLife* with 178 citations, analyzed over one million NSF proposals (1996–2019) and documented pervasive racial disparities. White PIs were funded at consistently higher rates than most non-white PIs, with an average relative advantage of +8.5% that increased from +2.8% in 1999 to +14.3% in 2019 (chen2022systemicracialdisparities pages 1-2). Asian PIs showed a relative funding deficit of –21.2%, and Black/African American PIs showed –8.1% on average. Disparities were larger for research proposals and appeared across all NSF directorates, with systematic offsets in external review score distributions by PI race (chen2022systemicracialdisparities pages 2-4, chen2022systemicracialdisparities pages 6-8, chen2022systemicracialdisparities pages 4-6). Notably, NSF's panel-based decision process appeared to partially counter lower peer-review scores for Black/AA PIs, suggesting post-review decision stages influence outcomes (chen2022systemicracialdisparities pages 12-13).

### PI Demographics: Gender

Rissler et al. (2020) found that women submit proposals to NSF at lower rates than expected relative to their representation in the professoriate across nearly all fields except engineering, but when women do submit, their funding success is comparable to men's (rissler2020genderdifferencesin pages 1-2, rissler2020genderdifferencesin pages 4-5). The primary driver of lower submission rates is that women report spending less time on research and more on teaching, mentoring, and service (rissler2020genderdifferencesin pages 5-6). Ginther (2022) confirmed that in 2019, women had higher funding rates than men in every NSF directorate, though cumulative grant dollars still favored men in some fields (ginther2022genderraceethnicityand pages 8-13, ginther2022genderraceethnicityand pages 35-44).

### Social Capital and Network Effects

Feinberg and Price (2004) analyzed 1,420 NSF Economics Program proposals (1996–2000) and found that NBER (National Bureau of Economic Research) affiliation—a proxy for social capital—significantly increased the probability of funding, with NBER-related social capital accounting for at least 15% of grant success for associates, even controlling for publication quality and other ability measures (feinberg2004thefundingof pages 5-6, feinberg2004thefundingof pages 1-2, feinberg2004thefundingof pages 7-8, feinberg2004thefundingof pages 6-7). Publication quality in top journals (not quantity) was also a strong predictor (feinberg2004thefundingof pages 6-7).

### Submission Strategy and Funding Rates

Roebber and Schultz (2011) used an agent-based model calibrated to NSF conditions and found that when funding rates drop below ~10–15%, submitting many proposals becomes the most effective strategy for individual scientists to maintain funding (roebber2011peerreviewprogram pages 1-3). Program officers who use more reviewers and require consensus can improve outcomes for less-prolific submitters, and program officers overturn approximately 25% of reviewer recommendations (roebber2011peerreviewprogram pages 3-4, roebber2011peerreviewprogram pages 4-6).

### Key Disagreements

There is a fundamental tension between the finding that promotional, complex language correlates with funding success (Markowitz, 2019; Peng et al., 2024) and the normative advice from NSF and grant-writing experts to write plainly and accessibly. Markowitz himself notes this "inconsistency between the injunctive norms of the NSF and the descriptive norms of science writing" (markowitz2019whatwordsare pages 1-3). Furthermore, whether racial funding disparities reflect bias in review versus structural factors (e.g., topic choice, institutional resources, submission patterns) remains actively debated (chen2022systemicracialdisparities pages 12-13, ginther2022genderraceethnicityand pages 13-17).

### Actionable Rules

1. Use confident, specific, professionally persuasive language while avoiding empty hype; empirical work suggests certainty and promotional framing may help, but causality is not fully established and excessive promotional language carries risks (peng2024promotionallanguageand pages 11-14, peng2024promotionallanguageand pages 14-16).
2. Increase odds structurally: cultivate collaborations, seek mentoring and network exposure, serve on review panels, and resubmit strategically—prior community embeddedness and repeated engagement matter (feinberg2004thefundingof pages 1-2, roebber2011peerreviewprogram pages 1-3).
3. Do not overinterpret descriptive correlates as universal rules: effects vary by field and mechanism, and structural inequities mean strong grantsmanship is necessary but not always sufficient (chen2022systemicracialdisparities pages 1-2, rissler2020genderdifferencesin pages 1-2, markowitz2019whatwordsare pages 14-17).

---

## Conclusion

The literature converges on several overarching principles for competitive NSF proposals in 2026. First, strict compliance with the current PAPPG is a prerequisite, not a formality (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 58-60, guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 14-16). Second, both Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts must be treated as substantive, parallel components with goals, methods, and assessment plans (guide2009proposalawardpolicies pages 69-71, hotaling2024thearisbroader pages 1-2). Third, effective proposals are written for reviewers—not for the PI—using clear, concise, persuasive, and accessible prose that hooks readers immediately and sustains a compelling argument (porter2017reprint2007why pages 6-9, cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 1-2, wisdom2015recommendationsforwriting pages 2-3). Fourth, early and repeated engagement with program officers and the research community (through panel service, networking, and resubmission) is consistently associated with success (cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 3-4, feinberg2004thefundingof pages 1-2, higgins2018whattoknow pages 1-4). Finally, while empirical studies have identified linguistic and demographic correlates of funding, these should inform but not mechanistically dictate writing strategy; the strongest predictor of success remains the quality and clarity of the scientific idea itself, presented in a format optimized for the review process (cook2020thoughtsonwriting pages 2-3, markowitz2019whatwordsare pages 14-17, peng2024promotionallanguageand pages 14-16).
