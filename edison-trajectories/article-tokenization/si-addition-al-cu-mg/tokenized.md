# Effects of Si addition on the microstructure evolution of Al–Cu–Mg alloys in the α + S + T phase field

## Title
Effects of Si addition on the microstructure evolution of Al–Cu–Mg alloys in the α + S + T phase field

## Authors
Zhiguo Chen, Jishuai Zhang, Jun Shu, Gang Sha, Junhai Xia, Shiyong Wang and S.P. Ringer

School of Materials Science and Engineering, Central South University, Lushan South Road, Changsha, Hunan 410083, P.R. China  
Department of Mechanical and Electrical Engineering, Hunan University of Humanities, Science and Technology, Dixing Road, Loudi, Hunan 417000, P.R. China  
Australian Centre for Microscopy and Microanalysis, The University of Sydney, Sydney, NSW 2006, Australia

(Received 28 December 2012; accepted 11 August 2013)

## Abstract
The precipitation behaviour and age-hardening response of Al–1.5Cu–4.0Mg (wt.%) alloys microalloyed with Si have been investigated by means of hardness measurement, TEM and HRTEM. Compared to the ternary alloy, the quaternary alloys exhibit a higher hardness. It is found that the underaged microstructure in the Al–1.5Cu–4.0Mg alloy contains some fine precipitates, which are identified as the T phase by FFT spectra. The peak-aged microstructures of the ternary alloy is dominated by the T phase, while the peak-aged microstructures of the Si-containing alloys are dominated by the S phase. The volume fraction of the S phase is found to increase as more Si is added.

Keywords: Al–Cu–Mg alloys; precipitation; TEM; T phase; Si

## Introduction
Al–Cu–Mg alloys are capable of developing considerable strength by precipitation, and the nature of the precipitation phase and microstructure evolution is determined by the Cu/Mg ratio in the alloy. The ternary Al–Cu–Mg alloys having high, medium, low Cu/Mg ratio lie in the (α + h + S), (α + S) and (α + S + T) phase fields, respectively [1]. So, care must be taken in the use of microalloying additions since their effect has been shown to be sensitive to the bulk composition. As for the trace addition of Si, the investigations can be summarized based on the Cu/Mg ratio as follows.

### 1.1. α + h + S phase field (typical alloy: Al–4.0Cu–0.3Mg)
The accelerated precipitation of Ω–Al2Cu (thin platelet on {111}α planes) by small additions of Ag has generated great interest [2–4]. However, Gable et al. [5] revealed that the presence of trace Si in Al–4Cu–0.4Mg (wt.%) alloys suppressed the formation of the Ω phase and stimulated σ (Al5Cu6Mg2, Pm3, a = 0.831 nm) and h′ phase precipitation. On the other hand, Si additions to Al–Cu–Mg alloys tend to induce the Q (Al5Cu2Mg8Si6) phase [6,7]. Suzuki et al. [8] reported that the Q phase, the h′ phase and an unknown X phase were formed in Al–2.0Cu–0.9Mg–0.5Si (wt.%) alloy. Eskin [9] found that the precipitation sequence of Al–Cu–Mg–Si alloys with Mg/Si ratio < 1.0 did not follow the equilibrium phase diagram and involved Mg2Si, Al2Cu, Q and Si phases.

### 1.2. α + S phase field (typical alloy: Al–2.5Cu–1.5Mg)
The addition of Si to Al–Cu–Mg alloys in the α + S phase field was found to delay the room temperature age-hardening response and enhance the elevated temperature response [10]. Wilson et al. [11] found that S′ precipitation in the Al–2.5Cu–1.2Mg–0.25Si (wt.%) alloy was smaller and more finely distributed than that in the Si-free alloy. Moreover, Wang et al. [12] proposed two types of S phase, designated as Type I and Type II, in AA2024 and AA2324 alloys, and indicated that trace Si addition enhanced the formation of the Type I S phase. Coprecipitation of Ω and σ phases was reported in the Si-containing Al–Cu–Mg–Mn–Ag alloy by Mukhopadhyay [13]. In the study of Al–2.5Cu–1.5Mg–0.25Si–0.1Ag (wt.%) alloy by Raviprasad et al. [14], the Ω and X phases coprecipitated together with GPB zones in the peak hardness microstructure, and only small volumes of the σ phase together with the dominating S phase precipitated in the overaged microstructure.

### 1.3. α + S + T phase field (typical alloy: Al–1.5Cu–4.0Mg)
Up to now, most microalloying studies have been focused on the (α + h + S) and (α + S) phase field, with not much work being carried out on Al–Cu–Mg alloys with compositions in the (α + S + T) phase field [1,15–19]. It has been shown that the addition of Ag to low Cu/Mg ratio alloys stimulated a new cubic phase (m3m, a = 1.999 nm) designated Z, previously suggested as T phase [1], and suppressed S phase precipitation [19,20]. More recently, investigation of Ge addition to Al–4Mg–1.5Cu has revealed that Z′ coexists with GPB zones and the S phase in the peak-aged alloy [21].

Considering Si belongs to the same IVA group in the Periodic Table as Ge, there is a lack of detailed information regarding the precipitation processes of alloys microalloyed with Si in this phase field. In this paper, the precipitation processes in Al–1.5Cu–4.0Mg (wt.%) alloy microalloyed with Si has been investigated. Of particular interest is whether the T phase (Al6CuMg4) could form in such an alloy since the T phase was not observed in some previous studies [1,15,16], and also to examine the effects of Si addition on the microstructure evolution.

## Methods/Experimental
The alloys were prepared by melting and chill casting into a cast iron mould. Their compositions are given in Table 1. The ingots were homogenized, scalped, then hot rolled and finally formed into 2 mm sheets by cold rolling. The samples were solution-treated in a salt bath at 500°C for 1 h followed by immediate quenching in cold water. The samples were subsequently aged at 200°C in order to examine the sequence of the nucleation of precipitates of interest during ageing. The age-hardening response of alloys was monitored by Vickers hardness measurements using a 1.96 N load applied for 15 s. TEM and HRTEM observations were performed on TECNAI G220 and JEOL JEM-3010 transmission electron microscopes, respectively, with operating voltages of 200 kV. The specimens for TEM observation were prepared by the standard twin-jet electropolishing method using a solution of methanol and nitric acid (3:1 in volume) at −25°C.

## Results
The age-hardening curves of the alloys at 200°C are plotted in Figure 1. It can be seen that the age-hardening response of the alloys is enhanced by the addition of Si. Compared with the Si-free alloy, the time taken to peak hardness is reduced for the Si-containing alloys, and the effects become slightly more obvious with higher silicon content. Furthermore, the hardness of Si-containing alloys decreases more slowly than the Si-free alloy after peak ageing, indicating a slower rate of softening with Si addition.

Figures 2 and 3 are transmission electron micrographs recorded near the h001iα zone axis and corresponding SAED patterns from underaged to overaged stages in the studied alloys. During the underaged stage (as shown in Figure 2), the microstructure of the Si-free alloy contains some dislocations and dislocation loops which result from the condensation and collapse of quenched-in vacancies. However, fewer dislocation loops have been found in these Si-containing alloys; especially in the 0.5% Si-containing alloy in which few dislocations can be found. What is more, some fine rod-shape precipitates in the Si-containing alloys can be identified as GPBs, since the SAED patterns (Figure 2b and c) exhibit faint diffuse streaking through {100}α along the h100iα directions, with extra intensity near the {100}α positions, which is consistent with GPB zone formation.

The HRTEM micrograph and corresponding FFT spectra of the fine precipitates in the Al–1.5Cu–4.0Mg alloy aged for 1 h are shown in Figure 4a and b. The obtained FFT spectra of the fine precipitates match the diffraction pattern of the T phase, which gives rise to diffraction spots similar to the Ω phase (whose diffraction spots occur at 1/3h220iAl and 2/3h220iAl when viewed along the h001iAl orientation). The HRTEM micrograph and the corresponding FFT spectra of the T phase (cubic-shaped phase in overaged condition of Al–1.5Cu–4.0Mg alloy) are also given in Figure 4c and d. The two FFT spectra suggest the fine precipitates and the T phase are the same phase despite their different sizes. We can conclude that the T phase has been formed within 1 h and then grows up as the ageing time is prolonged.

The peak hardness microstructure (Figure 3a) of the ternary alloy is dominated by a uniform distribution of cubic-shaped T phase, which was not observed by some previous studies in low Cu/Mg ratio Al–Cu–Mg alloys [15–19]. Very few S phases can also be found nucleated at dislocations. But the volume fraction of the S phase was too small to be resolved clearly in the corresponding SAED pattern. Figure 3b and c show the peak-aged microstructures of the Si-containing alloys. Figure 3b reveals a microstructure characterized by the lath-shaped S phase together with a small amount of cuboidal T phase. It can be seen that most of the S phase precipitated in the matrix uniformly, while heterogeneous precipitation of the S phase could also be found on the dislocation. But the interesting thing is that the microstructure for 0.25% Si-containing alloy is dominated by the S phase together with a small amount of the T phase, whereas a dense population of the S phase without obvious evidence of the T phase was shown in the 0.5% Si-bearing alloy. The SAED patterns also prove this point: the diffraction spot of the T phase is absent in Figure 3c and the extra characteristic crosses around the forbidden {110}α reflections are consistent with the S phase. Moreover, it can also be noted in Figure 3b and c that the S precipitate in the 0.5% Si-bearing alloy was finer than that in 0.25% Si-bearing alloy.

The overaged microstructures show a coarsening of the precipitates in the ternary alloys and the Si-containing alloys, which consequently causes a reduction in the hardness of the materials. The sizes of the T phase regions were much larger, and the density obviously decreased (Figure 3d). Prolonged ageing also led to progressive growth of the S precipitates. Although the S phase was slightly coarse, the distribution density remained relatively high in the Si-containing alloys. It is likely that the addition of silicon postpones the coarsening of the S phase [22], leading to a relatively slow rate of softening.

## Discussion
On the basis of the results given above, trace silicon additions have important thermodynamic and kinetic effects on the overall microstructural evolution in Al–1.5Cu–4.0Mg alloy. The reduced peak ageing time seems to result from enhanced homogeneous S precipitates with Si addition. TEM micrographs in the peak-aged and overaged conditions for each alloy show the density of the S phase increases with Si content, indicating that the Si boosts S phase precipitation in the alloys. The precipitation sequence in Al–1.5Cu–4.0Mg alloy at 200°C might be suggested as:

supersaturated solid solution (SSSS) → T + S.

However, the dominant precipitation sequence could be:

SSSS → GPB → S + T

when the Si content is 0.25 wt.%, and the T phase is almost suppressed when the Si content reaches 0.5 wt.%. It may be inferred that the T phase is sensitive to the Si content and one reason why the T phase was not found in some previous research could be attributed to the presence of Si impurity in the alloys.

The enhanced fine S phase could be associated with several things. First, the added Si might have captured the vacancy due to the strong bonding energy between them [24], thus inhibiting the formation of dislocation loops. Given the S phase was shown to occur preferentially on the dislocation [10,16], the reduced number of dislocations provides for less heterogeneous nucleation in the S phases, and so they tend to nucleate on the homogeneously distributed GPB zones. Hirosawa et al. [25] indicated that Si addition could enhance GPB zone formation by increasing the number of Mg/Cu/vacancy complexes. Wilson et al. [13] proposed that the addition of Si could modify the interfacial precipitate-matrix energy and lower the growth rate of the precipitate when silicon segregates to the interface.

As for the suppression of the T phase, it is reasonable to consider that the enhanced nucleation of the S phase would give rise to a large chemical potential gradient difference in Mg between the T and S phases. Just as in the study of the Al–2.5Cu–1.5Mg (wt.%) alloys microalloyed with Si by Hutchinson et al. [23], in our research, Mg2Si particles have been detected by X-ray diffraction chemical phase analysis in the Si-containing alloys. The T phase is likely to be suppressed on account of fewer available Mg atoms within the matrix, causing S phase precipitates to occur preferentially at the expense of the T phase with Si addition. It is suggested that the driving force of S phase precipitation is enhanced by the presence of Si and this pushes the alloy matrix composition progressively from the α + S + T to the α + S phase field. Since Al–Cu–Mg alloy with low Cu/Mg is a potential material for car bodies owing to its strength and cold formability [18,21], the presence of silicon should be considered when designing this system alloys.

## Conclusions
1. The age-hardening response of Al–1.5Cu–4.0Mg (wt.%) alloy can be enhanced by the addition of Si, and the time taken to the ageing peak is shortened.
2. Trace addition of Si results in fewer dislocations or dislocation loops during the underaged stage. The addition of Si stimulates the precipitation of the S phase, and restrains the formation of the T phase. The precipitation of T-phase is almost completely suppressed when the Si content reaches 0.5 wt.%.
3. The presence of 0.5 wt.% silicon causes S precipitates to be finer than that in the 0.25 wt.% silicon-containing alloy.
