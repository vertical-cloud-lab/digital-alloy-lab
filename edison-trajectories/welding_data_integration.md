# Applying Welding Metallurgy Data and Principles to Additive Manufacturing Alloy Discovery and Design

## Overview

Welding metallurgy and additive manufacturing (AM) share fundamental physics: both involve localized melting, solidification, and heat-affected zone transformations. This convergence has led to a growing body of work (2020–2026) exploring how decades of welding science—empirical diagrams, compositional criteria, computational models, filler metal databases, and qualification frameworks—can accelerate AM alloy discovery and process optimization. However, the transfer is not straightforward: AM's extreme cooling rates (10³–10⁷ K/s), layer-by-layer thermal cycling, and small melt pool volumes create conditions that deviate significantly from conventional welding (sotiriou2023modelingmicrostructuralevolution pages 29-32, liu2024alloydesignfor pages 10-11). The following sections address each sub-question in detail.

For **Topic 3B**, the welding–AM transfer is especially relevant because critical-mineral-lean alloy systems (Al-Zr, Al-Mn-Cr-Zr) have limited AM processability data, making welding-derived screening criteria and filler metal databases valuable as informative priors that can bootstrap Bayesian optimization before costly LPBF experiments.

## 1. ASTM Welding Compatibility Charts and Dissimilar Metal Welding Databases

Comprehensive dissimilar metal welding (DMW) databases catalog alloy-pair compatibility according to three primary failure categories: brittle intermetallic compound formation and solubility limitations, thermal property mismatches (melting temperature, coefficient of thermal expansion, thermal conductivity), and alloy-specific effects such as carbon migration, sensitization, and species redistribution (reichardt2021advancesinadditive pages 7-9, jones2023investigatingmultimaterialadditive pages 20-25). Reichardt et al. (2021) compiled compatibility tables for a wide range of stainless steel, nickel, titanium, and copper alloy combinations, providing a foundational reference for functionally graded material (FGM) design via DED (reichardt2021advancesinadditive pages 6-7). These data are structured around alloy-pair combinations with qualitative assessments of joint viability, known interlayer solutions, and recommended joining methods.

For AM multi-material and compositionally graded design, these databases serve as essential prior knowledge. DED enables controlled, gradual compositional transitions that can bypass or "skip over" deleterious intermediate compositions where brittle phases form (jones2023investigatingmultimaterialadditive pages 88-94). Jones (2023) demonstrated this approach by using melt-pool simulation to design laser power/velocity parameter arrays targeting specific dilution levels (~50% added-material to re-melted-substrate), thereby avoiding intermetallic-forming composition windows in the Fe-Cr-Ni system (jones2023investigatingmultimaterialadditive pages 88-94). Welding compatibility charts also inform the selection of interlayer materials (e.g., nickel barriers to inhibit intermetallic formation in Ti/steel or Al/Mg systems) and the thermodynamic optimization of compositional paths (jones2023investigatingmultimaterialadditive pages 20-25).

A key limitation is that these databases assume conventional fusion welding thermal histories. AM's rapid solidification and repeated reheating produce different phase equilibria and microstructures, requiring compositional path optimization to be coupled with CALPHAD thermodynamics and melt-pool mixing models rather than used in isolation (jones2023investigatingmultimaterialadditive pages 20-25).

## 2. Weldability Concepts and Their Translation to LPBF/DED Processability Prediction

### Carbon Equivalent

The carbon equivalent (CE = C + (Mn+Si)/6 + (Ni+Cu)/15 + (Cr+Mo+V)/5) has long been the primary weldability screening tool for steels, with values above 0.35 wt.% indicating poor weldability and susceptibility to hydrogen-induced cracking (wang2020uncertaintyquantificationand pages 13-16). Wang and Xiong (2020) incorporated CE directly into a CALPHAD-based ICME framework for HSLA-115 steel AM composition optimization, using it alongside Graville diagram criteria and freezing-range calculations to screen ~450,000 compositions and identify an optimized nominal that increased successful AM build probability by 44.7% (wang2020uncertaintyquantificationand pages 1-4, wang2020uncertaintyquantificationand pages 4-7, wang2020uncertaintyquantificationand pages 13-16).

However, CE does not reliably predict LPBF printability. Liu et al. (2024) showed that high CE does not necessarily correspond to poor printability—some tool steels with low CE but high carbon content are crack-prone, while low CE values may simply reflect low Cr/Ni rather than genuine processability (liu2024alloydesignfor pages 10-11, liu2024alloydesignfor pages 6-8, liu2024alloydesignfor pages 8-10). As a general guideline, steels with carbon content below ~0.22 wt.% can be processed by LPBF without powder bed preheating, but this is an empirical rule rather than a predictive framework (liu2024alloydesignfor pages 8-10).

### Schaeffler/DeLong/WRC Diagrams

These constitution diagrams map Cr_eq and Ni_eq to predicted phase constitution (austenite, ferrite, martensite). Assi et al. (2024) used the Hull extension of the Schaeffler diagram as a front-end filter to define a feasible austenitic search space for LPBF alloy design, constraining compositions to maintain <5% ferrite and maximizing distance from martensite boundaries. This was then coupled with Thermo-Calc Scheil simulations to predict solidification sequences and hot-cracking susceptibility (assi2024designandassessment pages 5-7, assi2024designandassessment pages 3-5).

For DED-Arc graded structures, Rimpl et al. (2025) demonstrated that the Schaeffler diagram effectively predicted phase boundaries and hardness transitions in 42CrMo4-to-Alloy 625 gradients produced by tandem DED-Arc, identifying three distinct hardness regions that aligned closely with Schaeffler-predicted phase boundaries, with peak hardness of 580 HV1 at ~10% Alloy 625 (rimpl2025applicationofthe pages 1-2). Leicher et al. (2020) similarly used Schaeffler-based classification of filler wires for multi-material WAAM design (leicher2020multimaterialdesignin pages 1-3).

The critical limitation is that Schaeffler/DeLong diagrams assume near-equilibrium solidification. LPBF's rapid cooling suppresses expected phase transformations, produces elemental partitioning and local phase transitions not captured by these diagrams, and can lead to metastable microstructures that deviate from predictions (liu2024alloydesignfor pages 10-11, assi2024designandassessment pages 3-5). These diagrams work best as compositional pre-screening tools when augmented with CALPHAD/Scheil simulations, not as standalone predictors.

### Hot Cracking Susceptibility (Suutala/Lippold Criteria)

Suutala diagrams (relating Cr_eq/Ni_eq ratio to cracking susceptibility) and Lippold solidification-mode diagrams remain valuable for ranking composition effects. Primary ferrite solidification (FA mode, achieved when Cr_eq/Ni_eq approaches ~1.48) reduces cracking susceptibility because the δ-ferrite/austenite boundary efficiently accommodates impurities like S and P (assi2024designandassessment pages 5-7, assi2024designandassessment pages 7-8). However, these criteria were developed for conventional arc welding with slow-moving heat sources, and AM's much higher cooling rates shift solidification modes, delay solid-state transformations (e.g., δ→γ), and alter microsegregation patterns (xi2021discoveringthepotential pages 21-27, sotiriou2023modelingmicrostructuralevolution pages 29-32). Xi (2021) found that conventional weld tests could produce cracking that did not manifest in LPBF builds of the same alloy, highlighting the disconnect between welding-based predictions and AM outcomes (xi2021discoveringthepotential pages 69-77).

For nickel-based superalloys, traditional weldability charts based on (Ti+Al)% fail to predict LPBF printability because rapid cooling suppresses γ'/γ" precipitation and near-atomic-scale composition variation changes cracking behavior at the local scale (liu2024alloydesignfor pages 11-12).

## 3. Welding Metallurgy Principles Adapted for AM Process-Structure-Property Understanding

### Solidification Modes and Constitutional Supercooling

The foundational relationship between thermal gradient (G), solidification velocity (R), and grain morphology transfers directly from welding to AM. Pollock et al. (2020) emphasized that melt pool geometry, interface velocities, and substrate crystallography can predict dendrite microstructure and columnar-to-equiaxed transitions (CET), using geometrical models developed by Rappaz for welding (pollock2020designandtailoring pages 4-5, pollock2020designandtailoring pages 5-6). Constitutional supercooling—local liquidus depression due to solute segregation versus the imposed temperature gradient—governs interface stability and transitions between planar, cellular, columnar dendritic, and equiaxed dendritic morphologies (sutton2021developmentofa pages 50-55).

Brooke et al. (2025) evaluated three compositional parameters for predicting CET in AM alloys (solidification range ΔTs, growth restriction factor Q, and constitutional supercooling parameter P), finding that P most reliably predicts the selection of alloying elements for AM alloys across Ti-Fe, Ti-Cu, Ti-Cu-Fe, and Ti-Mo systems (pollock2020designandtailoring pages 4-5). The key AM adaptation is that melt pools are small, transient, and involve multilayer thermal memory, requiring process-specific coupling of thermal models with solidification theory. Madigan (2025) demonstrated that temporal and spatial control of adjacent melt events in electron beam PBF—using minimum separation times informed by melt-pool size and solidification/cooling times—can promote equiaxed nucleation beyond simple energy-density control (madigan2025columnartoequiaxed pages 121-125).

### Dilution and Epitaxial Growth

Dilution—the mixing of feedstock and substrate material—transfers directly from welding/cladding to DED. However, in AM it is spatially and temporally variable, affected by powder-feed delays, scan path, and imperfect melt-pool mixing (jones2023investigatingmultimaterialadditive pages 170-172, reichardt2021advancesinadditive pages 12-14). Epitaxial growth from substrate grains is expected when deposited and substrate chemistries are similar; the local temperature gradient and heat-source movement steer dendrite growth directions (sotiriou2023modelingmicrostructuralevolution pages 24-27). Cooling-rate scaling laws (DAS ∝ ε^n) link process cooling rates to primary/secondary arm spacings, informing microstructure morphologies (sotiriou2023modelingmicrostructuralevolution pages 24-27).

### Heat-Affected Zone Transformations

Cracking and stress models from welding must be modified for AM's cyclic, layer-by-layer thermal stresses. AM involves so-called "intrinsic heat treatment" where previously deposited layers experience repeated reheating, which can trigger precipitation (e.g., Al₃Sc in DED aluminum alloys), dissolution, or phase transformations not seen in single-pass welding (pollock2020designandtailoring pages 5-6). Isothermal JMA models and Christian's additivity rule from welding are applied to anisothermal AM thermal histories for microstructure prediction (irwin2021virtualpartqualification pages 14-18).

## 4. Welding Filler Metal Databases and AWS Classifications as Composition Search Spaces

Current AM feedstocks draw on decades of welding wire and powder metallurgy practice. Pollock et al. (2020) noted that standards exist for conventional Fe-, Ti-, Al-, Ni-, and Co-based feedstocks, but these standards are process-dependent—for example, welding wires designed for inert Ar shielding versus Ar+CO₂ require different deoxidizer content (pollock2020designandtailoring pages 1-2). WAAM literature heavily uses AWS-classified wires such as ER70S-6 and ER308L as starting feedstocks (mattera2025advancesinmachine pages 26-27, alsufyani2025evaluationthevariability pages 4-6, rodriguezgonzalez2023wirearcadditive pages 18-19).

AWS filler metal classifications represent an attractive but underexploited composition search space for AM alloy optimization. These databases provide commercially available, manufacturable chemistries with known arc/thermal behavior, organized by alloy system and mechanical property ranges. Wang and Xiong (2020) demonstrated that CALPHAD-based ICME frameworks can screen hundreds of thousands of compositions around a nominal for weldability, strength, and impact toughness simultaneously (wang2020uncertaintyquantificationand pages 1-4, wang2020uncertaintyquantificationand pages 13-16). Assi et al. (2024) used a multi-objective genetic algorithm coupled with CALPHAD/Scheil models, Schaeffler-type constraints, and Gaussian process regression for surface tension to optimize compositions for LPBF (assi2024designandassessment pages 5-7, assi2024designandassessment pages 7-8).

The key opportunity for Bayesian optimization is to use AWS filler metal composition ranges as informative priors or initial population seeds, constrained by welding-derived metrics (CE, freezing range, Cr_eq/Ni_eq ratio, Graville zone) as cheap-to-evaluate screening functions. The hot-cracking susceptibility criterion (freezing range < 13 K for good weldability), Graville diagram zone classification, and CE can serve as scalar constraints or penalty terms within the acquisition function (wang2020uncertaintyquantificationand pages 13-16). This approach has not yet been demonstrated as a fully closed-loop autonomous alloy discovery pipeline in the reviewed literature, representing a significant knowledge gap.

**Topic 3B connection:** For FLAIME's critical-mineral-lean alloy targets (Al-Zr, Al-Mn-Cr-Zr), the analogous approach uses Thermo-Calc CALPHAD screening (now validated on BYU HPC) and Scheil-based freezing range calculations (via Thermo-Calc AM Module simulations, provided through collaboration letter) as the cheap screening nodes in the pKGFN pipeline, with welding-derived criteria providing additional constraints for printability assessment. The AlSi10Mg baseline data (tensile specimens from U of Utah and 3D Powder Tech, plus SEM/EDS characterization) calibrates this pipeline before extending to novel compositions.

## 5. Computational Welding Mechanics Transferred to AM Process Simulation

### Rosenthal Solutions

The Rosenthal analytical solution for a moving point heat source remains the foundational baseline for rapid thermal prediction in AM. Keeley et al. (2025) extended the Rosenthal equation for AM by incorporating temperature-dependent thermophysical properties, producing a temperature-dependent Rosenthal model that yielded more accurate cooling rates than the original equation. The analytical model accurately predicted melt pool width but underestimated melt pool length at high velocities (keeley2025adevelopmentof pages 2-4). Extended Rosenthal formulations have been used to compute "thermal dose" metrics, predict lack-of-fusion boundaries, and provide fast scanning of process-parameter windows (keeley2025adevelopmentof pages 18-18). However, raw Rosenthal assumptions (point source, semi-infinite body, constant properties) over-simplify AM boundary conditions, and the model is best used for screening and control rather than full-fidelity simulation (irwin2021virtualpartqualification pages 18-22).

### Goldak Heat Source Model

Goldak's 3D double-ellipsoidal heat source model is the most widely adopted from welding to AM simulation. Kiran et al. (2022) compared Goldak's model with a simplified concentrated heat source for DED, finding good agreement for both models against experimental melt pool dimensions and residual stress data, but with the concentrated model requiring less than half the computational time (ilani2024modelingmeltpool pages 1-3). Ilani and Banad (2024) developed an analytical solution based on Goldak's semi-ellipsoidal model implemented in Python, validated against bead-on-plate experiments for laser-based AM (ilani2024modelingmeltpool pages 1-3). The Goldak model has been applied extensively to WAAM, LPBF, and DED for thermo-mechanical FE simulation of residual stress and distortion (irwin2021virtualpartqualification pages 115-118, bastola2023areviewof pages 16-18, bastola2023areviewof pages 28-29).

Key AM-specific adaptations include element birth/death techniques for layer deposition, adaptive mesh refinement, parallel time integrators, and hybrid inactive element methods to manage the computational cost of multitrack multilayer simulations (irwin2021virtualpartqualification pages 14-18). Commercial tools such as the ABAQUS AM module have incorporated Goldak-type heat sources for direct AM simulation (bastola2023areviewof pages 16-18).

### Residual Stress and Distortion

Residual stress prediction in AM directly inherits from computational welding mechanics, using coupled thermal and mechanical FE analyses with moving heat sources (bastola2023areviewof pages 16-18, bastola2023areviewof pages 28-29). The approach at both micro-scale (melt-pool and local temperature) and macro-scale (stress and distortion) has been validated across DED Ti-6Al-4V, 316L stainless steel, and various AM geometries (irwin2021virtualpartqualification pages 14-18, irwin2021virtualpartqualification pages 115-118). Integration of neural networks with FE models has been demonstrated for rapid residual stress prediction from process parameters (bastola2023areviewof pages 28-29).

## 6. Welding Procedure Qualification Data and AM Qualification Frameworks

### Existing AM Qualification Standards

AM qualification has not yet adopted a direct WPS/PQR analog. Instead, a constellation of standards addresses different aspects: ISO/ASTM 52900 provides terminology (jadhav2025recentprogressand pages 49-51); ASTM F3122 guides evaluation of mechanical properties of AM materials; ASTM F3301 addresses thermal post-processing for PBF. Welding qualification under ASME Section IX and AWS D1.1 remains relevant for WAAM processes, where joints must meet traditional weld qualification criteria (alsufyani2025evaluationthevariability pages 4-6).

Pollock et al. (2020) emphasized that AM's inherently digital nature makes it well-suited to "digital thread" qualification approaches incorporating uncertainty quantification, and that protocols for qualifying new additive processing paths are only now being developed (pollock2020designandtailoring pages 15-16). Irwin (2021) described process-mapping and process-structure-property mapping approaches—"defect structure process maps" and "process mapping for qualification across multiple direct metal AM processes"—as the AM equivalent of WPS/PQR, using process windows and defect/process maps to qualify builds (irwin2021virtualpartqualification pages 121-125).

### Welding Data as Training Data for AM Models

Mattera et al. (2025) proposed extending WAAM optimization frameworks to create "synergic lines"—data-driven parameter maps tailored to alloys, transfer modes, and geometries—modernizing classical welding practice for AM (mattera2025advancesinmachine pages 26-27). Welding qualification data (process parameters, mechanical test results, defect records) from WPS/PQR databases represent a potentially rich source of training data for AM process-property models, but systematic reuse has not been demonstrated. The primary barrier is that welding data are joint-centric (focused on specific weld configurations) while AM data must capture layerwise process-structure-property relationships.

### Open Data Initiatives

The WelDX (Welding Data Exchange Format), developed by BAM Berlin, is an open-source, Python-ecosystem-based file format specifically tailored for arc and laser-beam welding data, with FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) principles and published reference datasets (fabry2021recommendationsforan pages 6-7, fabry2021recommendationsforan pages 1-2, fabry2021recommendationsforan pages 7-8). Fabry et al. (2021) recommended community-agreed data schemas, hierarchical file formats, open APIs, and modern collaboration platforms (GitHub) to lower barriers for sharing welding research data (fabry2021recommendationsforan pages 4-5). This infrastructure could directly accelerate AM alloy discovery if extended to encompass AM-specific process parameters and layerwise build data.

### Key Knowledge Gaps

Based on the reviewed literature, the following critical gaps remain for applying welding data to autonomous AM alloy discovery:

**1. Weldability-to-printability mapping is unreliable.** Carbon equivalent, Schaeffler diagrams, and Suutala/Lippold criteria were developed for arc welding thermal cycles and do not directly predict LPBF behavior due to AM's extreme cooling rates, cyclic reheating, and non-equilibrium solidification (liu2024alloydesignfor pages 10-11, xi2021discoveringthepotential pages 69-77, xi2021discoveringthepotential pages 21-27). A systematic, quantitative recalibration of these criteria for AM-specific thermal histories is needed.

**2. Lack of standardized, open AM composition-process-property databases.** While WelDX provides a promising open data format for welding, there is no equivalent community-agreed format for AM experimental data. Publicly available AM datasets remain scarce, limiting ML-driven alloy discovery (fabry2021recommendationsforan pages 2-4, fabry2021recommendationsforan pages 1-2).

**3. No demonstrated closed-loop pipeline from welding priors to AM alloy optimization.** Although individual components exist—CALPHAD screening (wang2020uncertaintyquantificationand pages 1-4), welding-derived constraints (wang2020uncertaintyquantificationand pages 13-16), Bayesian optimization frameworks (assi2024designandassessment pages 7-8)—no end-to-end autonomous system has been demonstrated that uses welding filler metal databases as composition search spaces with welding-derived processability constraints in a closed-loop AM alloy discovery workflow. *FLAIME's pKGFN pipeline (implemented, BoTorch 0.12+) is designed to close this gap by integrating CALPHAD/Scheil screening nodes with Bayesian acquisition over the physical experiment DAG.*

**4. Incomplete coupling of melt-pool fluid dynamics with CALPHAD/phase-field models.** Current alloy design frameworks use Scheil simulations without accounting for melt-pool convection, Marangoni flow, and vapor recoil effects that influence local composition and microsegregation in AM (assi2024designandassessment pages 18-19).

**5. Multi-material and graded-composition AM lacks validated process-structure-property databases.** Welding compatibility charts provide starting points, but AM-specific data on graded transitions under rapid solidification are limited, and existing welding data cannot capture the complexity of layerwise composition evolution (jones2023investigatingmultimaterialadditive pages 170-172, jones2023investigatingmultimaterialadditive pages 20-25).

**6. Qualification frameworks need formalization.** AM qualification remains fragmented compared to the mature WPS/PQR system in welding. Digital-thread approaches and process-map-based qualification are promising but not yet standardized (pollock2020designandtailoring pages 15-16).

**7. Transfer of computational welding tools needs speed improvements.** Goldak-based FE models are computationally expensive for multilayer AM builds; reduced-order models, physics-informed neural networks, and analytical extensions (e.g., temperature-dependent Rosenthal) are needed for integration into closed-loop optimization (irwin2021virtualpartqualification pages 14-18, keeley2025adevelopmentof pages 2-4).

## Summary and Topic 3B Relevance

Welding metallurgy provides a rich foundation for AM alloy discovery, but the transfer requires careful adaptation. Constitution diagrams (Schaeffler/DeLong) and hot-cracking criteria (Suutala/Lippold) are useful as front-end composition filters when coupled with CALPHAD/Scheil simulations, but they cannot serve as standalone LPBF printability predictors. Computational welding tools (Rosenthal, Goldak) have been successfully adapted for AM thermal and residual stress modeling but need acceleration for closed-loop use. Welding filler metal databases represent an untapped composition search space for Bayesian optimization, and the WelDX open data format could serve as a backbone for cross-domain data sharing. The greatest opportunity lies in building integrated pipelines that combine welding-derived priors with CALPHAD, machine learning, and autonomous experimentation to accelerate AM alloy discovery—a capability that remains largely unrealized.

**Topic 3B alignment:** For critical-mineral-lean alloy discovery (Al-Zr, Al-Mn-Cr-Zr targeting elimination of Mg/Sc/REE dependence), welding data integration provides three concrete acceleration pathways: (1) Schaeffler/Suutala-type screening criteria serve as cheap BOFN nodes that filter Al alloy compositions before costly LPBF experiments; (2) Thermo-Calc AM Module simulations (available via collaboration letter) validate Scheil-based solidification predictions against welding-calibrated baselines; (3) the pKGFN pipeline implementation can ingest welding-derived priors alongside CALPHAD outputs to guide the composition search toward printable, high-performance, supply-chain-resilient alloys. FLAIME's preliminary results (atomizer installed, AlSi10Mg tensile baseline from U of Utah and 3D Powder Tech, SEM/EDS of precursor and atomized powders, TC AM Module simulations, BYU HPC CALPHAD runs, automated powder dispensing demonstration) validate each stage of this welding-to-AM transfer pipeline.
