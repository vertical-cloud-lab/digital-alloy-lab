## Eagar–Tsai Beam/Process Parameter Ranges for AlSi10Mg LPBF on an Aconity MIDI

### 1. Aconity MIDI Hardware

The Aconity MIDI+ (Aconity3D, Germany) is an open-architecture LPBF system. As documented in a process-development study by Kantonen (2023), the configuration used comprised an IPG single-mode ytterbium fiber laser at 1070 nm wavelength, with a maximum output power of 400 W, a variable spot size from 80 µm up to 500 µm, a 250 mm diameter build plate, and argon shielding gas (kantonen2023processingofnovel pages 14-21). Scanning speeds up to 2000 mm/s (2.0 m/s) were employed in that study; Aconity systems are generally capable of galvanometer scanning speeds up to approximately 5–8 m/s, though practical LPBF speeds for AlSi10Mg rarely exceed 2.5 m/s. The adjustable spot size (80–500 µm) implies an optical z-shifter or comparable defocus mechanism, which is characteristic of Aconity's open-architecture design (kantonen2023processingofnovel pages 14-21).

**Comparison with common LPBF machines.** A comprehensive cross-study comparison of AlSi10Mg LPBF machines reports beam diameters spanning approximately 0.06–0.20 mm across different systems (krasniqi2024comprehensivestudyon pages 25-27). The EOS M290—by far the most commonly used machine in AlSi10Mg literature—has a 400 W Yb fiber laser and a nominal spot size of approximately 100 µm (krasniqi2024comprehensivestudyon pages 25-27). The Concept Laser M2 uses a spot around 50–70 µm; the SLM Solutions SLM 280 typically quotes 80–115 µm; and Renishaw, TRUMPF, and 3D Systems machines generally fall within 50–100 µm. All use single-mode Yb fiber lasers near 1060–1080 nm. The Aconity MIDI's 80 µm focused spot is thus comparable to the EOS M290, while its variable defocus capability (up to 500 µm) is a distinctive feature that enables large-spot processing studies.

### 2. Laser Power and Scan Velocity for AlSi10Mg

**(a) Full explored range.** The broadest parameter exploration in the AlSi10Mg literature spans laser powers from 100 W up to 967 W and scan speeds from 250 to 2000 mm/s (0.25–2.0 m/s). Aboulkhair et al. (2016) studied single tracks and fusion lines at a fixed 100 W with scan speeds of 250–1500 mm/s (aboulkhair2016ontheformation pages 1-3, aboulkhair2016ontheformation pages 3-4). Zavala-Arredondo et al. (2019) evaluated L-PBF systems operating from 175 to 967 W with laser focus diameters of 75–300 µm (zavalaarredondo2019useofpower pages 1-2). On the Aconity MIDI+, Kantonen (2023) used laser powers from 110 to 300 W and scan velocities from 400 to 2000 mm/s (kantonen2023processingofnovel pages 14-21).

**(b) Dense-part processing window (≥99.5% relative density).** The typical dense-part window for AlSi10Mg lies at approximately 200–370 W and 800–1600 mm/s (0.8–1.6 m/s). Liu et al. (2020) identified optimized LPBF processing windows for ≥99% dense AlSi10Mg samples using Gaussian process regression, with critical parameters of laser power and scan speed as primary variables (liu2020machinelearningassistedlaser pages 1-5). Krasniqi and Löffler (2024, doi:10.1007/s44245-024-00073-4) report 200–350 W and 400–1600 mm/s for achieving up to 99.96% density (krasniqi2024comprehensivestudyon pages 24-25). Defanti et al. (2022, doi:10.3390/jmmp6050112) fixed power at 370 W and varied speed from 800 to 2000 mm/s at 60 µm layer thickness (defanti2022boostingproductivityof pages 2-4). Common vendor default parameters for AlSi10Mg (e.g., EOS M290, 30 µm layer) typically center around 370 W and 1300 mm/s with 0.19 mm hatch spacing; for 60 µm layers, higher power (370 W) with moderate speeds (~1000–1300 mm/s) is used.

**(c) Printability-map sweep range.** To span the lack-of-fusion, balling, and keyhole regimes, the recommended power–velocity sweep for the Aconity MIDI is **50–400 W** and **100–2500 mm/s (0.1–2.5 m/s)**. The low-power/high-speed corner ensures lack-of-fusion conditions, while the high-power/low-speed corner captures keyhole-mode melting (aboulkhair2016ontheformation pages 1-3, krasniqi2024comprehensivestudyon pages 24-25, defanti2022boostingproductivityof pages 2-4).

### 3. Beam Diameter

**Spot sizes in the literature.** The most commonly quoted spot sizes in AlSi10Mg studies range from approximately 50 to 100 µm, though the beam-profile definition is rarely stated explicitly (krasniqi2024comprehensivestudyon pages 25-27, hierlihy2020laserpowderbed pages 14-19). The Aconity MIDI+ reports a spot size of 80–500 µm without specifying whether this is a 1/e² diameter, FWHM, or another convention (kantonen2023processingofnovel pages 14-21). Ye et al. (2019, doi:10.1002/adem.201900185) explicitly state their beam diameters of 50–100 µm are 1/e² diameters, which is the D4σ (= 4σ) convention for a Gaussian beam (ye2019energycouplingmechanisms pages 2-3).

**Conversion to the eagar-tsai `2σ` convention.** The ArroyaveLab `eagar-tsai` package defines `beam_diameter = 2σ`, where σ is the standard deviation of the Gaussian heat-source distribution. Since the D4σ (= 1/e²) diameter equals 4σ, the `beam_diameter` input is exactly **half** the 1/e² diameter. The FWHM relates to σ as FWHM = 2√(2 ln 2) · σ ≈ 2.355σ, so beam_diameter ≈ 0.849 × FWHM. The conversions for common spot sizes are:

- **Aconity MIDI focused spot** (80 µm, assumed 1/e²): `beam_diameter = 40 µm = 4.0×10⁻⁵ m`
- **EOS M290** (~100 µm, assumed 1/e²): `beam_diameter = 50 µm = 5.0×10⁻⁵ m`
- **Concept Laser M2** (~50 µm 1/e²): `beam_diameter = 25 µm = 2.5×10⁻⁵ m`
- **Aconity MIDI defocused** (500 µm, assumed 1/e²): `beam_diameter = 250 µm = 2.5×10⁻⁴ m`

Because most manufacturer specifications do not state the beam-profile convention, there is inherent uncertainty of up to a factor of ~1.2 (i.e., between FWHM and 1/e² definitions). Users should note that if the manufacturer quotes a FWHM, the `beam_diameter` for eagar-tsai is approximately 0.849 × FWHM.

**Defocused/large-spot studies.** Zavala-Arredondo et al. (2019) studied laser focus diameters up to 300 µm in AlSi10Mg (zavalaarredondo2019useofpower pages 1-2). The Aconity MIDI's variable spot to 500 µm enables defocused processing experiments.

### 4. Absorptivity at ~1060–1080 nm for AlSi10Mg and Al Alloys

**(a) Flat, polished solid aluminium.** The ideal (oxide-free) flat-surface absorptivity of pure aluminium at ~1 µm is approximately 0.05 (5%) (boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 1-2). However, Boley et al. (2016, doi:10.1364/AO.55.006496) measured a bulk aluminium absorptivity of approximately 0.20 (20%), attributing the discrepancy to the native oxide layer, surface roughness, and defects on real aluminium surfaces (boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 5-5, boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 1-2). Their ray-tracing calculations used a flat-surface absorptivity of 0.047 for ideal aluminium at 1 µm (boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 2-3).

**(b) Liquid aluminium.** The absorptivity of liquid aluminium at ~1 µm is expected to be somewhat higher than the solid due to increased electrical resistivity, but explicit measurements in the LPBF context are sparse. The Drude model predicts an increase as resistivity rises upon melting.

**(c) Measured powder-bed absorptance.** Boley et al. (2016) measured room-temperature powder absorptivity of 0.53 ± 0.03 for pure Al and 0.58 ± 0.03 for Al 6061 using calorimetry at ~970 nm (boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 5-5). Their ray-tracing calculations yielded 0.18–0.24 for aluminium powder depending on particle arrangement, using an ideal flat-surface absorptivity of 0.047 (boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 2-3). When using the measured oxide-film-enhanced flat-surface absorptivity of 0.20, the ray-tracing model predicts powder absorptivity consistent with the experimental 0.53 value (boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 3-5, boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 5-5). Trapp et al. (2017, doi:10.1016/j.apmt.2017.08.006) demonstrated that powder layers have approximately twice the absorptivity of bare polished discs at low laser power for all metals studied (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 1-2). For AlSi10Mg specifically, Solyaev et al. (2026, doi:10.1007/s00161-026-01516-x) measured absorptivity of AlSi10Mg powders sieved to different particle sizes at 1070 nm: absorptivity ranged from 0.11 for the coarsest fraction (d50 = 63 µm) to 0.38 for the finest (d50 = 28 µm), with the standard powder (d50 = 44 µm) at approximately 0.22 (solyaev2026ontheinfluence pages 18-20). Spectroscopy studies cited by Solyaev reported absorptivity of AlSi10Mg powders in the range 0.59–0.63 for d50 = 31–41 µm powders (solyaev2026ontheinfluence pages 1-3).

**(d) Keyhole-mode effective absorptivity.** Ye et al. (2019, doi:10.1002/adem.201900185) demonstrated that absorptivity rises sharply at the conduction-to-keyhole transition and asymptotically saturates at approximately 0.70 for multiple alloys including Ti-6Al-4V, Inconel 625, and 316L SS, described by Ae = 0.70[1 − exp(−0.66βAmL*th)] (ye2019energycouplingmechanisms pages 7-9). The minimum conduction-mode absorptivities (Am) measured on bare substrates were 0.26–0.28 for these metals (ye2019energycouplingmechanisms pages 3-4). For Al alloys, the keyhole enhancement is similarly expected, with absorptivity potentially rising from ~0.15–0.25 in conduction mode toward 0.5–0.7 in deep keyhole mode, though direct calorimetric measurements for aluminium in keyhole mode are limited.

**(e) Back-calibrated values from Eagar–Tsai models.** Solyaev et al. (2026) back-calibrated absorptivity by fitting Eagar–Tsai model (ETM) melt-pool widths to measured AlSi10Mg single-track cross-sections. They found absorptivity of 0.11–0.38 depending on powder particle size (coarsest to finest fractions), with the standard powder at ~0.22 by scaling-law analysis and ~0.26 by finite-element simulation (solyaev2026ontheinfluence pages 18-20). These values were obtained from the lack-of-fusion boundary analysis at different Peclet numbers (solyaev2026ontheinfluence pages 15-18). The coarsest powder approached the bare-surface absorptivity of approximately 0.10 (solyaev2026ontheinfluence pages 18-20).

**Role of oxide film, multiple scattering, and Mg evaporation.** The native Al₂O₃ oxide layer substantially increases absorption beyond the ideal metal value—Boley et al. (2016) showed that using 20% rather than 5% flat-surface absorptivity reconciled the model with measured powder absorptivity (boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 5-5, boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 1-2). Multiple scattering between powder particles further enhances absorption by a factor of approximately 2–5× relative to the flat surface (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 1-2, boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 2-3). Mg evaporation at temperatures above ~1100°C can create a plume/plasma above the melt pool that attenuates the laser beam; this is partially captured in "effective" absorptivity measurements but is not modeled in the Eagar–Tsai framework.

**Values used in CALPHAD-based printability map studies.** Sheikh et al. (2024, doi:10.1038/s41524-024-01436-x; preprint doi:10.48550/arxiv.2304.04113) estimated absorptivity using Drude's theory: A = 0.365√(ρ₀/λ), where ρ₀ is the electrical resistivity and λ is the laser wavelength, with material properties calculated at the liquidus temperature via ThermoCalc (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 5-8). For aluminium alloys, this yields an absorptivity on the order of 0.08–0.15 (depending on the resistivity value used), representing a flat-surface estimate. Whalen et al. (2021, doi:10.1007/s40192-021-00238-z) used Bayesian inference to calibrate absorptivity as a power-dependent distribution for 316L SS in a modified Eagar–Tsai framework, demonstrating that absorptivity should not be treated as a constant across the entire power–velocity space (whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 4-7, whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 1-4). Johnson et al. (2019, Acta Materialia, doi:10.1016/j.actamat.2019.07.005) used a similar Eagar–Tsai-based approach with a fixed absorptivity calibrated to experimental melt-pool data. The Thermo-Calc AM Module typically uses CALPHAD-calculated material properties and either a Drude-estimated or user-specified absorptivity.

**Key notes on the table:**

1. **Beam diameter** is the most sensitive to definition ambiguity. The Aconity MIDI reports 80 µm without specifying whether this is a 1/e² or FWHM value (kantonen2023processingofnovel pages 14-21). Assuming 1/e² (the most common manufacturer convention), `beam_diameter = 2σ = 40 µm`. If the manufacturer quotes FWHM, the correct value would be ~34 µm. This ~15% uncertainty should be noted.

2. **Power** is strongly constrained by hardware: the Aconity MIDI's 400 W single-mode Yb fiber laser sets the upper bound (kantonen2023processingofnovel pages 14-21). The recommended sweep of 50–400 W ensures coverage from lack-of-fusion through keyhole regimes.

3. **Velocity** should extend from 0.1 m/s (deep keyhole at high power) to at least 2.0 m/s (balling/lack-of-fusion at moderate power) (aboulkhair2016ontheformation pages 1-3, krasniqi2024comprehensivestudyon pages 24-25, defanti2022boostingproductivityof pages 2-4). Extending to 2.5 m/s provides margin.

4. **Absorptivity** is the most uncertain parameter and the one most commonly used as a calibration variable. A single constant of 0.20–0.30 captures conduction-mode behavior for standard AlSi10Mg powder (d50 ≈ 30–45 µm) on the Aconity MIDI (solyaev2026ontheinfluence pages 18-20, boley2016metalpowderabsorptivity pages 5-5). However, users should recognize that: (i) absorptivity varies with process conditions—it is lower (~0.10–0.15) at the flat-surface/Drude limit and much higher (~0.5–0.7) in keyhole mode (ye2019energycouplingmechanisms pages 7-9); (ii) the Eagar–Tsai model cannot physically capture keyhole-enhanced absorption, so calibrated values in the keyhole regime are effective/phenomenological; and (iii) a Bayesian calibration approach as in Whalen et al. (2021) is recommended for rigorous uncertainty quantification (whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 4-7).
