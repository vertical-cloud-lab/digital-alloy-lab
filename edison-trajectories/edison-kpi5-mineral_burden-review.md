2. Is ≥1.15 hypervolume improvement realistic? What hypervolume improvements have been demonstrated in comparable multi-objective BO campaigns? Reference BIRDSHOT/ULTIMATE (54% hypervolume increase, Hastings et al. 2024), Szymanski et al. (2023) A-Lab, other MOBO campaigns in materials science.
3. The mineral-burden scalarization approach (folding supply risk into the BOFN objective) — is this well-established? How does it compare to explicit multi-objective optimization (EHVI, MESMO)? Could scalarization mask important trade-offs?

6. What improvements would make this KPI more impactful and defensible?

# Critical Literature-Grounded Review of the FLAIME "Mineral-Burden Optimization Efficacy" KPI

## 1. Is Hypervolume Ratio the Right Metric?

The hypervolume (HV) indicator is the most theoretically justified choice among common multi-objective quality indicators for this KPI. Shang et al. (2021), in their comprehensive survey, establish that hypervolume is the only Pareto-compliant unary indicator widely used in evolutionary multi-objective optimization, meaning that if one solution set strictly dominates another, the hypervolume indicator will reflect this (shang2021asurveyon pages 1-1, shang2021asurveyon pages 1-2). Crucially, hypervolume simultaneously captures both convergence toward the true Pareto front and diversity/coverage of solutions, and it does not require knowledge of the true Pareto front — a practical necessity in experimental materials discovery where the true front is unknown (ibrahim2017towardenhancementof pages 57-62, shang2021asurveyon pages 1-1).

Alternative indicators each have significant limitations in this context:

The **epsilon-indicator** (ε) measures only the worst-case dominance gap (convergence), not diversity (ibrahim2017towardenhancementof pages 57-62). **Generational distance (GD)** and **inverted generational distance (IGD)** both require a known or trusted true Pareto front, which is unavailable in closed-loop experimental campaigns (ibrahim2017towardenhancementof pages 57-62). **Spread (Δ)** measures only diversity/uniformity and ignores convergence quality (ibrahim2017towardenhancementof pages 57-62). The **R2 indicator** can encode preference information and is computationally cheaper, but lacks the formal Pareto-compliance guarantee of hypervolume (shang2021asurveyon pages 1-1).

However, hypervolume has known weaknesses: it is computationally expensive in higher objective dimensions, and the choice of reference point can significantly influence the resulting solution distribution (shang2021asurveyon pages 1-1, shang2021asurveyon pages 13-14). The standard hypervolume may also produce nonuniform optimal distributions on nonlinear Pareto fronts (shang2021asurveyon pages 14-15). Hastings et al. (2024) explicitly noted that "future optimization studies should complement hypervolume with additional measures" (hastings2024aninteroperablemulti pages 15-17).

**What Mulukutla et al. (2024) used:** In the closest precedent work on supply-risk-aware alloy optimization (ArXiv 2409.15391), Mulukutla et al. incorporated a composite Supply Risk Index (SRI) — based on Helbig et al.'s framework with twelve elemental-level indicators across supply reduction, demand, market concentration, and political risk categories — as an explicit additional objective in multi-objective batch Bayesian optimization (MOBBO) (mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 8-10, mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 2-5). They used hypervolume improvement (HVI) as their convergence metric, defined as HVI(x) = HV(P ∪ x) − HV(P), and tracked cumulative hypervolume across iterations (mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 10-12). Critically, they treated supply risk as a separate Pareto objective rather than scalarizing it into a combined utility, and used EHVI-based acquisition functions (mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 10-12). This validates hypervolume as the metric of choice for supply-risk-aware MOBO, but Mulukutla et al. did not compute a "hypervolume ratio" between with-SRI and without-SRI campaigns; they instead compared Pareto fronts and convergence behavior across four case configurations (mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 12-16, mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 8-10).

**Recommendation:** Hypervolume ratio is defensible as the primary metric but should be supplemented with (a) Pareto front cardinality and coverage statistics, (b) a dominance comparison showing what fraction of mineral-burden-aware solutions dominate performance-only solutions, and (c) reference point sensitivity analysis.

## 2. Is ≥1.15 Hypervolume Improvement Realistic?

The proposed Phase I target of a hypervolume ratio ≥1.15 (i.e., ≥15% improvement) must be evaluated carefully, because the FLAIME comparison is fundamentally different from published benchmarks. The literature reports iteration-over-iteration hypervolume growth within a single MOBO campaign, not the incremental value of adding an objective to the same campaign.

Paramore et al. (2025) reported a 54% increase in the hypervolume of the Pareto front between the first and second iterations of a two-shot batch BO campaign on compositionally complex refractory alloys using the BIRDSHOT framework, where 10 of 24 second-iteration alloys dominated all first-iteration alloys (paramore2025twoshotoptimizationof pages 16-18, paramore2025twoshotoptimizationof pages 1-3). Hastings et al. (2024) reported that hypervolume "consistently increases with each iteration" over five experimental iterations in a three-objective FCC HEA campaign, though they did not report a single percentage figure and cautioned that "improvement from the 1st iteration" alone is insufficient as a metric (hastings2024aninteroperablemulti pages 13-15, hastings2024aninteroperablemulti pages 12-13). Mulukutla et al. (2024) reported that by the sixth iteration, marginal hypervolume improvement was less than 5% (mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 12-16). Alvi et al. (2026) showed hypervolume improvements ranging from ~9% on ZDT1 benchmarks to over 100% on latent multi-output test problems, depending on surrogate quality and baseline (alvi2026deepgaussianprocessbased pages 6-9).

**Critical distinction:** These published gains measure how much BO improves across iterations within a fixed objective formulation. FLAIME proposes measuring the value added by including mineral-burden as an objective versus excluding it. This is a smaller, more subtle effect: adding a partially correlated objective to an existing MOBO campaign will not necessarily produce the same magnitude of hypervolume gain as additional optimization iterations. If the search space is already restricted to mineral-lean compositions (Al-Zr, Al-Mn-Cr-Zr families), the mineral-burden objective may have limited variance, making even a 10% hypervolume ratio ambitious.

**Assessment:** The base target of ≥1.10 is likely achievable if the mineral-burden objective introduces meaningful conflict with at least one performance objective. The stretch target of ≥1.15 is plausible but not guaranteed, particularly in a 9-month Phase I with limited experimental iterations. The target would benefit from being contextualized against a simulated in-silico baseline before experimental execution.

## 3. Scalarization vs. Explicit Multi-Objective Optimization

The description of FLAIME suggests using BOFN (Bayesian Optimization with Function Networks) as the optimization engine. Original BOFN (Buathong et al. 2023) was designed for scalar objectives — it models the function network structure with GP priors on individual nodes and uses a knowledge-gradient acquisition function, but handles only a single scalar objective output (buathong2023bayesianoptimizationof pages 3-4, buathong2023bayesianoptimizationof pages 1-2). This means that if FLAIME uses standard BOFN, multi-objective handling would require either scalarization (folding mineral-burden into a weighted sum with performance) or an extension to multi-objective BOFN.

Kudva et al. (2025) recently introduced MOBONS, the first multi-objective extension of function-network BO, which uses Thompson sampling to natively find Pareto sets without scalarization and evaluates results via the hypervolume metric (kudva2502multiobjectivebayesianoptimization pages 4-6, kudva2502multiobjectivebayesianoptimization pages 6-9, kudva2502multiobjectivebayesianoptimization pages 13-16). This suggests that native multi-objective function-network BO is feasible but very new.

The literature strongly cautions against scalarization for this use case. Yong et al. (2025) conducted a tightly controlled comparison showing that EHVI (Pareto-aware) consistently outperforms scalarized EI in terms of Pareto front coverage, convergence speed, and chemical diversity, with large effect sizes (Cohen's d = 0.576–1.093 for hypervolume) (yong2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 5-6, yong2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 6-8). Scalarization "collapses multiple objectives into a single score" requiring a priori knowledge of objective weightings, yields "only a single point on the Pareto front" per run, and can miss nonconvex regions of the Pareto front (yong2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 1-3). Daulton et al. (2020) emphasize that "optimizing a single scalarized objective often sacrifices other outcomes" and that forming a scalarization is difficult for most practitioners (daulton2020differentiableexpectedhypervolume pages 1-2, daulton2020differentiableexpectedhypervolume pages 9-11). Feliot et al. (2017) characterize aggregation-based approaches as heuristic, noting that "none of them emerges from a proper Bayesian formulation" (feliot2017abayesianapproach pages 3-5).

**Critical implication for FLAIME:** If mineral-burden is scalarized into the BOFN objective (e.g., as a weighted penalty), this will (a) mask the performance-vs.-mineral-burden trade-off that is the core value proposition of the KPI, (b) make the hypervolume ratio metric conceptually incoherent (because there would be no multi-dimensional Pareto front to compute hypervolume over in the "with mineral-burden" case), and (c) make results sensitive to the arbitrary weighting choice. The proposal should clarify whether BOFN will be extended to native multi-objective handling (e.g., via MOBONS-style Thompson sampling) or whether mineral-burden will be treated as a separate Pareto objective using EHVI-based acquisition.

## 4. Baseline Definition

The proposed baseline — "performance-only BO" optimizing only YS/EL without the mineral-burden constraint — raises definitional concerns. Mulukutla et al. (2024) addressed this by running four distinct case studies: full design space with and without supply risk, and constrained feasible space with and without supply risk (mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 8-10). Their supply-risk-inclusive runs converged in 6 iterations versus 7 without, and the inclusion of supply risk opened different regions of composition space (mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 12-16).

For FLAIME, the baseline definition is complicated by the fact that the primary alloy families (Al-Zr, Al-Mn-Cr-Zr) were deliberately chosen to be mineral-lean. If the search space is already restricted to these families, then a "performance-only" BO operating in the same space will also produce mineral-lean alloys by construction. The mineral-burden objective would then have limited additional steering power, and the hypervolume ratio would approach 1.0 (no benefit).

**Recommendation:** The baseline should be defined as performance-only BO over a broader composition space that includes both mineral-lean and mineral-rich candidates (e.g., including higher levels of potentially critical elements). Alternatively, the Cu-Cr-Zr complement space could serve as a testbed where mineral-burden objectives have more variance and can meaningfully differentiate candidate compositions.

## 5. Risk of Trivial Satisfaction

This is a genuine and serious risk. If ~80% of the experimental effort targets Al-Zr and Al-Mn-Cr-Zr families that are inherently low in critical minerals (Al, Zr, Mn, and Cr are all relatively non-critical), then the mineral-burden objective may exhibit minimal variance across the feasible design space. In such a scenario, virtually all compositions would satisfy any reasonable mineral-burden threshold, and the hypervolume ratio would be near unity regardless of whether the objective was included or not — rendering the KPI meaningless.

The ~20% Cu-Cr-Zr stretch work (targeting Be-Cu replacement) offers more potential for meaningful mineral-burden discrimination (Be is a critical and hazardous material), but at 20% of effort this may not provide enough data to demonstrate the KPI with statistical power.

**Safeguards:**
- Pre-compute the variance of the Supply Risk Index across the candidate composition space before experimental execution. If variance is below a threshold, the KPI should be redesigned.
- Include sufficient compositional diversity that some candidates have meaningfully higher mineral-burden scores than others.
- Report the "mineral-burden conflict coefficient" — the correlation between performance objectives and mineral-burden — to demonstrate that the objectives are not trivially aligned.

## 6. Recommendations for a More Impactful and Defensible KPI

Based on the literature review, the following improvements would strengthen the "Mineral-Burden Optimization Efficacy" KPI:

**(a) Adopt explicit multi-objective treatment.** Following Mulukutla et al. (2024), treat mineral-burden as a separate Pareto objective rather than scalarizing it into BOFN (mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 8-10, mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 10-12). Either extend BOFN to multi-objective (kudva2502multiobjectivebayesianoptimization pages 4-6, kudva2502multiobjectivebayesianoptimization pages 6-9) or use a standard MOBO framework (qEHVI) alongside the function-network surrogate structure.

**(b) Define a meaningful baseline over a broader space.** Run performance-only BO over a composition space that includes both mineral-lean and mineral-rich candidates, so that the mineral-burden objective has room to redirect the search. Report the Pareto front comparison, not just a single hypervolume number.

**(c) Report multiple quality metrics.** In addition to hypervolume ratio, report: (i) number of non-dominated solutions in each campaign, (ii) the fraction of mineral-burden-aware Pareto-optimal solutions that dominate performance-only solutions on all performance axes, (iii) the mineral-burden variance across the Pareto set, and (iv) a reference point sensitivity analysis (shang2021asurveyon pages 1-1, shang2021asurveyon pages 13-14, hastings2024aninteroperablemulti pages 15-17).

**(d) Add a "mineral-burden conflict" pre-check.** Before claiming the KPI, demonstrate that mineral-burden and at least one performance objective are genuinely conflicting in the design space (negative correlation or trade-off region). If they are not conflicting, the KPI is not meaningful. Mulukutla et al. showed this by demonstrating that Mo-rich alloys had higher supply risk, creating genuine multi-objective trade-offs (mulukutla2024supplyriskawarealloy pages 12-16).

**(e) Calibrate targets against in-silico experiments.** Run the with-vs-without-mineral-burden comparison in silico (using CALPHAD-only predicted properties) before committing to a Phase I experimental target. This would provide a data-driven basis for the ≥1.15 threshold rather than an assumption.

**(f) Consider a tiered KPI.** Phase I (9 months) could target a simpler KPI: demonstrate that mineral-burden-aware BO identifies at least one Pareto-optimal alloy composition that a performance-only BO would not have explored, with comparable or improved performance. Phase II can then target the more ambitious multi-objective Pareto hypervolume comparison with ≥3 objectives.

**(g) Fix the reference point protocol.** Since hypervolume is sensitive to the reference point (shang2021asurveyon pages 1-1, shang2021asurveyon pages 13-14), specify in advance a fixed, worst-case reference point derived from the boundary of the composition space. This prevents post-hoc manipulation of the hypervolume ratio.

In summary, the hypervolume ratio is a theoretically sound choice, and a ≥1.15 target is plausible but ambitious given the constrained, already-mineral-lean search space. The most critical risk is trivial satisfaction — that the mineral-burden objective adds no meaningful signal when the search space is already restricted to non-critical-mineral alloy families. Addressing this through broader search spaces, conflict verification, and multi-metric reporting would substantially strengthen the KPI's defensibility for DOE reviewers.
