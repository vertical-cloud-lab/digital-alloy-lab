# Machine Learning for Process–Structure–Property Relationships in Laser Powder Bed Fusion: State of the Art (2022–2026)

## Overview

The application of machine learning (ML) to laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) has matured rapidly from 2022 to 2026, with data-driven and physics-informed approaches now spanning the full process–structure–property (PSP) chain. This review synthesizes the current state of the art across five interconnected domains: process–defect models, process–microstructure models, microstructure–property models, ML-driven process optimization, and in-situ monitoring with ML for real-time quality assurance.

## 1. ML Models Connecting Process Parameters to Defect Formation

Machine learning models that map LPBF process parameters (laser power, scan speed, hatch spacing, layer thickness) to defect formation (porosity, cracking, surface roughness, balling) represent the most developed area. The dominant approaches include tree-based ensemble methods, neural networks, support vector machines, Gaussian process (GP) surrogates, and convolutional neural networks.

**Tree-based ensembles** have shown the strongest performance in tabular process–parameter spaces. A comprehensive benchmark of 15 ML algorithms for melt-pool-based defect classification found that CatBoost, LightGBM, and XGBoost achieved the highest classification accuracies (92.47%, 91.08%, and 90.89%, respectively), while a deep neural network reached 88.55% (ilani2509amdefectnetadditivemanufacturing pages 1-2). **Artificial neural networks** applied to density ratio and surface roughness prediction have achieved R² ≈ 0.99 in some studies (liu2023areviewof pages 11-13). For **balling defect prediction**, a neural network trained on a literature-derived composition–processing dataset achieved 92.3% accuracy, while a random forest regressor for porosity percentage attained MSE ≈ 0.0119 (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 30-35, roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 35-38). Feature-importance analysis consistently identifies laser power and scan speed as the most influential process parameters, alongside material descriptors such as thermal conductivity, carbon content, and silicon content (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 35-38).

**Gaussian process surrogates** are widely used because they provide built-in uncertainty estimates and perform well with small datasets (tens to ~100 data points), making them suitable for mapping keyhole regimes and process windows (mccarthy2025surrogatebasedmultiobjectiveoptimization pages 31-36). **Image-based approaches** using convolutional neural networks on melt-pool or scan-track images have reported classification accuracies in the 90–99% range for distinguishing defective from acceptable conditions, including porosity, over-melting, lack-of-fusion, and cracking defects (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 5-7). However, very few physics-based, imaging-independent models exist that link alloy chemistry to printability (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 5-7). A notable exception used mechanistic descriptors (Marangoni number, surface tension, Richardson number, pool aspect ratio, solidification time, volumetric energy density) to predict balling with 90% accuracy (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 5-7).

A key limitation is that the literature is hampered by a lack of large open experimental datasets and costly labeling, which limits generalization and direct comparison of ML model accuracies across studies (atwya2024insituporosityprediction pages 2-4).

## 2. ML Models Predicting Microstructure from Process Parameters

Predicting microstructural features—grain morphology, crystallographic texture, phase fractions, and solidification structure—from LPBF process parameters represents a rapidly evolving frontier. The approaches span classical ML, deep learning, and hybrid physics-informed frameworks.

**Convolutional neural networks** (including 3D CNNs, U-Net, and VGGNet architectures) are widely applied for image-based grain morphology, orientation, and defect mapping from SEM, EBSD, optical microscopy, and X-ray computed tomography data (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 14-16). **Generative models**, particularly conditional GANs (cGANs), are used to reconstruct microstructural features from processing parameters and to augment scarce experimental datasets (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 14-16, patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 16-19). **Sequence models** such as LSTM-based architectures (e.g., GrainNN) capture temporal grain growth and solidification evolution, while **graph neural networks** (GNNs, GrainGNN) represent grains as nodes and boundaries as edges to capture topological features important for texture and local mechanical response (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 14-16).

**Physics-informed machine learning (PIML)** has emerged as a particularly promising paradigm. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) embed governing PDEs (heat transfer, momentum, grain growth equations) into loss functions, reducing data requirements and improving physical plausibility (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 19-21). Variants include transfer-learning PINNs for 3D temperature prediction, thermoelastic PINNs, and hybrid models coupling PINNs with cellular automata (CA) for thermo-microstructural simulation and calibration (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 19-21). **Physics-Embedded Graph Networks (PEGN)** reformulate phase-field microstructure evolution as an unsupervised graph ML task, accelerating simulations by orders of magnitude while maintaining physical consistency (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 14-16).

Hybrid physics-simulation + ML pipelines are also common: cellular automata coupled with finite-element thermal models (CAFE) provide synthetic training data for surrogate ML models, kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) approaches including SPPARKS and Potts-type models capture grain-boundary migration and crystallographic texture, and phase-field methods are integrated with ML and finite-difference Monte Carlo for dendrite growth and phase transformation prediction (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 11-14). The physical relationships between thermal gradient G and solidification rate R (and cooling rate G×R) that determine grain morphology, texture, and phase formation serve as key physics constraints for these models (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 3-5). Remaining challenges include data scarcity, multi-scale coupling, uncertainty quantification, and achieving real-time responsiveness across platforms (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 3-5, patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 26-28).

## 3. ML Models Linking Microstructure to Mechanical Properties

Connecting microstructure to mechanical properties (strength, ductility, fatigue, hardness) using ML represents the final link in the PSP chain. The recently developed **MPR-Net** (metallography–property relationship neural network) maps optical metallography images of LPBF 316L stainless steel to mechanical properties, achieving R² = 0.96 for tensile strength and R² = 0.91 for Vickers hardness. Gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) and clustering provide interpretability by linking learned features to molten pool morphology and grain formation mechanisms (zhang2024deeplearningdrivenprediction pages 1-2).

Other architectures include CNN/VGGNet models trained on 3D microstructure images for yield strength prediction, random forest classifiers applied to segmented α-particle metrics correlated with hardness, ensemble kriging/RBF/SVR models for tensile strength, and GP surrogates that replace expensive mechanistic simulations (liu2023areviewof pages 18-21). **Graph neural networks** have been applied to fatigue damage prediction by representing grain topology as graph structures, capturing the connectivity and adjacency important to crack initiation and propagation (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 14-16, patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 33-35). Manifold-learning methods (diffusion maps) have been used to infer low-dimensional structure–property dependence from small datasets (liu2023areviewof pages 18-21).

**Transfer learning** has been demonstrated through Bayesian knowledge transfer between Inconel alloys, where a process–relative density model for IN625 was built using knowledge from IN718, achieving R = 0.95 and outperforming pure IN625-only models across different dataset sizes (lu2022knowledgetransferusing pages 14-16, lu2022knowledgetransferusing pages 1-3). The transfer approach reduced mean RMSE and MAE by up to 0.7% and 0.5%, respectively, demonstrating that cross-alloy knowledge transfer is feasible when alloys share similar thermophysical behavior (lu2022knowledgetransferusing pages 1-3). Fine-tuning simulation-trained models on sparse experimental data is another form of transfer learning that improves real-world predictive performance (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 16-19).

**Physics-informed approaches** for property prediction include PEGN models that accelerate phase-field simulations for microstructure-dependent properties, PINNs for fatigue life prediction incorporating continuous damage mechanics, and hybrid data-physics pipelines that couple thermal simulations with data-driven property inference (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 33-35, patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 16-19). Multi-task learning and hierarchical multi-scale surrogates enable simultaneous prediction of multiple microstructural quantities underlying mechanical properties (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 16-19).

## 4. Process Parameter Optimization Using ML

Bayesian optimization (BO) and multi-objective optimization have become the standard approaches for efficient LPBF process development, dramatically reducing the number of required experiments.

**Multi-objective Bayesian optimization (MOBO)** using Gaussian process surrogates has been demonstrated for SS316L, where the DGEMO algorithm converged in approximately six iterations, achieving experimental Pareto solutions with porosity of 0.2–0.37% and hardness of 224–235 HV (chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 7-9, chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 1-2). The recommended optimal parameters were 58 W laser power, 257 mm/s scan speed, 45 µm hatch distance, with a scan rotation angle of 131°. This approach reduced the experimental burden by orders of magnitude compared to full-factorial designs (e.g., 15,625 tests for a five-level, six-factor design) (chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 1-2). The AutoOED platform was used for automated experimental design and Bayesian updates (chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 2-4).

For Ti-6Al-4V, a combined **NSGA-II + GP regression + active learning** framework optimized the trade-off between relative density and build rate for modulated-wave LPBF (mccarthy2025surrogatebasedmultiobjectiveoptimization pages 98-103). Batch active learning improved surrogate accuracy, reducing test error from 9.61% to 6.59% over two iterations of 24 samples. The resulting Pareto sets revealed three operating regions: conventional high-quality processing, regions amenable to hot isostatic pressing remediation, and faster but lower-quality regimes (mccarthy2025surrogatebasedmultiobjectiveoptimization pages 98-103).

A study on **AZ31 magnesium alloy** compared initialization strategies (Latin Hypercube Sampling, balanced-marginal, fractional-factorial) for MOBO using DGEMO and TSEMO algorithms (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 1-2). Results showed that initialization strategy strongly governs subsequent optimization efficiency: prior fractional-factorial sampling yielded the highest hypervolume, while balanced-marginal sampling produced the most uniform Pareto approximation. Importantly, in constrained discrete LPBF design spaces, MOBO primarily served to validate and refine well-designed initial datasets rather than discover dramatically new optima (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 1-2). This finding highlights the practical importance of initial experimental design for data-efficient optimization.

## 5. In-Situ Monitoring Data Combined with ML for Real-Time Defect Prediction and Closed-Loop Control

The integration of in-situ monitoring with ML represents a critical pathway toward autonomous LPBF manufacturing. Sensor modalities include high-speed melt pool imaging, infrared/thermal cameras, pyrometers, photodiodes (including multispectral), and acoustic emission sensors. These are increasingly combined in **multi-sensor fusion** approaches to overcome individual sensor limitations (aydogan2024reviewofin pages 20-22).

**Thermographic-based ML** has achieved strong performance: random forest models trained on voxel-level thermal features (time above melting threshold and maximum radiance) achieved F1 scores above 0.96 for microporosity prediction in LPBF stainless steel, with the maximum radiance feature being the most predictive (estalaki2022predictingdefectsin pages 1-5). CNNs applied to thermographic data for larger defect detection have reported overall accuracy of ~97.9% (estalaki2022predictingdefectsin pages 1-5). Infrared thermal methods reliably detect lack-of-fusion defects larger than ~100 µm with near-100% accuracy, though performance drops significantly (~50%) for defects smaller than 50 µm (aydogan2024reviewofin pages 9-11). **Prior-guided neural networks** that incorporate abundant nominal-condition data as physics priors have localized micro-porosity as small as ~38 µm spherical equivalent diameter, representing a five-fold improvement over previous spectral-emission-based approaches, achieving classification accuracy of 73.13 ± 1.57% (atwya2024insituporosityprediction pages 2-4).

**Acoustic signal-based monitoring** represents a growing area, with deep learning approaches (spectral CNNs, wavelet-CNN hybrids) used for in-situ crack and keyhole pore detection, representing the first demonstrations of acoustic-signal-based in-situ defect classification in laser AM (chen2023insitucrackand pages 32-33). Multi-sensor fusion combining optical, thermal, and acoustic data with ML algorithms (CNN, KNN, SVM, MLP, SeDANN) provides complementary information about powder-bed irregularities, melt-pool geometry, and airborne signatures (aydogan2024reviewofin pages 20-22).

For **closed-loop control**, practical implementations focus primarily on laser power modulation because it is directly controllable, with demonstrations of real-time thermal-sensor-based power adjustments and layer-wise control strategies (suresh2025recentadvancesinin pages 23-24). However, a complete ML-based closed-loop system that autonomously detects, classifies, and corrects defects in LPBF has not yet been fully implemented (suresh2025recentadvancesinin pages 24-26). Key barriers include extreme laser scan speeds requiring >1 kHz sensing rates, high demands on low-latency data processing, and the difficulty of mapping detected anomalies to actionable process corrections in real time (suresh2025recentadvancesinin pages 23-24).

### Existing Datasets and Benchmarks

The data infrastructure for ML in LPBF remains fragmented. The **NIST Additive Manufacturing Materials Database (AMMD)** is the most prominent public repository, aggregating experimental process–property data, though broader community contributions and standardized data formats are still needed (liu2023areviewof pages 22-24). Most ML studies rely on **study-specific datasets** with typical sizes ranging from tens to thousands of labeled samples (e.g., image datasets of ~1,200 to ~100,000 patches for defect classification) (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 5-7). The **AM-DefectNet** benchmark evaluated 15 ML models on 1,514 training and 505 test samples for melt-pool-based defect classification (ilani2509amdefectnetadditivemanufacturing pages 1-2). Composition-based datasets remain rare; one notable effort compiled 267 data points for balling and 138 for porosity from literature spanning single- and multi-principal-element alloys (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 1-5).

### Key Challenges in Cross-Alloy Generalization

**Data scarcity** is the primary bottleneck, as acquiring calibrated, labeled AM data is time-consuming and costly, and physics-based simulations for synthetic data generation are computationally expensive (liu2023areviewof pages 22-24). **Heterogeneous reporting** across laboratories—inconsistent parameter nomenclature, missing metadata, and varying geometries and sensor configurations—hampers reproducibility and benchmarking (luo2025identificationofprocessstructureproperty pages 31-36, liu2023areviewof pages 22-24).

**Alloy-specific physics** fundamentally limits transferability: chemistry is "primal in determining the nature of the melt and how fusion takes place," and even within a single nominal alloy (e.g., SS316L can have Ni composition anywhere between 10–14 wt.%, with 10 elements varying by up to 4 wt.%), defect behavior can shift dramatically (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 5-7). Thermal properties (conductivity, specific heat), solidification pathways, and phase transformation kinetics differ substantially across alloy families, meaning models trained on one system (e.g., Ti-6Al-4V) cannot readily transfer to another (e.g., AlSi10Mg) without adaptation (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 1-5). **Machine and sensor variability** further complicates generalization, as different printers, optics, calibration procedures, and sensing modalities alter signal distributions (luo2025identificationofprocessstructureproperty pages 31-36).

### Emerging Cross-Alloy Approaches

Two notable strategies address alloy generalization. **Bayesian transfer learning** between Inconel alloys (IN718→IN625) demonstrated that process–property knowledge from a well-studied alloy can significantly improve prediction accuracy for a less-studied alloy (R = 0.95), with benefits increasing when some target-alloy data are available (lu2022knowledgetransferusing pages 14-16, lu2022knowledgetransferusing pages 1-3, lu2022knowledgetransferusing pages 3-4). An **alloy-agnostic convolutional neural network** using invariant information clustering (IIC) with a hybrid unsupervised/semi-supervised training approach achieved up to 96% accuracy in predicting melting-mode process maps across different alloys and generalized to materials outside the training set, leveraging powder-free single-track experiments as cost-effective proxies (wilkinson2024analloyagnosticmachine pages 1-2).

## Summary and Future Directions

The 2022–2026 period has seen ML approaches advance from single-linkage, single-alloy models to increasingly integrated PSP frameworks incorporating physics-informed constraints, multi-modal sensing, and cross-alloy transfer. Key trends include the emergence of PINNs and physics-embedded graph networks that improve data efficiency and physical consistency (patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 19-21, patel2025computationaldatadrivenand pages 1-3), the adoption of Bayesian multi-objective optimization for rapid process development (chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 7-9, pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 1-2), and early demonstrations of multi-sensor ML pipelines for in-situ quality monitoring (aydogan2024reviewofin pages 20-22, estalaki2022predictingdefectsin pages 1-5). However, critical gaps persist: fully closed-loop ML-controlled LPBF remains unrealized (suresh2025recentadvancesinin pages 24-26), standardized datasets and benchmarks are lacking (liu2023areviewof pages 22-24), uncertainty quantification pipelines are underdeveloped (vu2025modelingtheprocessmicrostructuremechanical pages 2-3), and robust generalization across alloy systems requires fundamentally new approaches that integrate alloy chemistry, thermophysical properties, and transferable physics-informed representations (roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 1-5, roy2024chemicalcompositionbased pages 5-7). Addressing these challenges will require community-wide data sharing, standardized experimental protocols, and hybrid ML frameworks that seamlessly couple domain knowledge with data-driven learning.
