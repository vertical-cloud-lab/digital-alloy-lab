## EMAIL 1: From an AMMTO Program Manager (Focus Area F perspective)

**Subject: Re: FLAIME concept — quick thoughts before you write**

Hey —

Thanks for sharing this early. I'll be candid. A few things jump out.

**What I like.** The fact that you already have a working atomizer at BYU producing Al alloy powder is *huge*. Most full proposals that cross my desk at this stage are PowerPoint-only. Having operational equipment and preliminary data puts you ahead of probably 60% of what I'll read. The commitment to ASTM E8/E8M full-size tensile specimens is exactly what AMMTO wants to hear — our whole qualification mandate is about bridging lab-scale curiosity to qualification-grade data, and miniature specimens or hardness-only studies make reviewers nervous (li2025advancedmaterialsand pages 6-10, kenny20242023peerreview pages 12-15). The BOFN framework treating the pipeline as a cost-aware DAG is genuinely novel for this application space, and it maps onto what we mean by "AI-enabled" — not just slapping an ML model on top of data, but using AI to intelligently orchestrate the entire discovery-to-qualification pipeline.

**Focus Area F vs. B: My honest take.** Focus Area F ("AI-Enabled Materials Discovery, Development, and Qualification") is the right home for this. Your *lead story* is the BOFN+LLM orchestration framework that treats the powder-to-part pipeline as a DAG and makes cost-aware decisions about where to invest experimental resources. That's an AI methods contribution applied to materials. Focus Area B would want you to lead with the materials science outcomes — novel alloy systems, microstructure-property relationships — and frankly, an Al-only scope in Focus Area B would feel narrow to reviewers who expect multi-material platforms. In Focus Area F, the Al family becomes a well-scoped demonstrator system for a generalizable AI framework, which is much more defensible. Frame it as: "We demonstrate BOFN on Al alloys in Phase I; the framework generalizes to Ni/Fe/Ti when the arc module comes online in Phase II."

**What concerns me.** (1) The distributed workflow — daily trips to University of Utah for printing — is a logistics risk. Reviewers will wonder what happens when the LPBF machine is down, or when builds fail. Build in explicit contingency and show you've thought about batch scheduling. (2) The LLM integration is exciting but also risky from a review perspective. If you frame LLAMBO warmstarting, LILO-style interpretation, and LLM-informed priors as co-equal with the BOFN core, reviewers may think you're over-scoping. LLAMBO itself is only a 2024 ICLR paper that's been validated on hyperparameter tuning, not on materials experiments (liu2024largelanguagemodels pages 1-2, liu2024largelanguagemodels pages 2-4). LILO has been deployed at Meta for A/B test optimization, not physical science (kobalczyk2025lilobayesianoptimization pages 9-10, kobalczyk2025lilobayesianoptimization pages 1-2). Present the LLM layer as *optional augmentation* of a disciplined BO core, not as mission-critical. (3) As a junior PI at BYU, you need senior credibility on the team. The JHU APL teaming is good if it's real — get that letter. ORNL MDF would be even better for the qualification narrative, given their established AMMTO funding history and digital thread expertise (kenny20242023peerreview pages 15-18). (4) Make the energy/decarbonization connection explicit. AMMTO's mission centers on manufacturing competitiveness and economy-wide decarbonization (kenny20242023peerreview pages 12-15). You need a paragraph explaining how lightweight structural Al alloys discovered via this platform reduce energy consumption in transportation/aerospace.

**Bottom line:** This is competitive enough to submit to Focus Area F, and I'd encourage you to do so. It's not a slam dunk — the distributed workflow, junior PI risk, and Al-only scope are vulnerabilities — but the combination of operational hardware + novel AI framework + qualification-grade testing is genuinely differentiated.

**3-week action items:** (1) Secure the ORNL MDF letter — even a short one about characterization support and digital thread collaboration. (2) Run a toy BOFN demo on your existing CALPHAD data to show a figure of the cost-aware node selection working. (3) Write a one-paragraph "pathway to qualification" story that ties your ASTM E8 tensile data to AM qualification frameworks.

## EMAIL 2: From a Senior SDL / Autonomous Experimentation Reviewer (Kusne/Maruyama/Abolhasani style)

**Subject: Re: Your FLAIME proposal — SDL assessment**

Hi —

I read through the FLAIME concept. Here's my honest take from an autonomous experimentation perspective.

**The BOFN-as-DAG idea is the gem.** This is where you have a genuinely novel contribution. Bayesian Optimization over Function Networks treats your powder-to-part pipeline as a directed acyclic graph where each node (CALPHAD → atomization → LPBF → characterization) has its own GP surrogate, and the cost-aware acquisition function (p-KGFN) selects which node to evaluate next based on expected improvement per unit cost (buathong2506fastbayesianoptimization pages 1-2, buathong2506fastbayesianoptimization pages 2-4, buathong2506fastbayesianoptimization pages 8-9). This is exactly the right formalism for your problem: CALPHAD screening costs effectively nothing (~$1/eval), while a full atomize-print-test loop costs orders of magnitude more. The recently published Fast p-KGFN algorithm even addresses the computational overhead concern (buathong2506fastbayesianoptimization pages 4-6, buathong2506fastbayesianoptimization pages 6-8). The connection to Torresi & Friederich's MSBO framework for multi-stage SDLs is also relevant — they show that merely modeling cascade structure (as in vanilla BOFN) can be insufficient and that proxy-aware sampling with inventory-based resumable evaluation significantly improves performance (torresi2512multistagebayesianoptimisation pages 12-14, torresi2512multistagebayesianoptimisation pages 2-4, torresi2512multistagebayesianoptimisation pages 1-2, torresi2512multistagebayesianoptimisation pages 4-7). You should cite MSBO and position your work relative to it.

**The LLM integration is interesting but scientifically risky.** Let me break down your five proposed LLM integration points. LLAMBO-style warmstarting is the most defensible: it's been validated empirically and doesn't require fine-tuning (liu2024largelanguagemodels pages 1-2, liu2024largelanguagemodels pages 4-6, liu2024largelanguagemodels pages 8-10). LLM-informed GP priors are plausible but untested in materials contexts. BORA-style stage selection is novel but you need to explain how you weight the LLM suggestion against p-KGFN's principled decision — if the LLM can override the acquisition function, you lose the BO guarantees. LILO-style natural language interpretation of intermediate outputs (powder quality reports, SEM images) is the most speculative: LILO was designed for human preference elicitation in online A/B tests (kobalczyk2025lilobayesianoptimization pages 1-2, kobalczyk2025lilobayesianoptimization pages 4-6), not for interpreting scientific characterization data. The RAG component is reasonable. My advice: rank these by TRL. Present LLAMBO warmstarting and RAG as things you *will* do in Year 1. Present the rest as research questions you'll investigate. Don't promise all five working in the first year.

**Throughput assessment: honest but not jaw-dropping.** 15 unique compositions per week with full-size tensile data is *respectable* for a bulk AM pipeline, but it's not going to compete with thin-film combinatorial SDLs doing thousands per day. LLNL's APEX platform reports up to 48 samples/day via DED (conn2025selfdrivinglabto pages 1-8), and HT-READ demonstrated 16 compositions per build (vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 7-11). However, none of those produce full-size ASTM E8 tensile bars — that's your real differentiator. Most high-throughput AM groups use hardness, nanoindentation, or miniature specimens, which are cheaper but less qualification-relevant (liu2024high‐throughputpreparationfor pages 13-13). The question reviewers will ask is: "Can you really sustain 15/week?" You need a detailed bottleneck analysis: how many hours per atomization run? How many builds per day at U of U? How many tensile bars per build? What's your SEM/EBSD queue time? Show the math.

**What would make this top-tier vs. mid-tier:**
- *Top-tier:* A preliminary BOFN demonstration where the cost-aware acquisition correctly chose to screen 50 CALPHAD compositions, promote 5 to atomization, and 2 to full printing — even on synthetic or retrospective data. That would be electrifying in a full proposal.
- *Top-tier:* A clear figure showing the BOFN DAG with measured costs per node and a cumulative regret comparison against random and standard BO.
- *Mid-tier:* Describing BOFN conceptually without any numerical demonstration.
- *Below threshold:* Promising all five LLM integration points as deliverables without pilot data.

**Benchmarks I'd want to see:** (1) Regret curves: BOFN vs. standard BO vs. random on a retrospective Al alloy dataset. (2) Cost efficiency: show how many full-loop evaluations BOFN saves by exploiting cheap CALPHAD screening. (3) GP model quality: cross-validation R² for each node's surrogate. (4) LLAMBO warmstart quality: compare LLM-proposed initial compositions against random and CALPHAD-only initialization.

**Submit?** Yes, but sharpen the AI story. The BOFN core is publishable-grade novelty. The LLM layer is your "reach" innovation. Don't let the reach overshadow the core.

## EMAIL 3: From a Senior AM / Alloy Design Reviewer (Rollett/Thoma/Pollock style)

**Subject: Re: FLAIME — alloy design and AM feasibility thoughts**

Hi —

Good to see someone actually building end-to-end hardware for this. Some thoughts.

**Is 15 compositions/week credible?** It depends entirely on what you mean by "composition." If each composition requires a separate atomization run (melting, atomizing, collecting, sieving, characterizing powder), plus a separate LPBF build (plate setup, parameter development, build, cool-down, removal), plus tensile specimen machining and testing — 15/week is aggressive. Here's my back-of-envelope: Ultrasonic atomization of a single Al composition might take 2–4 hours including setup and cleanup. At 18 hours/day × 6 days, you get roughly 25–50 atomization runs per week if nothing goes wrong. But some compositions will fail (poor melting, excessive oxidation, clogging). Call it 15–20 successful powder batches. Then at University of Utah, you need to fit those powders into LPBF builds — and LPBF parameter development for a *new* alloy composition isn't trivial. If you're using the same base parameters across similar Al compositions (which is reasonable for Al-Si variants), you can probably do 2–3 multi-specimen builds per day. Full-size ASTM E8 tensile bars need substantial build volume — maybe 6–10 bars per build plate. So 2–3 builds/day × 6 days × 6–10 bars = 72–180 tensile bars per week, but those represent maybe 10–15 distinct compositions if you print multiple bars per composition. It's tight but not impossible. The key assumption is that you're not doing extensive parameter development for each new composition — the CALPHAD pre-screening plus BOFN's cost-aware staging should filter out unprintable candidates before they reach the LPBF. Make this argument explicitly.

**Is full-size tensile a real advantage?** Absolutely yes. Nanoindentation and microhardness can screen relative trends, but they don't give you yield strength, UTS, elongation, or strain-hardening behavior — the quantities that matter for structural qualification. ORNL's data-driven LPBF qualification framework trained on 6,299 tensile specimens shows the kind of dataset that's needed (liu2024high‐throughputpreparationfor pages 13-13). Your 15 compositions/week × 50 weeks × 5–10 bars each = 3,750–7,500 tensile specimens over three years. That's a genuinely valuable dataset for the AM community, and it's the kind of thing AMMTO should be funding.

**Which Al alloy families should you prioritize?** My strong recommendation:

1. **Al-Ce systems (Al-Ce-Mn, Al-Ce-Ca-Mn):** This is where the most exciting recent work is. The Al-3.0Ce-0.8Ca-1.9Mn composition achieved YS 321 MPa, UTS 429 MPa, and 10.9% elongation via LPBF — an outstanding combination (wei2025compositionoptimizationand pages 14-15, wei2025compositionoptimizationand pages 2-4). Ce forms thermally stable Al₁₁Ce₃ intermetallics with excellent coarsening resistance, narrow freezing range for good printability, and low hot-cracking susceptibility (yakubov2022laserpowderbed pages 29-34, yakubov2022laserpowderbed pages 152-155, liu2024reviewoflaser pages 28-29). The CALPHAD landscape here is rich: Ca reduces eutectic temperature and hot-cracking tendency, while Mn provides precipitation strengthening via stable Al₆Mn (wei2025compositionoptimizationand pages 2-4). This is *the* system where your BOFN approach can shine — there's a large, unexplored ternary/quaternary composition space where CALPHAD screening at Node 0 can efficiently guide expensive downstream evaluation.

2. **Al-Cu-based (2xxx-type, possibly with Ti/Cr/Fe):** There's active work on ultrasonically atomized Al-Cu alloys for LPBF, and the wide freezing range of binary Al-Cu creates a real cracking challenge that your CALPHAD cracking indices can help navigate (liu2024reviewoflaser pages 15-17). Less mature than Al-Ce but high payoff if you crack the printability problem.

3. **Al-Si variants as controls/baselines:** AlSi10Mg and AlSi12 are well-characterized and your ultrasonic atomizer has demonstrated proof-of-concept on AlSi12 (yankin2025effectofultrasonic pages 1-2). Use these as your calibration system — the benchmark against which your novel Al-Ce and Al-Cu compositions are compared.

**What I'd avoid:** Trying to do Al-Mg, Al-Zn, *and* Al-Ce *and* Al-Cu in Phase I. That's too many families. Pick two novel systems (Al-Ce-X and Al-Cu-X) plus one baseline (Al-Si-X), and let the BOFN decide where within each family to explore. The depth of exploration matters more than breadth.

**Concerns:** (1) Ultrasonic atomization of Ce-containing alloys may have oxidation issues — Ce is highly reactive. You should mention this risk and your mitigation (inert atmosphere in the rePOWDER, post-sieving characterization). (2) The powder-to-LPBF transfer introduces variability: different powder batches, moisture pickup during transport to U of U, possible contamination. Document your powder handling protocol. (3) Make sure your CALPHAD Node 0 is using the right databases — the Al-Ce thermodynamic descriptions in standard databases may be incomplete for ternary/quaternary systems. State which Thermo-Calc database versions you'll use and whether you'll validate with DTA/DSC on selected compositions.

**Bottom line on scope and submission:** Al-only is fine for Phase I if you sell it correctly. Frame it as: "Aluminum is the largest-volume structural metal for LPBF, but the printable alloy space is tiny (basically AlSi10Mg). FLAIME will systematically expand the printable aluminum design space using AI-guided powder-to-part experimentation with qualification-grade mechanical testing." That's a compelling one-sentence pitch. Submit it. It's a credible concept.
