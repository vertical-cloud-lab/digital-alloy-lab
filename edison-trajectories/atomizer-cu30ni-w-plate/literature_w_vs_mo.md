# Tungsten versus molybdenum on the rePOWDER atomizing plate

**Verdict: tungsten is the better *metallic surface*, but a simply “tungsten-dipped” carbon–carbon plate is not a qualified solution for this 1.1 kg pour.** At the relevant Cu-rich composition, the measured equilibrium capacity of the melt for W is orders of magnitude below that for Mo. That substantially reduces the *dissolution* incentive, not the separate risks of an incompletely consolidated coating, ultrasonic stripping, carbide-related cracking, or W-rich particles in specification-sensitive powder. A dense, adherent, inspected W surface is worth a controlled trial; an unspecified dip coating is not. (eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 1-2, eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 2-4, teodorovich1980solubilityofmolybdenum pages 1-3, atay2019refractorymetalcoatings pages 75-79)

## 1. Melt compatibility: a particularly favorable comparison for W

Eremenko and colleagues measured W saturation in liquid Cu–Ni against solid W. Their 1300 °C, low-Ni fit is **log₁₀(xW) = −7.011 + 7.770√z**, where *xW* is the atomic fraction of W and *z* is Ni/(Ni + Cu) **in atomic fractions**; its tabulated range is *z* = 0.0539–0.368. For 70 wt% Cu/30 wt% Ni, *z* ≈ 0.317. Substitution gives *xW* ≈ 0.0023, or **approximately 0.66 wt% W in the saturated ternary liquid**. At 1350 °C the published coefficients, −6.867 and +7.580, give approximately **0.7 wt% W** by the same calculation. These are evaluations of fitted equilibrium data, **not measured pickup during a pour**; no 1250 °C coefficient is supplied, and the values cannot establish a seconds-scale dissolution rate. (eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 1-2, eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 2-4)

Teodorovich and colleagues measured **3.71 wt% Mo at 1300 °C** for a Cu–Ni melt with Ni/(Ni + Cu) = **0.320 by weight**—close, but not identical, to this feedstock. Their experiments required **3–6 h** to saturate; the W experiments required **7–9 h** for lower-Ni melts under their particular natural-convection geometry. Thus the defensible 1300 °C equilibrium comparison is approximately **0.66 wt% W versus 3.71 wt% Mo, or ~5–6-fold less W by mass**, not zero W dissolution. Neither set of hour-scale saturation times is a rate constant transferable to a vibrating, renewing film. (eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 1-2, teodorovich1980solubilityofmolybdenum pages 1-3)

The binary end members explain why neither pure-metal analogy suffices. W is extremely sparingly soluble in **liquid Cu**: Eremenko could not directly resolve that endpoint and assessed it by extrapolation. W uptake rises steeply with Ni content; W–Ni–Cu heavy alloys are deliberately made by liquid-phase sintering or infiltration, so solution and reprecipitation are real processes, not evidence of W inertness. Their study also reports precipitation of W-rich material when saturated melts cool. **Pure Ni is not a liquid at 1250–1350 °C** (its melting point is ~1455 °C), so a “liquid-Ni solubility at the run temperature” is not a meaningful equilibrium comparator. On the Mo side, Cu-rich liquid becomes appreciably more Mo-soluble as Ni is added; the measured ternary behavior cannot be replaced with a pure-Cu estimate. The Mo study notes reported liquid-state layering elsewhere in the Cu–Ni–Mo diagram above 1300 °C, but did not investigate that regime as a blanket prediction for this composition. It found no NiMo interfacial phase in its tested range after rapid cooling; Mo-containing powder need not therefore show visible NiMo to be contaminated. (eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 1-2, eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 2-4, teodorovich1980solubilityofmolybdenum pages 1-3, eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 4-4, teodorovich1980solubilityofmolybdenum pages 3-3)

**How much coating could the pour remove?** For a deliberately explicit illustration, assume a **1 cm²** wetted patch, a **100 µm** liquid film and liquid density **8 g/cm³**. That film weighs ~0.08 g. If it arrived W- or Mo-free and actually reached the above 1300 °C saturation value before leaving, its capacity would correspond to **~0.27 µm of W** or **~2.9 µm of Mo** uniformly removed from that 1 cm² patch. The ratio follows from *film mass × equilibrium mass fraction/(coating density × area)*, using 19.3 g/cm³ for W and 10.2 g/cm³ for Mo. For a **10–100 µm** film, scale both results by 0.1–1; for *n* independently refreshed, fully saturating films, multiply by *n*. This is a **capacity ceiling per assumed film**, *not* a forecast of erosion in seconds: the actual wetted area, film thickness, residence time, temperature, flow renewal and mass-transfer coefficient have not been supplied. Across the **entire 1.1 kg batch**, reaching those equilibrium concentrations would demand about **7 g W or 41 g Mo**, far more material than many thin plate coatings contain. Conversely, a **10 µm coating over 10 cm²** contains only ~0.193 g W or ~0.102 g Mo: complete loss would average ~0.018 wt% W or ~0.009 wt% Mo in 1.1 kg, while producing potentially much more damaging *local* inclusions. These are inventory bounds, not predicted pickup. Cavitation, film impact and thermal fatigue could detach coating directly; no measured erosion coefficient for this particular sonotrode/plate/melt combination was found. The reported **2.6 wt% Mo** in an ultrasonically atomized Ni-rich HEA made with a Mo sonotrode demonstrates that serious pickup *can* happen on an AMAZEMET rePOWDER, but is neither a rate measurement nor an expected value for this Cu-rich melt and carbon plate. (smid1998developmentoftungsten pages 3-4, eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 1-2, teodorovich1980solubilityofmolybdenum pages 1-3, goncharov2026designofcobaltfree pages 10-13, goncharov2026designofcobaltfree pages 2-4)

## 2. Wetting is useful only if the coating survives

There is **no defensible published contact angle located for this precise 70Cu–30Ni liquid on either W or Mo at 1260–1300 °C**. Comparators show why quoting one universal angle would mislead. Clean, oxygen-free **pure Cu on W** was measured at roughly **14–15° near 1220 °C** in one preparation, whereas a different oxygen-free Cu preparation showed markedly different angles; oxygen-bearing Cu could initially exceed 90°, and pre-oxidized W gave **140°** at 1145 °C. Just **41 ppm oxygen** inhibited wetting in these experiments. Pure Cu spreads on Mo, while added Ni reduced the angle on Mo in experiments with Cu containing **up to 10 wt% Ni**; extending that trend to **30 wt% Ni** is qualitative, not an angle measurement. Pure Cu on tested glassy carbon had an angle **above 120°**; graphite, glassy carbon and the proposed woven carbon–carbon are not interchangeable surfaces, and Ni's effect on this exact carbon surface remains unquantified. (nicholas1967theinfluenceof pages 2-4, nicholas1967theinfluenceof pages 1-2, nicholas1967theinfluenceof pages 4-5, minakova1988structureformationand pages 1-3, kitzmantel2008wettingandinfiltration pages 39-44)

The plate needs **reproducible contact and a suitably thin, replenished film** so vibration couples into liquid and ejects droplets. Neither complete dewetting and bouncing nor persistent, strongly adhered puddles and edge flooding are desirable; an equilibrium sessile-drop angle alone cannot optimize atomization. Ni can improve wetting of a clean refractory surface, but also raises W and Mo solubility relative to Cu alone. Oxide control, plate geometry, feed rate and observed film behavior matter as much as nominal surface chemistry. (teodorovich1980reactionsatphase pages 1-4, minakova1988structureformationand pages 1-3, nicholas1967theinfluenceof pages 2-4, eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 1-2)

## 3. Carbon interface, deposition and vibrating-plate mechanics

**“Dip in tungsten” cannot mean immersion in molten W:** W melts at ~3422 °C, versus ~2623 °C for Mo; either liquid metal would be an inappropriate way to coat this carbon plate. A practical dip would deposit a **W-powder slurry or paint plus binder**, followed by binder burnout and substantial consolidation; merely drying it leaves a particulate, permeable layer. Alternatives are **WF₆/H₂ chemical-vapor deposition**, sputtering/PVD, or controlled-atmosphere plasma spraying. Reported CVD W can be **gas-tight and >99.7% dense**; reported plasma-sprayed W on graphite includes **250–550 µm coatings with ~8–9% porosity**, while thin PVD/Mo/W stacks on carbon–carbon are on the order of **several to ~15 µm**. These are examples from different applications, not demonstrated rePOWDER specifications. Connected pores, pinholes and cracks offer pathways for hot metal to reach the carbon, regardless of nominal W thickness. A dense cross-section, leak/penetration assessment and bend/thermal-cycle inspection are consequently more meaningful acceptance criteria than a label saying “W-coated.” (edstrom1980literatureonfabrication pages 38-40, edstrom1980literatureonfabrication pages 40-41, atay2019refractorymetalcoatings pages 30-35, atay2019refractorymetalcoatings pages 35-39, rasinski2012thestructuraldegradation pages 18-22)

Neither metal is chemically inert to the substrate. In a documented carbon-fibre-composite/**4 µm Mo/12.7 µm W** stack, the Mo interlayer converted to **Mo₂C within 30 min at 1370 K (~1097 °C)**. W subsequently developed **W₂C, then WC**: at **1620 K (~1347 °C)**, their combined layer was ~**3.8 µm after 30 min** and **>12 µm after 20 h**. This shows that a high-temperature slurry-sintering step may damage the interface *before* atomization, and that a hot exposed carbon/W interface can carburize. It does **not** establish that several micrometres form during a seconds-long pour: those tests involved continuous solid-state heating and a particular Mo interlayer. A carbide may initially assist chemical attachment, but a growing continuous brittle layer and carbon-fibre anisotropy promote cracking or delamination; in the cited stack scratch-failure load fell with annealing. (atay2019refractorymetalcoatings pages 30-35, rasinski2012thestructuraldegradation pages 55-62, atay2019refractorymetalcoatings pages 35-39)

W's expansion coefficient is approximately **4.5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹**, versus the approximately **5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹** Mo value in the question. This modest W–Mo difference is **not** proof of a matched carbon–carbon interface: that composite's expansion depends on fibre orientation and direction. Both coatings experience steep temperature gradients, high-frequency cyclic strain and stress concentrations at pores and carbides. W is especially vulnerable to brittle cracking when cold or coarse-grained; reported plasma-sprayed W ductile–brittle transition temperatures are around **100–200 °C**, and CVD W need not be more ductile than wrought W. Published W coatings on carbon-fibre composites have indeed cracked and delaminated during thermal exposure; this is a credible risk under ultrasound, though those tests do not quantify atomizer fatigue life. (smid1998developmentoftungsten pages 3-4, rasinski2012thestructuraldegradation pages 18-22, atay2019refractorymetalcoatings pages 30-35, edstrom1980literatureonfabrication pages 40-41)

For equal coated area and thickness, **W adds 19.3/10.2 ≈ 1.89 times Mo's mass**: at **10 µm**, approximately **19.3 versus 10.2 mg/cm²**. The first-order frequency sensitivity for a small added *modal* mass is **Δf/f ≈ −½Δm_modal/m_modal**; a numerical frequency shift requires the actual plate, bond, coating footprint, mode shape and sonotrode assembly. A thicker W layer also changes stiffness and damping, so mass-only scaling is not a complete resonance model. The **1:1.5 booster and 100% amplitude do not establish safe stress or retained amplitude** after changing the tip; verify assembled resonance and vibration amplitude after installation and heating. (smid1998developmentoftungsten pages 3-4, atay2019refractorymetalcoatings pages 30-35)

## 4. Oxidation, powder quality and credible alternatives

**MoO₃ is generally more volatile than WO₃** under comparable dry oxidizing high-temperature conditions, a modest advantage for W—not immunity. Neither oxide supplies a reliably protective film here; oxygen or water ingress also changes wetting, and humidity can drive volatile W oxyhydroxides. The pure-Cu/W experiment's oxygen sensitivity illustrates why “argon” alone does not demonstrate an oxide-free interface. Log oxygen and moisture exposure through startup, pour and cooldown rather than treating the nominal shielding gas as a guarantee. (meschter2013watervapor–mediatedvolatilization pages 11-13, edstrom1980literatureonfabrication pages 19-21, nicholas1967theinfluenceof pages 2-4)

For a **UNS C71500-type** 70/30 marine alloy, W and Mo are **not intentional constituents** in the cited nominal composition, which includes roughly 30% Ni and controlled Fe/Mn. Do **not** invent a universal allowable W or Mo ppm limit: qualification depends on the applicable product/procurement specification, and a powder may have additional cleanliness requirements. Lower average dissolved W would favor the W surface, but detached **high-density W/WC particles** may remain discrete defects that a batch-average ICP result misses; Mo can similarly contaminate as dissolved element or debris. Assess time-resolved powder fractions with bulk W/Mo and O/C analyses, SEM–EDS on anomalous particles and polished cross-sections, plus pre/post plate microscopy and mass. Stop the run on new surface loss or increasing refractory pickup. (mccright1988anannotatedhistory pages 58-60, eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 2-4, goncharov2026designofcobaltfree pages 10-13, rasinski2012thestructuraldegradation pages 55-62)

A **replaceable solid wrought W plate/tip** avoids coating porosity and a carbon/metal bond at the wetted face, but increases inertial load and may crack; a **solid Mo plate or TZM** is mechanically more familiar but retains the documented Mo-dissolution exposure. **W-coated Mo** removes carbon-interface exposure only while its W barrier remains intact; pinholes then expose Mo. **BN, alumina and zirconia** may avoid W/Mo alloy contamination, and **SiC** presents another potentially robust surface, but wetting, thermal shock, bonding to this vibrating assembly, and B/O/Si/C pickup all require qualification. The BN-coated crucible and BN stopper demonstrate *use in this melt-handling setup*, **not** that BN will work as the driven atomizing face. The retrieved rePOWDER study documents a Mo sonotrode and its contamination, not a successful head-to-head ultrasonic atomization trial of these ceramic or solid-W alternatives. (goncharov2026designofcobaltfree pages 10-13, goncharov2026designofcobaltfree pages 2-4, atay2019refractorymetalcoatings pages 75-79, edstrom1980literatureonfabrication pages 38-40)

**Decision:** choose **W over Mo only if it is a demonstrably dense, adherent, appropriately mass-matched and replaceable/inspectable surface**. Do not approve an uncharacterized tungsten slurry “dip” for the full 1.1 kg production run merely because W melts higher or has the lower equilibrium solubility. Begin with a short, sacrificial Cu–Ni trial and a predeclared powder-cleanliness and plate-integrity stop criterion. (eremenko1977solubilityoftungsten pages 1-2, teodorovich1980solubilityofmolybdenum pages 1-3, atay2019refractorymetalcoatings pages 75-79, goncharov2026designofcobaltfree pages 10-13)
