# Equipment and providers for mechanical testing of metals

**Scope.** The ranges below are *demonstrated test conditions* unless identified as an equipment rating. A furnace’s rated temperature is not necessarily a validated specimen temperature, and an actuator speed is not itself a specimen strain rate. Published methods often name the frame and heating supplier but not the maker of a custom Hopkinson bar or gas gun. (arnold2026developmentcharacterizationand pages 32-34, bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 3-5, mates2024onthedynamic pages 4-5)

## 1. Elevated-temperature tension, fatigue and strain measurement

| Heating approach | Documented equipment and use | Temperature-control consideration |
|---|---|---|
| **Split-tube resistance furnace** | An **Applied Test Systems (ATS)** three-zone split-tube furnace, **Watlow PM** controller and **Instron 5969** frame were paired with an **Epsilon 7650A** high-temperature extensometer. Three Type-K thermocouples were welded to the specimen gauge with a **HotSpot II** welder and read using an **Omega CSi32**. Separately, **ATS 2961** three-zone split furnaces on creep frames are rated to **1,200 °C**. | Control and report *gauge* temperature, not just furnace set point; several thermocouples detect axial gradients. The cited furnace rating is not a claim that every load train and extensometer works at 1,200 °C. (pellicotte2025acceleratedmechanicaltests pages 55-59, natesan2012creeprupturetesting pages 9-13) |
| **Induction** | An **Ambrell EasyHeat** heater, **Yokogawa UT55A** control, **Metris M322** two-colour pyrometer and **FLIR A700** IR camera heated GRX-810 specimens loaded in an **Instron 6800-TM**. A coil designed using COMSOL produced an approximately **±1% gauge-temperature gradient**; tension was reported at **982, 1,093 and 1,204 °C**. | Fast, localized heating preserves optical access, but coil design, emissivity, surface-to-core gradients and measured axial uniformity matter. The ±1% is a result for this particular coil and geometry, not a generic induction specification. (pellicotte2025acceleratedmechanicaltests pages 108-113, bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 17-20) |
| **Radiant/infrared** | NASA describes **quartz-lamp heating** alongside resistance furnaces and induction. Its illustrated **Instron** furnace-equipped axial rig is capable of **1,400 °C**, while its **MTS** environmental-chamber rig accommodates vacuum or inert gas. Radiant heating should not be confused with an IR *camera*, which measures temperature rather than heating the specimen. | Establish the temperature distribution on the actual loaded specimen and assess reflected light/thermal radiation in optical strain measurements. (arnold2026developmentcharacterizationand pages 32-34, pellicotte2025acceleratedmechanicaltests pages 108-113) |
| **Direct specimen resistance/Joule heating** | **Dynamic Systems Inc. Gleeble 3800** grips pass current through the specimen. A reported Inconel 718 arrangement used a welded central Type-K control thermocouple plus thermocouples **15 mm either side**, vacuum below **0.5 torr**, and stereo DIC/IR observation to **850 °C**. **NIST** separately demonstrated direct-current pulse heating *inside a Kolsky-bar assembly* at up to **1,000 °C s⁻¹**, before impact—not a Gleeble test. | Water-cooled grips make the middle hotter than the ends; uniformity must be verified across the intended gauge. A Gleeble thermocouple’s position/attachment, and the difference between instrument temperature resolution and thermocouple accuracy, must be reported. NIST rejected runs indicating poor thermocouple attachment or inconsistent pyrometer/thermocouple readings. (blackwell2024hightemperaturemechanicalproperty pages 54-59, kuprienko2019developmentofphysicsbased pages 32-37, mates2024onthedynamic pages 4-5) |

**Extensometry.** Contact high-temperature extensometers use ceramic/quartz gauge-contact elements or knife edges to transmit movement out of the hot zone: the documented **Epsilon 7650A** has a **1-inch gauge**, up to **10% measured strain**, and is reported as ASTM E83 Class A. Another tensile setup used ceramic knife edges entering a **Severn Thermal Solutions** furnace on an **Instron 3369**; an **Instron 8862** arrangement used an **Imetrum video extensometer**. These are *strain-measurement choices*, not interchangeable temperature ratings. Laser interferometric/speckle-based optical gauges and video methods avoid hot contact but must handle window distortion, glowing surfaces, convection and stable fiducial features; the retrieved historical optical review principally examines ceramic composites, so it is not evidence that its limiting temperatures were demonstrated on metals. (pellicotte2025acceleratedmechanicaltests pages 55-59, tammpere2024profilometry‐basedindentationplastometry pages 2-2, hemann1994ahightemperature pages 10-19)

**High-temperature DIC.** One documented system combined **Correlated Solutions IR 3D-DIC**, two **Basler** cameras, a **FLIR** thermal camera, blue illumination/bandpass filters and heat-resistant iron-oxide/alumina-oxide speckles for induction-heated GRX-810. A separate Gleeble study maintained stereo DIC at **850 °C** using blue light and narrow-band filters. DIC maps strain localization rather than only average gauge strain; camera calibration, out-of-plane motion, thermal shimmer, speckle survival and distinguishing specimen temperature from camera-observed radiance require explicit checks. (pellicotte2025acceleratedmechanicaltests pages 108-113, blackwell2024hightemperaturemechanicalproperty pages 54-59, arnold2026developmentcharacterizationand pages 32-34)

## 2. Creep and stress rupture

Long-duration tensile creep usually employs **dead weights and a lever arm**, a controlled multi-zone furnace, thermocouples near the gauge and an LVDT or extensometer; rupture tests run until specimen failure. An **Argonne/Idaho** report documents **four ATS 2140 direct-load frames** (rated **500 lbf**) and **two ATS 2330 20:1 lever-arm frames** (rated **10,000 lbf**). Its Alloy 617 tests covered **750–1,000 °C, 12–135 MPa**, while A508/533 tests covered **350–390 °C, 414–483 MPa**; the ATS 2961 furnace rating was **1,200 °C**. Another Argonne report identifies **ATS Series 2300** 20:1 lever-arm frames and split-tube furnaces rated **1,100 °C**. A commercial precedent is **Metcut Research** testing with **SATEC Model LD** 5:1 lever-arm creep-rupture machines. These reports do **not** establish that all stated furnace ratings were used in every creep experiment. (natesan2012creeprupturetesting pages 9-13, zhang2021fy21statusreport pages 16-19, fingers1998creepbehaviorof pages 47-52)

**Throughput means concurrent specimens, not shortened creep physics.** Argonne commissioned **nine lever-arm frames** and ran nine Alloy 709 specimens concurrently at **725 °C/140 MPa**; an earlier program reported **22 tests loaded and 10 completed**, not 22 simultaneous positions. In a different approach, an **IBERTEST-50** frame loaded **four miniature 316L specimens in series** at **650 °C**, with different sections creating different stresses and independent camera/DIC readouts; the authors describe approximately **fourfold specimen throughput** versus successive single-specimen tests, subject to pauses to remove failures. Series-loaded specimens are coupled mechanically, so that arrangement should not be represented as four independently controlled creep stations. The retrieved study supplies no verified DOI. (zhang2023reportoncapability pages 9-14, zhang2021fy21statusreport pages 16-19, singh2026highthroughputcharacterizationof pages 58-63, singh2026highthroughputcharacterizationof pages 37-42)

## 3. High strain rate, impact and non-tensile loading

| Apparatus | Makers/models actually identified and useful range | What is measured or limited |
|---|---|---|
| **High-speed servo-hydraulic** | The methods review names **Instron, MTS, Shimadzu and Zwick/Roell**. It reports an **MTS 819** hybrid arrangement with a **16 m s⁻¹** maximum machine speed and experiments near **10² s⁻¹**. Conventional frames generally serve below roughly **10 s⁻¹**, whereas intermediate-rate techniques target approximately **10–1,000 s⁻¹**. | Tension, compression, bending or shear with suitable fixtures. A slack adapter lets the actuator accelerate before engagement but introduces stress-wave ringing; specimen-mounted/elastic-bar load sensing and equilibrium checks can improve data. Machine speed alone does not specify achievable specimen rate. *Bhujangrao et al.*, **DOI 10.3390/met10070894**. (bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 3-5, bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 5-7, cox2013developmentofhigh pages 36-39) |
| **Drop tower / drop-bar hybrids** | A documented “**Dropkinson**” hybrid attained up to **600 s⁻¹** and about **0.27 strain** in the cited configuration; instrumentation combines impact actuation with an instrumented bar. | Useful for compression/impact and, with a special fixture, tension; pulse duration and oscillatory impact force constrain usable data. The reviewed configuration is **not** evidence of a particular commercial drop-tower manufacturer. *Bhujangrao et al.*, **DOI 10.3390/met10070894**. (bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 5-7) |
| **Split Hopkinson/Kolsky bars** | Typically laboratory-built bars, striker launcher, pulse shaper, strain gauges and high-speed acquisition; compression commonly covers **>10³ s⁻¹**, with tensile and torsional variants requiring different fixtures. **NIST** used twin **1.5 m × 15 mm maraging-steel bars**, a **375 mm striker** at **9.5–13.5 m s⁻¹**, copper pulse shapers and direct-current heating; two additional tests were near **790–1,000 s⁻¹**. | Reflected/transmitted elastic waves yield specimen stress, strain and rate after dynamic equilibrium; NIST’s heated steel tests reached **1,000 °C** after approximately **3 s** heating. Its described apparatus is a research installation, **not** an Instron- or MTS-manufactured bar. *Mates et al.*, **DOI 10.1007/s40870-024-00413-1**. (cox2013developmentofhigh pages 32-36, mates2024onthedynamic pages 2-4, mates2024onthedynamic pages 4-5) |
| **Gas guns** | A gas launcher can propel the **striker of a Hopkinson bar** or, in a **separate ballistic experiment**, a projectile at a metal panel. These are distinct experiments, despite both being called “gas-gun tests.” | The retrieved sources do not reliably name a commercial *maker* for the specific metal-test launchers or justify assigning a universal projectile velocity/metal strain rate; maker identification should therefore remain **custom/not reported** rather than guessed. (cox2013developmentofhigh pages 32-36, mates2024onthedynamic pages 4-5) |

## 4. Fatigue and fracture

**Servo-hydraulic MTS and Instron frames** provide force-, displacement- or strain-controlled axial fatigue, creep–fatigue holds and fracture/crack-growth loading; **electrodynamic Instron ElectroPuls E3000** is explicitly documented for fatigue crack-growth testing of electrical-steel laminates. NASA’s account also identifies electromechanical **Instron** and servo-hydraulic **MTS** load frames for cyclic tests and examples of resistance, induction and quartz-lamp specimen heating. Precracked specimens and crack-length monitoring are needed for fracture-mechanics quantities; smooth-bar S–N results are not crack-growth measurements. (arnold2026developmentcharacterizationand pages 32-34, jataUnknownyearairforceresearch pages 3-5)

At higher cycling frequencies, **RUMUL Testronic** resonant machines were used at **95 Hz** in a steel comparison; **BOKU Vienna** ultrasonic equipment ran at **20 kHz**, and **Shimadzu USF-2000** is another specifically named **20-kHz** metal-fatigue machine. At 20 kHz, **10⁹ cycles require 13.9 hours of uninterrupted cycling**, versus **579 days at 20 Hz** (calculated as cycles/frequency). This is potential cycle throughput, **not** proof of equivalence: strain-rate/frequency sensitivity and self-heating must be assessed. One ultrasonic steel study used pulse–pause operation, compressed-air cooling and IR monitoring to keep the specimen at **20 ± 5 °C**. *Krupp and Giertler*, **DOI 10.3390/met12111815**; *Yamaura et al.*, **DOI 10.4236/ojmetal.2014.43007**; *Seleznev et al.*, **DOI 10.1111/ffe.13252**. (yamaura2014ultrasonicfatigueof pages 1-3, krupp2022surfaceorinternal pages 2-5, seleznev2020ontheformation pages 3-3)

## 5. Small specimens and indentation

* **Small punch:** Clamp an approximately **9.5-mm-diameter, 0.5-mm-thick disc** between dies and press its centre with a hemispherical punch; use a furnace, near-disc thermocouples, inert atmosphere and displacement transducers for creep. **Swansea University/Swansea Materials Research & Testing** built a weighted-load rig with a **2-mm alumina punch** and demonstrated CM247LC creep tests at **950 ± 1 °C, 150 N** under argon. This is an **in-house** rig, not a verified off-the-shelf Swansea-manufactured instrument. Small-punch load/deflection is not automatically a conventional uniaxial stress–strain or creep curve. *Hilal et al.*, **DOI 10.3390/ma12091390**. (hilal2019theinfluenceof pages 3-5)
* **High-temperature nanoindentation:** **Bruker Hysitron TI980 with xSOL 800** hot stage was used on iron through **500 °C**, with heated sample/tip surroundings, **95% N₂/5% H₂** forming gas, an oxidation barrier at elevated temperature and drift control. Tests used up to **250 mN** and repeated **6–9 indents per condition**; the *tested* upper temperature is 500 °C, not the “800” in the stage name. *Khvan et al.*, **DOI 10.1016/j.jnucmat.2022.153815**. (khvan2022hightemperaturenanoindentation pages 2-5, khvan2022hightemperaturenanoindentation pages 5-6)
* **Profilometry-based indentation plastometry (PIP):** **Plastometrex** indents with a relatively large spherical ball, profiles the *residual* imprint and inversely fits finite-element simulations to estimate a metallic stress–strain curve; it is neither Berkovich nanoindentation nor a direct tensile/creep test. Commercial high-temperature PIP was demonstrated to **800 °C**, with approximately **10 min heat-up, 20 s indentation and 20 min cooling**. The hot-block thermocouple may differ from the specimen surface by **tens of degrees, occasionally 100 °C**, necessitating a material/geometry-specific thermal correction; oxidation and creep can bias inferred curves. *Clyne et al.*, **DOI 10.1002/adem.202100437**; *Tammpere et al.*, **DOI 10.1002/adem.202301073**. (clyne2024profilometrybasedindentationplastometry pages 1-3, clyne2024profilometrybasedindentationplastometry pages 19-23, tammpere2024profilometry‐basedindentationplastometry pages 8-8, tammpere2024profilometry‐basedindentationplastometry pages 1-2)

## 6. Documented facilities, economics and evidence limits

**NASA Glenn** explicitly reports tension, creep, fatigue and crack-growth tests on ME3 disk alloy: tensile **75–1,500 °F**, creep **1,200–1,500 °F**, low-cycle fatigue **75–1,400 °F** and cyclic crack growth **75–1,500 °F**. A later **NASA Glenn–Metcut Research** GRX-810 campaign reports tension through **1,093 °C** and creep rupture at **1,093 °C**. **NASA Lewis** (the historical predecessor of Glenn), not Langley, documented ±20-kip uniaxial, tension–torsion biaxial, induction-heated and quartz-lamp systems; an example thermomechanical cycle spanned **395–606 °C**. *Gradl et al.*, **DOI 10.2514/6.2024-0997**; NASA elevated-temperature methods report **DOI 10.64631/fyfj1499**. (gabb2002characterizationofthe pages 5-7, gradl2024extremetemperatureadditively pages 4-8, thompson1986hightemperaturestressstrain pages 1-3, arnold2026developmentcharacterizationand pages 32-34)

**AFRL:** A documented AFRL Turbine Engine Fatigue Facility-associated Ti-6242Si tensile program covered **23–650 °C** at **8.33 × 10⁻⁵ and 10⁻² s⁻¹**, at least **three replicates per condition**; this is evidence for the *reported experimental campaign*, not a comprehensive AFRL machine inventory. *Gockel et al.*, **DOI 10.1186/s40192-016-0057-0**. **NIST** has the pulse-heated Kolsky-bar steel demonstration described above. **Argonne/Idaho** document ATS creep frames, while **Swansea Materials Research & Testing**, **Metcut**, **Westmoreland Mechanical Testing & Research** and **Element Materials Technology** appear as participating commercial/specialist testing organizations in retrieved methods. **Southwest Research Institute (SwRI)** documents a ten-specimen DMLS Inconel 738LC creep/tensile investigation, but its retrieved report does **not** name the load-frame manufacturer. (natesan2012creeprupturetesting pages 9-13, zhang2023reportoncapability pages 9-14, fingers1998creepbehaviorof pages 47-52, mates2024onthedynamic pages 2-4, gradl2024extremetemperatureadditively pages 4-8, hilal2019theinfluenceof pages 3-5, wilkes2018creepandtensile pages 5-8, wilkes2018creepandtensile pages 1-3)

**Langley qualification and costs:** NASA report boilerplate stating that the agency’s scientific-information office was operated by **Langley** does *not* establish that Glenn’s mechanical tests took place at Langley. The retrieved evidence is insufficient to give a defensible Langley elevated-temperature-metal-test-machine inventory or a rate/temperature envelope; it should not be conflated with Glenn. Likewise, no substantiated purchase price, contract-laboratory per-test tariff or full cost-per-data-point comparison was found. The SwRI study does report approximately **US$1,000 to produce each printed Inconel 738LC specimen**—a *specimen manufacturing cost*, **not** a creep-test fee—and explains why its program restricted test count. Concurrent creep frames, four-in-series miniatures, rapid PIP and 20-kHz cycling improve different notions of throughput and cannot be compared as though they deliver identical qualification data. (gabb2002characterizationofthe pages 1-5, wilkes2018creepandtensile pages 1-3, zhang2023reportoncapability pages 9-14, singh2026highthroughputcharacterizationof pages 58-63, tammpere2024profilometry‐basedindentationplastometry pages 1-2, yamaura2014ultrasonicfatigueof pages 1-3)
