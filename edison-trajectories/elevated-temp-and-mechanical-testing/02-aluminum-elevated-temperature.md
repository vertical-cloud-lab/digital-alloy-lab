# Elevated-Temperature Mechanical Behavior of Aluminum Alloys for Aerospace: A Review with Quantitative Data

## 1. Introduction and Scope

Commercial cast and wrought aluminum alloys cannot generally be used for sustained structural service above approximately 200–250 °C because their strengthening precipitates coarsen and dissolve, creating a technological gap currently filled by ferrous, titanium, and nickel-base alloys (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 1-3, michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 3-4). This review synthesizes quantitative data on wrought, cast, and additively manufactured (AM) aluminum alloys at elevated temperature, explains the underlying metallurgical mechanisms, and identifies specific data gaps relevant to the BYU Vertical Cloud Lab's alloy-design and testing program.

## 2. Wrought Alloys: Elevated-Temperature Properties

### 2.1 2219-T61 (Al–Cu)
The ORNL DuAlumin-3D CRADA report (ORNL/TM-2024/3244, DOI: 10.2172/2301648) provides a direct comparison of wrought 2219-T61 against the AM DuAlumin-3D alloy, tested per ASTM E21 at RT, 93, 204, 343, and 398 °C. Reading from the published figures: 2219-T61 yield strength is approximately 275 MPa at RT, ~255 MPa at 93 °C, ~230 MPa at 204 °C, dropping sharply to ~55 MPa at 343 °C and ~25 MPa at 398 °C. The ultimate tensile strength follows a similar steep decline, while elongation increases from ~12% at RT to >60% at 398 °C (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 9-12, plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 12-17, plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum media 9fae2a72, plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum media 59dd48dd). This dramatic loss above ~200 °C exemplifies θ′-Al₂Cu precipitate coarsening and dissolution. Note: the figure caption erroneously labels the benchmark as "2618-T61," but the report text explicitly identifies it as 2219-T61 (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum media 9fae2a72).

### 2.2 2618/2618A (RR58, Concorde)
Alloy 2618-T6 was selected for the Concorde airframe structure (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 1-9). Its principal strengthening precipitate is S-Al₂CuMg, with Fe–Ni intermetallics (Al₉FeNi, Al₇Cu₂(Fe,Ni)) providing additional thermal stability. While temperature-resolved yield-strength tables were not recovered in this search, 2618 is traditionally rated for sustained service at approximately 150–200 °C, with MMPDS providing design allowables. The ORNL AM Al-Ce-Mn study reports that its alloy has yield strength "similar to 2618 up to 400 °C" after thermal exposure, implying that 2618 retains approximately 150–200 MPa at 200 °C but degrades substantially by 300 °C (plotkowski2020microstructureandproperties pages 4-6). **This claim requires verification against MMPDS data, which were not directly accessed here.**

### 2.3 8009 (Al–8.5Fe–1.3V–1.7Si, FVS0812)
The dispersion-strengthened PM alloy 8009 contains 27 vol.% ultrafine Al₁₂(Fe,V)₃Si dispersoids (~47 nm average radius) that provide exceptional elevated-temperature creep resistance. Peng et al. (DOI: 10.1016/S0921-5093(98)00876-4) measured compression creep at 300–450 °C and found apparent stress exponents of 14–18 and an apparent activation energy of 296 kJ/mol. After threshold-stress correction, the true stress exponent is ~5 (dislocation climb). The threshold stresses are: **112 MPa at 300 °C (573 K), 95 MPa at 350 °C (623 K), 70 MPa at 400 °C (673 K), and 55 MPa at 450 °C (723 K)** (peng1999hightemperaturecreep pages 1-2, peng1999hightemperaturecreep pages 4-7). Kennedy et al. (NASA CR-187614, 1991) showed that FVS0812 sheet tensile properties were essentially unchanged after 504 h at 400 °C, with only ~10% reduction after 120 h at 510 °C (kennedy1991effectofthermal pages 21-23). A ductility trough between ~100–275 °C due to dynamic strain aging limits formability in that range (kennedy1991effectofthermal pages 63-69).

### 2.4 Other Wrought Alloys
For 7xxx alloys (7075, 7050), η′-MgZn₂ precipitates coarsen and dissolve rapidly above ~120–150 °C, making these alloys unsuitable for sustained elevated-temperature service. For 2024-T3/T4, S′/S-phase coarsening limits service to ~150 °C. For 6061/6013, β″/β′ Mg₂Si precipitates overage above ~150–175 °C. **Quantitative temperature-resolved data for these alloys were not directly recovered in this search; the reader is referred to MMPDS-17/MMPDS-18 (formerly MIL-HDBK-5) for design allowables** (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 1-3, michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 3-4).

## 3. Why Precipitation-Hardened Alloys Lose Strength Above 150–200 °C

The strength of age-hardened aluminum alloys derives from a high number density of nanoscale, coherent or semi-coherent precipitates that impede dislocation motion by shearing or Orowan looping (roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 4-6). Above approximately 150–250 °C (depending on alloy system), several degradation mechanisms accelerate:

**θ′ coarsening in Al–Cu alloys:** Enhanced Cu diffusion at elevated temperature drives Ostwald ripening of metastable θ′-Al₂Cu plates. As larger precipitates grow at the expense of smaller ones, the number density falls, interparticle spacing increases, and the aspect ratio decreases—all reducing strengthening. Eventually θ′ transforms to the equilibrium θ phase, which is fully incoherent and an ineffective obstacle to dislocations (roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 8-10, shower2019onthethermal pages 24-29, bahl2021agingbehaviorand pages 1-2, roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 4-6). In conventional Al–Cu alloys, this begins rapidly above ~225 °C (shower2019onthethermal pages 24-29, shower2019onthethermala pages 24-29).

**S-phase coarsening in Al–Cu–Mg:** In 2024 and 2618, S-Al₂CuMg precipitates overage similarly, though the Fe–Ni intermetallics in 2618 provide some additional stability (roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 8-10).

**η′ dissolution in 7xxx:** MgZn₂-based precipitates are thermodynamically unstable at relatively low temperatures and dissolve/coarsen above ~120–150 °C (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 1-3).

### 3.1 Stabilizing Additions

**Mn and Zr in ACMZ alloys:** Mn segregates rapidly to coherent and semi-coherent θ′/α-Al interfaces, reducing interfacial energy. Slower-diffusing Zr subsequently segregates to coherent interfaces, providing additional solute drag and impeding the θ′→θ transformation. In the ACMZ alloy, θ′ precipitates showed **no measurable coarsening at 300 °C for 5,000 hours**, and hardness remained essentially stable (bahl2021agingbehaviorand pages 1-2). Zr can also form L1₂-Al₃Zr dispersoids that nucleate at θ′ interfaces, further stabilizing the microstructure to ~350 °C (roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 8-10, roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 24-26).

**Sc and Zr (L1₂ Al₃(Sc,Zr)):** In Scalmalloy-type alloys, coherent L1₂ precipitates with Sc-rich cores and Zr-rich shells resist coarsening due to low interfacial energy and slow Zr diffusion. LPBF processing creates supersaturated solid solutions that precipitate an unusually high number density of nanoscale Al₃(Sc,Zr) during aging at 300–375 °C (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 6-7, cerri2024mainheattreatments pages 7-11, cerri2024mainheattreatments pages 5-7). However, prolonged exposure at 325 °C already produces measurable coarsening (cerri2024mainheattreatments pages 7-11).

**Ag additions (Ω phase):** In Al–Cu–Mg–Ag alloys (2139, A205/A20X), Ag promotes the formation of Ω-phase plates on {111}α planes, which provide strong planar obstacles with better coarsening resistance than θ′ alone. However, service above ~200–250 °C still leads to overaging (roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 8-10, shakil2021microstructureandsmallscale pages 72-77).

**Ce (Al₁₁Ce₃):** Cerium has extremely low solubility and diffusivity in α-Al, forming a thermally stable eutectic Al₁₁Ce₃ intermetallic network. Cast Al–Ce alloys retain >60% of RT yield strength at 300 °C and show complete mechanical-property recovery after 500 °C/1,000 h exposure (sims2017highperformancealuminum–cerium pages 1-3).

**Fe, V, Si dispersoids:** In 8009, the 27–36 vol.% Al₁₂(Fe,V)₃Si dispersoids have a very low coarsening rate and provide creep threshold stresses of 55–112 MPa from 450 to 300 °C (peng1999hightemperaturecreep pages 1-2, peng1999hightemperaturecreep pages 4-7).

## 4. Cast Alloys

### 4.1 ACMZ (Al–Cu–Mn–Zr)
The ORNL-developed ACMZ family was designed to replace Al–Si–Cu alloys (such as 319) in high-temperature applications like cylinder heads. Cast ACMZ with ~8 wt.% Cu exhibits a peak-aged RT UTS of ~300 MPa. After prolonged exposure at 300 °C, ACMZ retains nearly twice the UTS and yield strength of a base Al–Cu alloy (roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 16-19). Its hardness remains essentially stable through 5,000 h at 300 °C due to Mn/Zr interfacial segregation stabilizing θ′ (bahl2021agingbehaviorand pages 1-2). Compressive yield strengths at 300 °C after T7A stabilization range from 122 MPa (0 Mg) to 142 MPa (0.24 wt.% Mg) (hu2023elevatedtemperaturemechanicalcreepa pages 54-60). Creep at 300 °C shows a stress exponent near unity at low stress (diffusional/Coble creep) transitioning to dislocation creep at higher stress, where grain-boundary precipitates impede vacancy movement (roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 19-21).

### 4.2 Cast Al–Ce Alloys
Binary and ternary Al–Ce alloys show remarkable thermal stability due to the Al₁₁Ce₃ intermetallic. Sims et al. (DOI: 10.1039/C7MH00391A) reported RT yield strength of ~320 MPa and UTS of ~400 MPa for extruded Al–Ce, with ~80% mechanical-property retention at 240 °C and >60% yield-strength retention at 300 °C (sims2017highperformancealuminum–cerium pages 1-3). Weiss (DOI: 10.1007/s11665-019-3884-2) showed complete RT property recovery after 500 °C/1,000 h exposure in some Al–Ce compositions.

### 4.3 Conventional Cast Alloys (319, A356, 242, 4032)
These Si-containing alloys are the current industry standard for engine components. Cast 319 (Al–Si–Cu) is widely used but loses strength above ~250 °C. ACMZ alloys were specifically developed to outperform 319 at elevated temperature, and corrosion studies show ACMZ alloys to be more resistant than 319 due to the absence of deleterious Si particles (roy2022newagealcumnzr(acmz) pages 19-21). **Quantitative temperature-resolved data for 319, A356, 242, and 4032 were not directly recovered in this search; the reader is referred to MMPDS and ASM Handbook Vol. 2.**

## 5. Additively Manufactured Aluminum Alloys

### 5.1 Overview
The comprehensive ORNL review by Michi et al. (DOI: 10.1080/09506608.2021.1951580) categorizes potential high-temperature AM Al alloys into three classes: (i) high-temperature precipitation-strengthened alloys (HTPSAs), such as Scalmalloy and Addalloy; (ii) high-intermetallic-fraction (HiFI) alloys, such as Al–Ce, Al–Ce–Ni–Mn, and Al–Fe–V–Si derivatives; and (iii) ceramic dispersion alloys (CDAs). The review concludes that **evaluations of high-temperature mechanical properties and corrosion responses are severely deficient** across all three categories (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 1-3).

### 5.2 LPBF AlSi10Mg
The most widely studied AM Al alloy, AlSi10Mg relies on a fine eutectic Si cellular network for strengthening. However, Si has relatively high diffusivity in α-Al, causing the network to rapidly coarsen above ~200 °C, destroying the Orowan strengthening contribution (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 26-28). From Figure 19 of the Michi review, LPBF AlSi10Mg yield strength drops from ~220 MPa at RT to near zero above 300 °C (michi2021towardshightemperatureapplications media 7756ef39). Limited creep studies exist at 260–300 °C.

### 5.3 Scalmalloy (Al–Mg–Sc–Zr)
After aging at 325 °C/4 h, LPBF Scalmalloy achieves RT yield strengths of 468–493 MPa and UTS of 510–547 MPa (cerri2024mainheattreatments pages 14-16). However, Mg solid-solution strengthening provides little benefit above ~200 °C, and despite the coarsening-resistant L1₂-Al₃(Sc,Zr) precipitates, yield strength drops to ~60 MPa at 300 °C based on the Michi Figure 19 comparison (michi2021towardshightemperatureapplications media 7756ef39). The Al₃(Sc,Zr) precipitates provide microstructural stability but cannot overcome the fundamental loss of matrix strength at high homologous temperature.

### 5.4 DuAlumin-3D (Al–9Ce–4Ni–0.5Mn–1Zr)
This ORNL-designed HiFI alloy exploits a ternary Al–Ce–Ni eutectic to achieve both LPBF processability and thermal stability. After T5 aging (350 °C/8 h), DuAlumin-3D achieves approximate yield strengths (XY plane) of **350 MPa at RT, 300 MPa at 93 °C, 240 MPa at 204 °C, 170 MPa at 343 °C, and 115 MPa at 398 °C** (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 9-12, plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 12-17, plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum media 9fae2a72). These significantly outperform 2219-T61 above 200 °C. The alloy shows anisotropic behavior in the as-printed state, with a ductility trough at ~200 °C along the build direction, but anisotropy decreases substantially after heat treatment (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 12-17). RT high-cycle fatigue performance approximately matches 2219-T61 at ~105 MPa runout stress (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 12-17).

A related LPBF Al–Ce–Ni–Mn alloy studied by Michi et al. (DOI: 10.1016/j.actamat.2022.117699) achieved RT properties of YS = 258 ± 4 MPa, UTS = 370 ± 4 MPa, and elongation = 10 ± 1%. Creep threshold stresses were **28 MPa at 300 °C, 16 MPa at 325 °C, 26 MPa at 350 °C, and 18 MPa at 400 °C**—approximately 40–50% lower than PM 8009 at a given minimum creep rate (michi2022acreepresistantadditively pages 14-18).

### 5.5 AM ACMZ (Al–8.6Cu–0.5Mn–0.9Zr)
Michi et al. (DOI: 10.1016/j.actamat.2022.118557) reported that LPBF ACMZ in the as-fabricated condition has a yield strength of **121 ± 2 MPa at 300 °C**, which decreases to 53 ± 1 MPa after 400 °C/200 h heat treatment. Tensile creep at 300 °C showed the applied stress for a minimum creep rate of 10⁻⁷ s⁻¹ was **56 MPa (as-fabricated)** vs. **30 MPa (400 °C/200 h)** (michi2023loadshufflingduring pages 30-33). Apparent stress exponents for dislocation creep were n = 8–18, reflecting load shuffling between grain-boundary sliding regions and strengthened grain interiors (michi2023loadshufflingduring pages 27-30). Failure strain approached ~50% at 300 °C and ~70% at 400 °C for the most heat-treated conditions (michi2023loadshufflingduring pages 8-13).

### 5.6 LPBF Al–Ce–Mg
Sisco et al. (DOI: 10.1038/s41598-021-86370-4) demonstrated that both near-eutectic (Al–11Ce–7Mg) and hyper-eutectic (Al–15Ce–9Mg) compositions showed **higher yield strength than AM AlSi10Mg for temperatures up to 350 °C and better strength retention than Scalmalloy** (sisco2021microstructureandproperties media 4e365c5d). From the published Figure 7, near-eutectic Al–Ce–Mg yields approximately 270–370 MPa at RT (condition-dependent), ~160 MPa at 200 °C, and ~130 MPa at 300 °C. The hypereutectic composition shows somewhat higher strength retention.

### 5.7 A205/A20X (Al–Cu–Mg–Ag–TiB₂)
This aerospace-qualified alloy combines Ω-phase strengthening with TiB₂ grain refinement. LPBF A205 achieves high RT strength but shows **marked degradation above ~250 °C**, making it unsuitable for sustained service beyond approximately 200–250 °C (shakil2021microstructureandsmallscale pages 72-77). There are almost no published creep data for LPBF A205 at the operationally relevant 150–200 °C range, motivating dedicated studies (shakil2021microstructureandsmallscale pages 72-77).

### 5.8 High-Temperature AM Alloy Summary (Figure 19)
The most comprehensive published comparison of LPBF Al alloy yield strength vs. temperature is Figure 19 from Michi et al. (DOI: 10.1080/09506608.2021.1951580). It includes Al–Si–Mg, Al–Mg–Zr, Al–Mg–Si–Sc–Zr, Al–Cu–Mn–Zr, Al–Cu–Mg–Ni–Fe–Ti–B, Al–Ce–Mn, Al–Si–Ni–Fe, Al–Nd–Ni–Co, and Al + 35 vol.% TiC. The Al–Nd–Ni–Co and Al + TiC composites show the highest elevated-temperature strength (>200 MPa at 400 °C), while the Al–Ce–Mn and Al–Cu–Mn–Zr families also show excellent retention (michi2021towardshightemperatureapplications media 7756ef39).

A recent Nature Communications study by Ma et al. (DOI: 10.1038/s41467-025-66441-0) reported an LPBF Al–La–Sc–Zr alloy with stable nanoscale eutectic cellular networks achieving RT YS of 418 MPa and 300 °C YS of ~246 MPa—outperforming commercial LPBF Al–Mg–Sc–Zr by 2–4× at 300 °C (ma2025hightemperaturestrengthin pages 4-6).

## 6. What a Designer of 150–300 °C Aluminum Parts Needs

The retrieved evidence strongly supports the following test requirements beyond standard RT tensile bars (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 1-3, michi2023loadshufflingduring pages 30-33, michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 25-26, michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 31-32):

1. **Temperature-dependent tensile testing** per ASTM E21 at the full service range (e.g., RT, 150, 200, 250, 300 °C), with controlled soak time (typically 10–30 min), strain rate (~10⁻⁴ s⁻¹), and both build orientations.

2. **Tensile creep and stress-rupture testing** per ASTM E139 at 2–3 temperatures and 3–5 stress levels, targeting minimum creep rates of 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁹ s⁻¹ and rupture lives of 100–10,000 h.

3. **Long-exposure thermal stability:** Pre-expose specimens at service temperature for 100, 1,000, and ideally 10,000 h, then re-test tensile, creep, and microstructural properties to quantify precipitate coarsening and strength degradation.

4. **Elevated-temperature fatigue:** High-cycle fatigue (R = −1 and R = 0.1) at service temperature; low-cycle fatigue if thermal cycling is expected; creep-fatigue interaction testing for components under combined thermal and mechanical loading.

5. **Microstructural characterization** (SEM, TEM, APT) before and after exposure to track precipitate size, morphology, volume fraction, and grain-boundary evolution.

6. **Elevated-temperature elastic modulus** (by resonance or ultrasonic methods), since design stiffness requirements often control component geometry.

**How much do elevated-temperature properties differ from RT values?** For precipitation-hardened alloys like 2219-T61, yield strength at 300 °C is only ~20% of the RT value, and at 400 °C it is <10% (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum media 9fae2a72, plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum media 59dd48dd). Even thermally stable HiFI alloys like DuAlumin-3D retain only ~50% at 343 °C and ~33% at 398 °C (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum media 9fae2a72). This underscores that **RT tensile data alone are fundamentally inadequate for designing 150–300 °C components.**

### 7.1 Specific Gaps for Critical-Mineral-Lean Compositions

Alloy systems lean in Sc, Li, and rare earths—precisely the compositions the BYU lab targets—have the **least mature elevated-temperature databases**:

- **Al–Mg–Zr (Addalloy-type):** Almost no published creep data, elevated-temperature fatigue data, or long-exposure stability data. The evolution of Zr supersaturation and Al₃Zr precipitation with aging time is insufficiently reported to design heat treatments (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 25-26).

- **Al–Fe–Zr and Al–Ni–Zr:** Very limited public data beyond selected RT tensile results. These systems exploit low-solubility, low-diffusivity elements and show promising thermal stability, but standardized creep and fatigue datasets are absent (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 31-32).

- **Al–Mn–Sc (lean Sc):** Some creep data exist for selected compositions, but broad stress–temperature maps are unavailable. Partial Mn substitution for Mg to reduce vaporization losses during LPBF is promising but under-characterized (rong2026recentadvancesin pages 28-29).

- **Al–Cu–Mn–Zr (AM):** Among the best-characterized AM high-temperature systems, but most data are at 300 °C only. The cast ACMZ 5,000 h stability matrix has not been replicated on AM builds (michi2023loadshufflingduring pages 27-30, michi2023loadshufflingduring pages 30-33).

### 7.2 Recommended Test Ladder for the BYU Lab

Given that the BYU Vertical Cloud Lab currently performs single tracks → density cubes → RT tensile bars, the evidence supports adding the following in priority order:

1. **Compression or indentation creep screening** at 200–300 °C using small specimens (compatible with limited powder quantities from ultrasonic atomization). This is the most efficient way to rank alloy candidates for thermal stability before committing to full ASTM E139 creep testing.

2. **Elevated-temperature tensile testing** at 200 and 300 °C per ASTM E21 on the best candidates from creep screening.

3. **Post-exposure RT tensile testing** after 100 h and 1,000 h at 200/250/300 °C to assess microstructural stability without requiring in-situ high-temperature testing equipment.

4. **Elevated-temperature fatigue** (high-cycle, R = −1) at 200 °C once candidate alloys are down-selected.

## 8. Confidence Assessment

**Strongly supported claims:**
- θ′ coarsening is the primary mechanism of strength loss in Al–Cu alloys above ~200 °C (multiple independent sources, DOIs provided).
- Mn and Zr co-segregation stabilizes θ′ in ACMZ to 300 °C for 5,000 h (Bahl et al. 2021, DOI: 10.1016/j.matdes.2020.109378).
- 8009 Al–Fe–V–Si creep threshold stresses (Peng et al. 1999, DOI: 10.1016/S0921-5093(98)00876-4).
- DuAlumin-3D outperforms 2219-T61 across the full temperature range (ORNL/TM-2024/3244, DOI: 10.2172/2301648).
- AM Al alloys severely lack elevated-temperature mechanical property evaluation (Michi et al. 2021, DOI: 10.1080/09506608.2021.1951580).

**Moderately supported claims:**
- Approximate yield-strength values read from published figures (noted as approximate throughout).
- Relative ranking of AM alloy families at elevated temperature from Figure 19.

**Weakly supported or not recovered:**
- MMPDS-level design allowables for 2618, 2024, 7075, 7050, 6061, 2014, 2519, 2139 at specific temperatures and exposure times. These exist in MMPDS-18 but are restricted distribution and were not directly accessed.
- Long-exposure (10,000 h) data for most alloy systems.
- Elevated-temperature fatigue data for essentially all LPBF Al alloy families.
- Specific Constellium Aheadd alloy data (proprietary).
- Detailed data for cast 319, A356, 354, 242, and 4032 at temperature (not recovered in this search but available in ASM handbooks).
