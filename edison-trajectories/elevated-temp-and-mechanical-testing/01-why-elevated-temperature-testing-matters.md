# Why Elevated-Temperature Mechanical Testing Is Essential for Aerospace Structural Alloys—and What It Reveals That Room-Temperature Testing Cannot

## 1. Where Aerospace Structures Get Hot

Aerospace structures encounter elevated temperatures across a wide range of applications, from moderate supersonic aerodynamic heating to extreme hypersonic and propulsion-system thermal environments. Room-temperature tensile data alone cannot predict structural integrity in any of these regimes.

**Supersonic transport skins.** The Concorde, cruising at Mach 2, experienced fuselage skin temperatures of approximately 100–127 °C due to aerodynamic frictional heating (mai2025aerospacecompositestheir pages 2-3, singh2025materialvulnerabilitiesin pages 7-9). The aluminum alloy RR58 (Hiduminium, U.S. designation 2618/CM001) was specifically selected and processed to optimize creep resistance at these temperatures over an intended structural life exceeding 60,000 hours (jr2004effectofthermal pages 1-5, jr2004effectofthermal pages 5-8). The NASA High-Speed Research (HSR) program evaluated candidate aluminum alloys for a Mach 2.4 HSCT, with structural temperatures in the 93–135 °C (200–275 °F) range; candidate Al–Cu–Mg–Ag and Al–Cu–Li–Mg–Ag alloys were tested at 200, 225, and 275 °F for thermal exposures up to 30,000 hours (jr2004effectofthermal pages 1-5, jr2004effectofthermal pages 25-28).

**Hypersonic vehicles and missiles.** At Mach 5–10, skin temperatures escalate dramatically. For air-breathing hypersonic vehicles, equilibrium skin temperatures can reach 1,370 °C (2,500 °F) or higher over large areas, and forebody panels on a studied blended-wing-body concept reached approximately 1,140–1,480 °C (blevins1993thermoacousticloadsand pages 4-5, blevins1993thermoacousticloadsand pages 1-2, tenney1989materialsandstructures pages 2-3). Conventional aluminum is limited to roughly 175 °C (350 °F); titanium-matrix composites degrade above approximately 540 °C (1,000 °F) through oxidation and creep; and carbon–carbon composites are required for the hottest zones (blevins1993thermoacousticloadsand pages 2-3, blevins1993thermoacousticloadsand pages 1-2).

**Engine and propulsion components.** In jet engine compressors, Ti-6Al-4V is used in the front stages (fatigue-limited), while Ti-6242S serves the creep-limited rear stages approaching 500–600 °C (williams2003progressinstructural pages 17-19, kumar2021recentadvancesin pages 5-6). Superalloys in turbine hot sections, combustors, and heat exchangers operate at approximately 760–1,093 °C, with sustained use at 1,400–2,000 °F (tenney1989materialsandstructures pages 8-9). Rocket nozzle skin without active cooling can exceed 1,650 °C (3,000 °F), driven by heat fluxes of 2.0–2.83 MW/m² (blevins1993thermoacousticloadsand pages 4-4, blevins1993thermoacousticloadsand pages 3-4).

**Thermal protection system substructure.** On vehicles like the Space Shuttle, rigid and flexible ceramic TPS protected an aluminum primary structure; the substructure temperature must remain below aluminum's allowable limit even though the outer surface may exceed 1,370 °C (tenney1989materialsandstructures pages 3-4, tenney1989materialsandstructures pages 2-3).

## 2. What Changes with Temperature and Time at Temperature

### 2.1 Loss of Strength, Modulus, and Elongation at Temperature

Elevated-temperature tensile testing reveals immediate reductions in yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and elastic modulus that room-temperature data cannot predict. For LPBF AlSi10Mg (stress-relieved at 350 °C for 2 h), UTS dropped from 280.2 MPa at room temperature to 162.8 MPa at 250 °C and 34.4 MPa at 450 °C, while elongation increased from 6.4% to 15.6% and 26.5%, respectively (lehmhus2022hightemperaturemechanicalproperties pages 1-2). For LPBF Al–Mg–Sc–Zr (aged at 350 °C for 4 h), UTS declined from 560.6 MPa at RT to 435.1 MPa at 100 °C, 269.4 MPa at 150 °C, 102.8 MPa at 200 °C, and 54.0 MPa at 250 °C, with corresponding elongation increases from 11.1% to 72.2% (ren2024studyonmicrostructure pages 1-2, ren2024studyonmicrostructure pages 13-14). These data demonstrate that at typical aerospace service temperatures (100–250 °C for aluminum), strength retention can be severely reduced—information that is entirely invisible to room-temperature tensile bars.

### 2.2 Property Loss After Thermal Exposure (Overaging, Precipitate Coarsening)

Distinct from instantaneous hot-strength loss, prolonged thermal exposure degrades room-temperature properties through microstructural evolution. NASA's evaluation of HSCT candidate alloys showed that at 200 °F (93 °C) and 225 °F (107 °C), tensile strengths remained within about 5% of as-received values after 10,000 hours; however, at 275 °F (135 °C), tensile-strength reductions of 5–25% occurred after 15,000 hours, associated with precipitate coarsening and dissolution of the strengthening Ω phase (jr2004effectofthermal pages 25-28, jr2004effectofthermal pages 21-25). For Concorde's RR58, prolonged exposure at approximately 120–127 °C caused Ostwald ripening of Cu- and Mg-based precipitates, reducing yield strength, fatigue resistance, and creep performance over time (singh2025materialvulnerabilitiesin pages 12-14). MMPDS-style "effect of thermal exposure" curves present this degradation by plotting room-temperature residual UTS, YS, and elongation versus exposure time at fixed temperatures, with durations spanning from hours to 30,000 hours (jr2004effectofthermal pages 89-94, jr2004effectofthermal pages 76-80, jr2004effectofthermal pages 80-84).

### 2.3 Creep, Stress Rupture, and Stress Relaxation

Creep—time-dependent plastic deformation under sustained stress at elevated temperature—is a failure mode invisible to short-time tensile testing. The Concorde structural design imposed a stringent limit of 0.1% permanent strain over the aircraft lifetime, requiring extensive creep data and specialized alloy processing (taminger1999analysisofcreep pages 30-34). Creep becomes significant for aluminum alloys even at 75–250 °F over long durations, and for titanium alloys the rear compressor stages approaching 500–600 °C become creep-limited rather than fatigue-limited (taminger1999analysisofcreep pages 13-17, williams2003progressinstructural pages 17-19). Stress relaxation—the decline of stress at constant strain—affects bolted joints, seals, and interference fits at temperature and is measured per ASTM E328.

### 2.4 Creep-Life Extrapolation Methods

Because full-life creep tests at service temperature are often impractical (60,000+ hours for a supersonic transport), parametric methods are used to extrapolate short-term data:

**Larson–Miller parameter (LMP):** P_LM = T(C + log₁₀ t), where T is absolute temperature, t is rupture time (hours), and C is an empirical constant (commonly approximated as 20 but alloy-dependent). The parameter collapses stress–rupture data from multiple temperatures onto a single master curve (taminger1999analysisofcreep pages 30-34, gustin2022creeplifeprediction pages 2-4, abdallah2018creeplifingmodels pages 3-7). Caution is warranted: small changes in C can substantially alter predicted life, and C may vary with cold work, thermomechanical processing, and phase transitions (gustin2022creeplifeprediction pages 2-4, abdallah2018creeplifingmodels pages 3-7).

**Monkman–Grant relation:** ε̇_min^m · t_f = C_MG, linking minimum creep strain rate to rupture time. Because minimum creep rate can be measured early in a test, this enables rupture-time estimation from shorter experiments. Reported exponents m range from approximately 0.8 to 1.0 (jr2020computationalmethodsfor pages 27-29, abdallah2018creeplifingmodels pages 11-13).

**Wilshire equations:** Normalize applied stress by the temperature-dependent ultimate tensile strength: ε̇_min = A*(σ/σ_TS)^n exp(−Q_c*/RT). This reduces scatter and improves extrapolation behavior at low stresses relative to power-law models, though the stress exponent n can change unpredictably at low σ/σ_TS (abdallah2018creeplifingmodels pages 16-18, abdallah2018creeplifingmodels pages 31-34).

### 2.5 Low-Cycle Fatigue, Thermomechanical Fatigue, and Creep–Fatigue Interaction

Isothermal low-cycle fatigue (ASTM E606) at temperature reveals cycle-dependent damage that includes creep effects absent at room temperature. Thermomechanical fatigue (TMF, ASTM E2368) independently cycles temperature and mechanical strain: in-phase (IP) TMF places peak tensile strain at peak temperature, promoting creep damage, while out-of-phase (OP) TMF places peak tension at minimum temperature, promoting oxidation-related damage. Representative superalloy TMF tests use 500–1,000 °C temperature cycles with 0.5–1.0% mechanical strain amplitude and 72-second ramps (shi2026experimentalandintrinsic pages 7-10, shi2026experimentalandintrinsic pages 4-7). Under identical loading, IP-TMF typically produces the shortest fatigue life (moverare2025areviewof pages 5-6). TMF with peak-temperature holds introduces creep–fatigue interaction: studies have used 60–1,200-second holds at maximum temperature, finding that longer dwells substantially reduce life (moverare2025areviewof pages 5-6, ahmed2017thermomechanicallowcyclefatiguecreep pages 1-2). Creep–fatigue testing per ASTM E2714 specifically evaluates damage enhanced by creep through extended dwell periods, capturing behavior that separate fatigue and creep tests cannot predict (slotwinski2012mechanicalpropertiestesting pages 9-12).

### 2.6 Fatigue-Crack Growth and Fracture Toughness at Temperature

At elevated temperature, environmental effects (oxidation, moisture), creep-induced damage accumulation at crack tips, and time-dependent deformation alter fatigue-crack growth rates and fracture toughness. Creep crack growth (ASTM E1457) measures time-dependent crack extension in precracked specimens under sustained load at elevated temperature—a failure mode completely absent from room-temperature testing. Titanium alloys at engine temperatures show microstructure-dependent and environment-dependent fatigue-crack growth behavior (williams2003progressinstructural pages 17-19).

### 2.7 Oxidation

Oxidation becomes an increasingly important degradation mechanism at elevated temperature. Titanium alloys form an oxygen-enriched "alpha case" that reduces fatigue strength; nickel superalloys rely on protective chromia or alumina scales, with spallation leading to accelerated hot corrosion. Carbon–carbon composites require protective coatings because oxidation is a major failure mechanism above approximately 500 °C (blevins1993thermoacousticloadsand pages 2-3, tenney1989materialsandstructures pages 8-9).

### 2.8 Dynamic Strain Aging

Dynamic strain aging (DSA) produces the Portevin–Le Chatelier (PLC) effect—serrated/jerky flow in stress–strain curves—within specific temperature and strain-rate windows. In aluminum alloys, DSA arises from interactions between mobile dislocations and diffusing solute atoms (primarily Mg in Al–Mg systems). For Al 5019 (a 5xxx alloy), PLC activation energy was approximately 56 kJ/mol, consistent with pipe diffusion of Mg (dryzek2024serratedflowbehavior pages 8-11, dryzek2024serratedflowbehavior pages 13-14). In Al 3004, serrated flow was observed over 253–453 K (−20 to 180 °C) at strain rates of 5.56 × 10⁻⁵ to 5.56 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ (peng2006studyondynamic pages 1-2). In AA 2024, natural aging and GP-zone formation suppress PLC, illustrating the interaction between DSA and precipitation state (delaunois2019contributiontothe pages 1-4). DSA manifests as negative strain-rate sensitivity, localized deformation bands, and reduced ductility—phenomena invisible at room temperature in some alloy conditions but potentially significant at LPBF processing temperatures where solute states may be non-equilibrium (tamimi2015modellingtheportevinle pages 1-3, dryzek2024serratedflowbehavior pages 1-2).

## 3. How Design Allowables Handle Temperature

### 3.1 MMPDS "Effect of Temperature" and "Effect of Exposure Time" Curves

The Metallic Materials Properties Development and Standardization (MMPDS) handbook—the successor to MIL-HDBK-5—presents elevated-temperature design data in two principal formats. **Effect-of-temperature curves** plot tensile properties (Ftu, Fty, E) as ratios of their elevated-temperature values to room-temperature values, as a function of test temperature. **Effect-of-thermal-exposure curves** plot room-temperature residual properties after prior exposure at specified temperatures for durations typically ranging from 0.5 to 10,000 hours (and beyond). Both formats are essential: the former sizes structure for instantaneous hot loads; the latter determines whether the alloy will still meet room-temperature allowables after accumulated service.

The NASA evaluation of HSCT candidate alloys directly illustrates these two curve types. Figures plotted UTS, YS, and elongation versus test temperature (−65 to 275 °F), and separately versus exposure time (0 to 30,000 hours) at 200, 225, and 275 °F, for alloys C415, C416, ML377, RX818, CM001, and 1143 (jr2004effectofthermal pages 89-94, jr2004effectofthermal pages 76-80, jr2004effectofthermal pages 84-89, jr2004effectofthermal pages 69-76, jr2004effectofthermal pages 80-84).

### 3.2 Differences Among Alloy Families

**Aluminum alloys** are most sensitive to thermal exposure: even at 93–107 °C (200–225 °F), multi-thousand-hour exposures cause measurable overaging, and at 135 °C (275 °F) strength losses can reach 25% (jr2004effectofthermal pages 25-28, jr2004effectofthermal pages 21-25). **Titanium alloys** retain strength to approximately 300–400 °C with minimal time-dependent degradation, but above 500 °C creep and oxidation (alpha-case) become controlling, and exposure-time curves become important (williams2003progressinstructural pages 17-19, kumar2021recentadvancesin pages 5-6). **High-strength steels** generally retain strength well into the 300–400 °C range but may lose toughness through temper embrittlement during long exposures (weakly supported by available evidence). **Nickel superalloys** are designed for sustained operation at 650–1,050 °C, and their MMPDS-equivalent data include extensive creep-rupture and stress-rupture curves as a function of temperature and time; thermal-exposure effects (γ′ coarsening, TCP-phase formation) are managed through compositional design and are typically characterized for >1,000-hour exposures (weakly supported; specific MMPDS superalloy pages were not retrieved).

**Heating methods and temperature control.** Elevated-temperature tensile testing (ASTM E21) typically employs resistance (split-tube) furnaces, with temperature maintained within ±5 °F (±2.8 °C) across the gauge section, as demonstrated in a comprehensive evaluation of elevated-temperature tensile practice (manning1960anevaluationof pages 9-14). Temperature is monitored by multiple thermocouples attached directly to the specimen gauge section (lee2013experimentalinvestigationof pages 1-2, lee2013experimentalinvestigationof pages 4-5). Induction heating and radiant-lamp furnaces are also used, particularly for TMF where rapid heating rates (e.g., 6.94 °C/s) are required (shi2026experimentalandintrinsic pages 7-10).

**Strain-rate control.** Historical ASTM E21 practice specified strain rates of approximately 0.005 min⁻¹ up to yield and 0.05 min⁻¹ thereafter, emphasizing that rates must be measured in the specimen gauge length rather than from crosshead speed (manning1960anevaluationof pages 9-14). Elevated-temperature tensile testing of steel at rates of 0.01 and 0.1 in./min showed that loading rate strongly affects measured strength above 600 °C, underscoring the need for rate control (lee2013experimentalinvestigationof pages 1-2).

**High-temperature extensometry.** Air-cooled high-temperature extensometers (e.g., MTS 632.54E-11 with 1-inch gauge length and −5% to +10% strain range) are used for elevated-temperature tensile tests; resetting is required when large strains are anticipated (lee2013experimentalinvestigationof pages 1-2, lee2013experimentalinvestigationof pages 4-5). For TMF, axial extensometers must simultaneously track mechanical strain while the temperature gradient across the gauge section is maintained within 15 °C (shi2026experimentalandintrinsic pages 7-10).

**Soak time.** Temperature stabilization prior to loading is critical. Reported practice reached target temperature in approximately 20 minutes followed by a one-hour soak (lee2013experimentalinvestigationof pages 4-5). For creep testing (ASTM E139), thermal equilibrium must be established before load application; test durations may extend to 25,000 hours (messner2023asmecodequalification pages 65-68).

**Creep and stress-rupture testing (ASTM E139/ISO 204)** records minimum creep rate, time to specified strain (e.g., 0.1%, 0.2%, 1.0%), onset of tertiary creep, and rupture time under constant load at constant temperature. For AM qualification, one proposed plan specified testing from 500 to 900 °C with six replicates per condition (messner2023asmecodequalification pages 65-68).

**TMF testing (ASTM E2368)** independently and simultaneously cycles temperature and mechanical strain fields. Representative Ni-superalloy tests used 500–1,000 °C temperature ranges, 0.5–1.0% mechanical strain amplitudes, 72-second heating/cooling ramps, and optional 60- or 300-second peak-temperature holds (shi2026experimentalandintrinsic pages 7-10, shi2026experimentalandintrinsic pages 4-7).

**Creep–fatigue testing (ASTM E2714)** uses strain- or force-controlled cycling with extended elevated-temperature hold periods. It evaluates whether dwell-enhanced damage reduces life below predictions from separate fatigue and creep data. Hold times of 1,200 seconds have been employed in superalloy studies (slotwinski2012mechanicalpropertiestesting pages 9-12, moverare2025areviewof pages 5-6).

## 5. Failures, Anomalies, and Design Changes Traced to Elevated-Temperature Property Considerations

**Concorde RR58 thermal degradation.** The Concorde's Hiduminium RR58 (2618A) alloy experienced cumulative thermal degradation at skin temperatures of approximately 120–127 °C over thousands of flight cycles. Cu- and Mg-based strengthening precipitates coarsened through Ostwald ripening, reducing yield strength and fatigue resistance over time. Above approximately 120 °C, grain-boundary precipitates lost their pinning effectiveness, facilitating grain-boundary sliding and increased creep deformation. Joints, fastener holes, and constrained regions were identified as fatigue-critical locations where thermally degraded properties could compromise structural integrity (singh2025materialvulnerabilitiesin pages 12-14). This experience directly motivated the development and testing of improved Al–Cu–Mg–Ag alloys with 20%+ higher yield strength than CM001 for the HSCT program (jr2004effectofthermal pages 1-5, jr2004effectofthermal pages 25-28). **[Strongly supported by multiple sources.]**

**Alloy selection driven by creep data.** The Concorde's selection of RR58/CM001 over other aluminum alloys was explicitly driven by elevated-temperature creep data: the alloy was processed with iron additions and thermomechanical treatment to create Al–Fe intermetallic dispersoids that improved creep resistance, and a 0.1% permanent-strain lifetime limit was enforced (taminger1999analysisofcreep pages 30-34). This represents a design change relative to conventional subsonic-aircraft practice, where creep is negligible. The subsequent HSCT program's replacement of CM001 with candidate alloys C415, C416, and ML377—which maintained stability up to 30,000 hours at 200–225 °F—was similarly driven by improved thermal-exposure data (jr2004effectofthermal pages 25-28). **[Strongly supported.]**

**Compressor-stage alloy selection.** The transition from Ti-6Al-4V in front compressor stages to Ti-6242S in rear stages, and ultimately to nickel-base superalloys in the hottest stages, is directly driven by creep data: front stages are fatigue-limited, while rear stages are creep-limited (williams2003progressinstructural pages 17-19). An alloy selected on the basis of room-temperature fatigue and tensile data alone would be inadequate for the rear compressor stages. The selection of 2124 aluminum for certain applications was later revised to other alloys with improved fatigue strength, illustrating the consequences of incomplete property characterization (williams2003progressinstructural pages 17-19). **[Strongly supported.]**

**Insufficient high-temperature data for LPBF alloys.** A comprehensive review concluded that evaluations of high-temperature mechanical properties and corrosion responses of additively manufactured aluminum alloys are "severely deficient," with little published data above 200 °C and essentially no creep or stress-rupture data for most LPBF aluminum systems (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 25-26). This deficiency directly limits the qualification of LPBF aluminum alloys for any elevated-temperature aerospace application. **[Strongly supported.]**

For LPBF AlSi10Mg, the eutectic silicon network that provides strengthening at room temperature coarsens and spheroidizes above approximately 200 °C, destroying Orowan strengthening (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 26-28, wang2024areviewon pages 7-9). For LPBF Al–Mg–Sc–Zr alloys—closer to the BYU lab's design space of critical-mineral-lean compositions with Sc, Zr, and potentially Ce—L1₂ Al₃(Sc,Zr) nanoprecipitates provide better coarsening resistance, but rapid solidification produces supersaturated solid solutions that evolve upon thermal exposure, and the volume fraction and stability of these precipitates directly control elevated-temperature strength retention (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 26-28, michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 25-26).

**Recommended elevated-temperature tests to add**, in approximate priority order based on the evidence reviewed:

1. **Elevated-temperature tensile testing (ASTM E21/ISO 6892-2)** at 100–300 °C, to quantify instantaneous strength retention.
2. **Thermal-exposure testing followed by room-temperature tensile testing**, at 150–300 °C for 100–10,000 hours, to assess overaging and precipitate coarsening.
3. **Creep and stress-rupture testing (ASTM E139/ISO 204)** at 150–300 °C under representative stresses, to determine whether LPBF microstructures resist time-dependent deformation.
4. **Stress-relaxation testing (ASTM E328)** if fastener or interference-fit applications are anticipated.
5. **Low-cycle fatigue at temperature (ASTM E606)** and potentially creep–fatigue testing (ASTM E2714), if cyclic thermal-mechanical loading is expected.
6. **Fatigue-crack growth at temperature** and thermal-cycle testing, to evaluate damage tolerance in the presence of LPBF defects at elevated temperature.

These tests will close the "severely deficient" data gap identified for LPBF aluminum alloys (michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 25-26) and position the alloys for aerospace qualification in thermal environments where room-temperature data alone provide no assurance of structural integrity.
