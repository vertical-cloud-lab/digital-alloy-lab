## How Test Settings—Especially Strain Rate—Change Measured Tensile Properties of Aluminum Alloys, and How a Lab Should Choose and Report Them

### 1. Quasi-Static Strain Rate: Standards, Control Methods, and Property Shifts

**ASTM E8/E8M and B557.** ASTM E8/E8M (Standard Test Methods for Tension Testing of Metallic Materials) and ASTM B557 (Standard Test Methods for Tension Testing Wrought and Cast Aluminum- and Magnesium-Alloy Products) specify crosshead-speed-based rate control. ASTM E8 permits crosshead speeds that produce strain rates in the range of approximately 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻² s⁻¹, with rate increases allowed after yielding. ASTM B557 follows a similar framework but is specific to aluminum and magnesium products.

**ISO 6892-1 Method A versus Method B.** ISO 6892-1:2016 provides two control methods. Method A uses closed-loop extensometer feedback to maintain prescribed strain rates at four stages of the test: Range 1 (elastic region): 0.00007 s⁻¹; Range 2 (through yield): 0.00025 s⁻¹; Range 3 (post-yield to UTS): 0.0067 s⁻¹; and Range 4 (beyond UTS): 0.025 s⁻¹. Method B uses crosshead displacement rate control, which does not maintain a constant specimen strain rate because machine compliance, grip slip, and specimen stiffness changes cause the actual strain rate to vary during the test (haefliger2021influenceofquasistatic pages 1-3, haefliger2021influenceofquasistatic pages 3-4). The proportional gauge length in ISO 6892-1 uses the slimness ratio k = 5.65 (L₀ = 5.65√A₀), whereas ASTM E8 uses k = 4.51 (moura2020ahighlyaccurate pages 1-3, doksanovic2018stress–strainrelationshipsand pages 4-7).

**Crosshead versus extensometer control.** Displacement-controlled (crosshead) testing does not maintain constant strain rate on the specimen. Naumann and Merklein (2024; DOI: 10.21741/9781644903131-101) demonstrated that optical strain rate control (OSRC) using DIC maintains the nominal 0.4%/s strain rate with RMSE of only 2.4×10⁻⁵ %/s, whereas displacement strain rate control (DSRC) produced actual average rates of 0.369–0.428 %/s with deviations up to 0.278 %/s and non-monotonic rate evolution during testing (naumann2024influenceofan pages 1-3, naumann2024influenceofan pages 3-5). Closed-loop extensometer-controlled tests produce lower yield strengths and less scatter than open-loop machines; Gray and Sharp reported scatter bands of ±1.7 ksi for extensometer control versus ±3.0 ksi for open-loop control in steel (clinch1997thevariationin pages 46-51).

**Magnitude of property shifts across quasi-static rates.** For reinforcing steel, Häfliger et al. (2021; DOI: 10.1016/j.conbuildmat.2021.122967) measured up to 8% stress increase across the quasi-static strain-rate range of 0.004–1.0 ‰/s and 5% difference between the slowest and fastest quasi-static rates (haefliger2021influenceofquasistatic pages 1-3). For aluminum alloys, the effect is generally smaller but non-negligible. Ma'at et al. (2019) tested AA6061 at 2.53×10⁻³, 2.53×10⁻², and 2.53×10⁻¹ s⁻¹ at room temperature and found yield strength of approximately 270–272 MPa and UTS of 365–384 MPa, indicating modest quasi-static rate sensitivity at room temperature (maat2019effectsoftemperatures pages 3-5). The sensitivity increases substantially at elevated temperatures: at 250°C, yield strength ranged from 192 to 220 MPa across the same rate range (maat2019effectsoftemperatures pages 3-5).

For **AA6016** sheet, Ubertalli et al. (2020; DOI: 10.3390/met10020242) compared static (10⁻³ s⁻¹) and dynamic (5×10² s⁻¹) tensile properties: yield strength increased by 54% (T4 temper) and 38% (T6 temper); UTS increased by 13–14%; and elongation at fracture increased dramatically by 75% (T4) and 105% (T6) (ubertalli2020highstrainrate pages 3-6, ubertalli2020highstrainrate pages 6-8). These large elongation increases at high rate are strongly supported by multiple studies.

For **AA5754 and AA5182**, Smerd et al. (2005; DOI: 10.1016/j.ijimpeng.2005.04.013) found relatively low flow-stress rate sensitivity but substantially higher ductility at strain rates of 600–1500 s⁻¹, with elongations exceeding 40% at rates above 2000 s⁻¹ compared to ~30% quasi-statically (smerd2005highstrainrate pages 1-3, smerd2005highstrainrate pages 17-20). This is strongly supported (555 citations).

For **LPBF AlSi10Mg**, quasi-static properties are orientation-dependent: flat—UTS 305±2 MPa, YS 192±3 MPa, elongation 5.4±0.1%; horizontal—UTS 320±1 MPa, YS 200.8±0.9 MPa, elongation 4.84±0.02%; vertical—UTS 315±1 MPa, YS 180±1 MPa, elongation 3.3±0.1% (ghisi2022theinfluenceof pages 4-6).

### 2. Strain-Rate Sensitivity: The Exponent m, Negative SRS, and PLC Serrations

**Strain-rate sensitivity exponent.** The strain-rate sensitivity parameter m = ∂ln σ / ∂ln ε̇ describes how flow stress changes with strain rate. For most FCC aluminum alloys at room temperature, m is small and positive (typically 0.001–0.02). However, Al-Mg (5xxx) alloys exhibit a regime of negative m associated with dynamic strain aging (DSA).

**Negative SRS and PLC effect in Al-Mg alloys.** Yamada et al. (2018; DOI: 10.3390/met8080576) systematically investigated AA5005, AA5021, AA5082, and AA5182 from 10⁻⁴ to 10³ s⁻¹ at room temperature. Pure aluminum A1070 and AA5005 showed positive strain-rate dependence. AA5021 exhibited serrations and negative strain-rate dependence between 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻² s⁻¹. AA5082 and AA5182 showed strong negative SRS across the full quasi-static range (10⁻⁴ to 10⁻¹ s⁻¹) due to higher Mg content promoting dislocation pinning (yamada2018strainratedependence pages 3-7, yamada2018strainratedependence pages 7-10, yamada2018strainratedependence pages 1-3). The transition from negative to positive SRS for AA5182 occurs near ~100 s⁻¹ at room temperature; PLC serrations were reported from 193–383 K at strain rates ≤10⁻¹ s⁻¹ (yamada2018strainratedependence pages 7-10).

The PLC mechanism involves Mg solute atoms diffusing to and temporarily pinning mobile dislocations. At sufficiently low strain rates, waiting times allow complete solute atmospheres to form; at high rates, Mg cannot migrate fast enough and serrations disappear (mrkobrada2025portevin–lechateliereffect pages 3-4, tamimi2015modellingtheportevinle pages 3-5, tamimi2015modellingtheportevinle pages 1-3). PLC bands are classified as Type A (high rate/low temperature), Type B (intermediate), and Type C (low rate/high temperature) (mrkobrada2025portevin–lechateliereffect pages 3-4). Increasing Mg content intensifies DSA, strengthens PLC serrations, and can reduce ductility and formability (mrkobrada2025portevin–lechateliereffect pages 4-6).

Hopperstad et al. (2007; DOI: 10.1088/0965-0393/15/7/004) modeled the PLC effect in AA5083-H116 and showed that negative steady-state SRS occurs at intermediate strain rates, while positive SRS is recovered at both low and high rates. Importantly, PLC bands reduce strain to necking and increase surface roughness (hopperstad2007anumericalstudy pages 3-5). This is strongly supported (62 citations).

**Negative SRS in LPBF aluminum.** Salandari-Rabori (2022) reported a transition to negative SRS in as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg between 10⁻² and 10⁻¹ s⁻¹, attributed to the disappearance of DSA and changing nanoprecipitate interactions from shearing to looping/bypassing. Activation volumes span approximately 20–95 b³ (salandarirabori2022deformationratedependentbehavior pages 149-152). This finding is moderately supported.

**Strain-rate jump and stress-relaxation tests.** These methods measure m on a single specimen by suddenly changing rate or halting the crosshead and monitoring stress decay. Häfliger et al. (2021) used machine stops of 2–45 minutes and found logarithmic stress decay; for fast preceding rates (1 ‰/s), the slope decreased after 70–100 s, while for slow rates (0.004 ‰/s), decay continued logarithmically even after 45 min (haefliger2021influenceofquasistatic pages 3-4).

**Zener-Hollomon parameter and superplastic forming.** The Zener-Hollomon parameter Z = ε̇ exp(Q/RT) captures the coupled effect of temperature and strain rate on hot deformation. Wang et al. (2025; DOI: 10.3390/ma19010091) established a strain-compensated Z model for 7085 aluminum alloy (593–743 K, 0.001–1 s⁻¹) with R² = 0.992. The optimal processing window was 693–743 K at 0.01–0.001 s⁻¹. Softening transitions from dynamic recovery alone (high lnZ ≥ 20) to combined dynamic recovery and recrystallization (low lnZ ≤ 15). Superplastic forming of Al-Mg-Zn-Cu-Zr alloys achieved 700–800% elongation at 400–480°C and strain rates of 10⁻²–10⁻¹ s⁻¹.

### 3. Intermediate and High Strain Rates: Testing Methods and Constitutive Models

**Intermediate rates (1–1000 s⁻¹).** Bhujangrao et al. (2020; DOI: 10.3390/met10070894) comprehensively reviewed intermediate strain-rate testing devices. Conventional servo-hydraulic machines are reliable only below ~10 s⁻¹; above ~50 s⁻¹, actuator/grip inertia and stress-wave oscillations corrupt load signals (bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 3-5, bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 5-7). ISO 26203-2:2011 specifically addresses metallic-material tensile testing at high strain rates using servo-hydraulic systems (bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 21-23). High-speed servo-hydraulic machines use slack adaptors to reduce inertia effects but can generate stress-wave oscillations upon sudden engagement (bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 3-5). Drop towers are used for crushing studies at medium strain rates; the "Dropkinson bar" combines a drop table with a Hopkinson bar, achieving strain rates up to 600 s⁻¹ with 0.5 ms loading times (bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 5-7). A hybrid apparatus combining servo-hydraulic loading with Hopkinson-bar strain-gauge load measurement substantially improves data quality around 10² s⁻¹ compared to piezoelectric load cells alone (bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 5-7).

**Split Hopkinson (Kolsky) bars (10²–10⁴ s⁻¹).** Seidt (2010) used compression, tension, and torsion SHB setups for 2024-T351 aluminum, noting that typical SHB installations cover approximately 500–5000 s⁻¹, with the attainable range depending on loading mode and apparatus dimensions (seidt2010plasticdeformationand pages 62-65). Load ringing is a primary concern: raw dynamic curves show marked oscillations in the elastic-plastic transition zone because the suddenly applied load is not homogeneous along the specimen (ubertalli2020highstrainrate pages 6-8). Smerd et al. (2005) used low-impedance integrated grips and frequency-domain wave propagation to reconstruct signals, with specimen geometry designed for short gauge length to reduce ring-up time (smerd2005highstrainrate pages 3-6).

**Adiabatic heating.** At high strain rates, deformation is essentially adiabatic, with the Taylor-Quinney factor (typically ~0.95 for aluminum) converting plastic work to heat. Smerd et al. calculated temperature rise from plastic work using ΔT = ∫σdε/(ρCp) (smerd2005highstrainrate pages 3-6). Ghisi et al. (2022) suggested that localized temperatures during SHPB testing of LPBF AlSi10Mg may exceed 150°C, potentially initiating silicon-network decomposition (ghisi2022theinfluenceof pages 10-13).

**Specimen design for high-rate testing.** Short gauge lengths are preferred to reduce ring-up time and inertial effects while maintaining uniaxial stress state. Ubertalli et al. (2020) used 10 mm gauge length specimens for dynamic tests compared to 80 mm for static tests, and demonstrated that short and long specimens overlapped almost completely in static testing (ubertalli2020highstrainrate pages 3-6). Jakkula (2025) used a 4×15 mm cylindrical gauge with 2D DIC for Scalmalloy SHTB testing (jakkula2025novelexperimentalsplithopkinson pages 69-72).

The 6061 calibration by Yadav et al. (2022; DOI: 10.1155/2022/3707200) used a reference strain rate of 10⁻³ s⁻¹ and dynamic compression at 1000–3000 s⁻¹ at 20–200°C, yielding A = 318 MPa, B = 395 MPa, n = 0.737, C = 0.012, m = 1.77 (yadav2022experimentalandnumerical pages 11-15, yadav2022experimentalandnumerical pages 19-21). The compilation by Radhi and Rashied (2026; DOI: 10.61268/xqn01h69) provides widely-used reference values for machining simulations: 2024-T3 (A=369, B=684, n=0.73, C=0.0083, m=1.70), 6061-T6 (A=324, B=114, n=0.42, C=0.0028, m=1.34), and 7075-T6 (A=546, B=678, n=0.71, C=0.0024, m=1.56) (radhi2026thermomechanicalnumericalsimulation pages 3-4).

For **LPBF AlSi10Mg**, Ghisi et al. (2022; DOI: 10.2139/ssrn.4135305) fitted orientation-dependent J-C parameters: flat build A=178.7 MPa, B=734.5 MPa, n=0.56, C=0.017; vertical A=182.8 MPa, B=2159.5 MPa, n=0.77, C=0.014; horizontal A=191.9 MPa, B=1044.9 MPa, n=0.65, C=0.004. The strain-rate coefficient C was small in all cases, confirming weak rate hardening in as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg (ghisi2022theinfluenceof pages 13-14, ghisi2022theinfluenceof pages 10-13).

**Zerilli-Armstrong model.** The Z-A model is physically based and distinguishes FCC from BCC crystal behavior. For FCC aluminum, it includes thermally activated dislocation glide terms. El-Aty et al. (2024; DOI: 10.3390/ma17071584) fitted a modified Z-A model to AA2060 at 100–500°C and 0.01–10 s⁻¹, obtaining C₁=498 MPa, C₂=125.34 MPa, n=0.51, C₃=0.0042, C₄=0.0066, C₅=0.0184, C₆=0.0001 (elaty2024modellingtheflow pages 6-9). Comparative studies suggest Z-A better captures the interaction between strain hardening and thermal softening at high cutting speeds above 200 m/min for Al 6061 (radhi2026thermomechanicalnumericalsimulation pages 3-4). This claim is moderately supported.

### 4. Applications Requiring Rate-Dependent Data

**Crashworthiness.** Vehicle crash events produce local strain rates of approximately 500 s⁻¹ in folding regions, with higher rates possible locally (smerd2005highstrainrate pages 1-3). Ubertalli et al. (2020) emphasized that reliable dynamic material data are essential for crashworthiness simulations of body-in-white aluminum components, as UTS increases 13–14% and elongation 75–105% at dynamic rates versus static (ubertalli2020highstrainrate pages 6-8). This is strongly supported.

**Bird strike.** EASA CS-25.631 requires aircraft to sustain impact with a 4 lb (1.8 kg) bird at cruise speed at sea level, or 0.85 cruise speed at 8000 ft (chen2023designforbird pages 1-4). For empennage structures, an 8 lb (3.6 kg) bird criterion applies (guida2008studydesignand pages 16-22). Strain rates during bird impact are characteristic of the 10²–10³ s⁻¹ range. McCarthy et al. (2004; DOI: 10.1023/b:acma.0000037133.64496.13) modeled 2024-T3 aluminum layers in fibre-metal-laminate leading edges but notably excluded strain-rate dependency for aluminum, assuming rate effects arose from the glass layers—a simplification that may not be conservative (mccarthy2004modellingofbird pages 7-10).

**Fan-blade-out containment and ballistic protection.** The FAA Uncontained Engine Debris Mitigation Program specifically investigates 2024-T351 aluminum because it is common in aircraft structures around engines. Engine-rotor failures produce high-velocity fragments, requiring constitutive models calibrated across relevant strain rates, temperatures, and stress states (seidt2010plasticdeformationand pages 18-22). This strongly supports the need for rate-dependent data.

**Forming.** Sheet-metal forming operations produce strain rates of approximately 10⁰–10² s⁻¹, while electromagnetic forming can reach ~3500 s⁻¹ (bhujangrao2020reviewofintermediate pages 1-3, smerd2005highstrainrate pages 1-3). Rate-dependent data are needed for constitutive models in stamping simulations and for predicting springback in rate-sensitive materials.

### 5. Other Test Settings That Change the Result

**Specimen size, geometry, and gauge length.** Yield strength and UTS are largely insensitive to specimen geometry (round vs. flat, standard vs. sub-size), but elongation is strongly affected. Masete et al. (2018; DOI: 10.1088/1757-899x/430/1/012015) found approximately 10% difference in elongation for different gauge lengths in Ti-6Al-4V, with UTS unaffected; specimens with matched L/√A ratios produced more comparable elongation results (only 6% difference) than specimens matched by L/W ratio (14% difference) (masete2018theeffectof pages 1-4, masete2018theeffectof pages 4-6). This principle applies to aluminum alloys as well.

ISO 6892-1 uses proportional specimens with k = L₀/√A₀ = 5.65 (the "A5.65" convention), while ASTM E8 uses k = 4.51 (moura2020ahighlyaccurate pages 1-3, doksanovic2018stress–strainrelationshipsand pages 4-7). The A50 (50 mm fixed gauge length) and A80 (80 mm) conventions in ISO 6892-1 are non-proportional and produce elongation values that cannot be directly compared with proportional specimens without correction. Dokšanović et al. (2018; DOI: 10.13167/2018.16.5) reported that increasing gauge length from 40 to 90 mm changed strain at fracture by up to 35.8%, while modulus of elasticity varied by ~9.6% and strain at yield by ~8.1% (doksanovic2018stress–strainrelationshipsand pages 9-12).

For aluminum specifically, Pourhassan et al. (2017; DOI: 10.1016/j.prostr.2017.07.198) found that standard versus non-standard specimen geometries for 2024-T3 ALCLAD produced yield strengths of 304 versus 290 MPa (4.6% difference), while ductility was nearly identical (pourhassan2017materialpropertiesof pages 4-6). Ubertalli et al. (2020) confirmed that short (10 mm) and long (80 mm) gauge-length specimens overlapped almost completely in stress-strain behavior for AA6016 (ubertalli2020highstrainrate pages 3-6). This is moderately supported.

**Surface condition: as-built versus machined LPBF.** Ghisi et al. (2022) emphasized that machined LPBF specimens remove surface defects and produce better apparent properties, so machined results may not represent net-shape AM parts (ghisi2022theinfluenceof pages 1-2). García-Zapata et al. (2024; DOI: 10.3390/ma17153655) reported that at 60 µm layer height, as-built surface roughness was Ra = 25 µm (Rz = 126 µm), and vertical specimens showed 19% lower resistance and 64% lower ductility than flat specimens; at 30 µm layer height, reductions were 7% and 47%, respectively (garciazapata2024effectsofbuilding pages 19-22). This strongly supports the need to report surface condition.

**Build orientation.** For LPBF AlSi10Mg, build orientation is the most influential parameter on both quasi-static and dynamic properties (amir2022studyonthe pages 10-11, amir2022studyonthe pages 1-2). Vertical specimens exhibit lower strain-to-failure because loading is parallel to columnar grains and perpendicular to melt-pool boundaries (ghisi2022theinfluenceof pages 4-6). Kan et al. (2019; DOI: 10.1016/j.addma.2019.100805) found horizontal (X) specimens had YS of 307±11 MPa versus vertical (Z) YS of 210±8 MPa—a 32% reduction attributed to unfavorably oriented lack-of-fusion defects (kan2019factorsthataffect pages 19-23). This is strongly supported (202 citations).

**Test temperature and soak.** Temperature strongly interacts with strain rate. Vilamosa et al. (2015; DOI: 10.1051/epjconf/20159404018) showed that AA6060 has negligible SRS below 200°C but exhibits strong positive SRS at higher temperatures, with both yielding and work hardening affected (this finding from their abstract). The coupled strain-rate and temperature sensitivity is challenging to capture with most existing constitutive models.

**Strain measurement method.** Clip-on extensometers provide standard gauge-length-averaged strain but cannot capture post-necking local strain. Video extensometers and DIC provide full-field strain measurement. Hedayati et al. (2017; DOI: 10.1051/meca/2016060) showed that DIC strain measurement error can be less than 2% compared to clip-on extensometer when properly configured. DIC is essential for high-rate testing where contact extensometers cannot be used. Pourhassan et al. (2017) demonstrated good agreement between clip-on extensometer and DIC for standard aluminum tensile specimens (pourhassan2017materialpropertiesof pages 4-6).

**Machine stiffness.** Machine compliance affects the actual specimen strain rate during crosshead-controlled testing. Clinch (1997) analyzed that machine stiffness can produce yield-strength variations up to 2.69 ksi (~18.5 MPa) depending on machine hardness and elastic strain-rate range (clinch1997thevariationin pages 41-46). Open-loop machines produced higher yield strengths and greater scatter than closed-loop extensometer-controlled machines (clinch1997thevariationin pages 46-51).

### Recommendations for the BYU Vertical Cloud Lab

Based on this review, the following recommendations emerge for a lab designing LPBF aluminum alloys and testing room-temperature tensile bars:

1. **Report strain rate explicitly.** Always report the actual strain rate used, the control method (crosshead vs. extensometer feedback), and the standard followed (ASTM E8/B557 or ISO 6892-1 Method A/B). The ISO 6892-1 Method A strain-rate ranges (0.00025 s⁻¹ through yield, 0.0067 s⁻¹ post-yield) provide better reproducibility than crosshead control.

2. **Use consistent specimen geometry.** Report gauge length, cross-section dimensions, and the slimness ratio k. For LPBF, test in multiple build orientations (at minimum horizontal and vertical) and report surface condition (as-built or machined).

3. **For elevated-temperature and rate-dependent testing to add**, consider:
   - **Intermediate-rate servo-hydraulic testing** (1–100 s⁻¹) per ISO 26203-2 for crashworthiness data
   - **Split Hopkinson bar testing** (10²–10⁴ s⁻¹) if ballistic or impact applications are targeted
   - **Strain-rate jump tests** to efficiently measure m on single specimens
   - **Hot compression/tension** at temperatures and rates relevant to forming (300–500°C, 10⁻³–10⁻¹ s⁻¹) with Zener-Hollomon analysis

4. **Be aware of PLC effects** in Al-Mg-containing LPBF alloys: negative SRS and serrated flow can occur in specific strain-rate and temperature windows, complicating constitutive modeling and forming operations.

5. **When fitting J-C parameters**, always report the reference strain rate, the range of strain rates and temperatures used for calibration, and the build orientation for LPBF materials. Parameters are not universal constants—they are calibration-specific.
