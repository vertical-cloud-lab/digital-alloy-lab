Question: How have aerospace and automotive programs documented elevated-temperature and strain-rate testing to select and qualify aluminum alloys? Examples: Concorde's 2618A (RR58 / AU2GN) skin and its long-time creep and thermal-exposure testing at about 100-130 C; the NASA High-Speed Research (HSCT) program's evaluation of 2xxx and 6xxx alloys (for example C415, C416, 2519, 6013) for 60,000 h at about 107-177 C; MMPDS thermal-exposure data; aluminum in engine nacelles, pylons, inlets and auxiliary power units; 2618 and 2219 in compressor wheels and impellers; aluminum on missiles and hypersonic vehicles; and additively manufactured high-temperature aluminum parts (heat exchangers, brackets near engines). Also crash and impact testing of aluminum (automotive crash boxes, bird strike, fan-blade containment) that drove high-strain-rate testing and Johnson-Cook calibration. For each, give the temperatures, exposure times or strain rates used, the properties measured, and NASA, FAA or AFRL technical reports or conference papers with citations, DOIs or NTRS numbers.

# Elevated-temperature and impact testing of aluminum alloys

**The governing distinction is between a service-life target, time actually spent in a test furnace, and the much shorter duration of an impact.** The best-documented supersonic-airframe program below specified a 60,000-hour service objective but published tensile results after exposures of *up to 30,000 hours*; its 177 °C upper end is not supported by that tensile report. Impact programs instead measured deformation and failure over specified strain rates or projectile speeds, then tested whether a calibrated model predicted *other* impact conditions. (jr2004effectofthermal pages 1-5, jr1993nasauvalightaerospace pages 226-226, cordasco2017astatusreview pages 2-5)

## Long-duration thermal qualification

| Program or material | Documented conditions | Properties and selection result | Traceable source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Concorde skin: 2618A / RR58-derived CM001** | Mach 2.05 skin maximum **approximately 130 °C**; design life **7,000 flights/15,000 hours**. Creep deformation and fatigue resistance informed its safe-life design. The 15,000 hours is a *design life*, not an exposure duration established by the cited source. | CM001 was made by modifying RR58 composition and processing to meet Concorde creep, durability, strength and fracture requirements. NASA identifies **2618 as the US equivalent** of RR58/CM001; equivalent designations do not make all tempers and product forms interchangeable. | Hénaff *et al.*, *Creep and Creep-Fatigue Crack Growth in Aluminium Alloys*, DOI **10.5772/15153**; Edahl and Domack, **NASA/TP-2004-212988**. (hnaff2011creepandcreepfatigue pages 1-3, jr2004effectofthermal pages 1-5, jr2004effectofthermal pages 5-8) |
| **Later supersonic-skin creep–fatigue research: 2650-T6** | A proposed **20,000-flight/60,000-hour** life near **130 °C** motivated damage-tolerance work. *Actual 2650 tests*, not Concorde 2618A tests, included prior exposure **30,000 hours at 100 or 130 °C** and **20,000 hours at 175 °C**, followed by hardness and tensile tests; creep/crack-growth experiments principally covered **100–175 °C**. | No significant loss of measured properties after those prior exposures was reported. Compact-tension specimens were used for creep, fatigue and creep–fatigue crack growth; the study distinguishes their mechanisms and hold-time effects from simple uncracked tensile strength. | Hénaff *et al.*, DOI **10.5772/15153**. (hnaff2011creepandcreepfatigue pages 1-3) |
| **NASA–University of Virginia HSCT screening** | **60,000 hours** was a performance objective and **120,000 hours** a stated certification goal—not demonstrated furnace-test durations. The program considered ingot-metallurgy **2xxx and 6xxx** families; **2519-T87** was a 2xxx baseline and **6013-T6** a 6xxx reference for lower-wing/fuselage concepts. | Screening addressed elevated-temperature strength retention, creep resistance and damage tolerance. The early program record does **not** establish that either reference alloy passed a 60,000-hour exposure test. | Starke, **NASA CR-4517** (1993); the subsequently cited program report is **NASA/CR-97-206248** (1997). (jr1993nasauvalightaerospace pages 226-226, jr1993nasauvalightaerospace pages 5-9, jr1993nasauvalightaerospace pages 14-18, jr2004effectofthermal pages 28-32) |
| **NASA HSCT long-exposure tensile comparison** | Exposures at **200, 225 and 275 °F (93, 107 and 135 °C)**, for **up to 30,000 hours**; tensile tests at **−65 °F, room temperature, 200, 225 and 275 °F**. Actual candidates were **C415, C416, RX818 and ML377**; **CM001/2618-equivalent and 1143** were comparators. **C415/C416 were derived from 2519, not specimens designated 2519; 6013 was not in this test matrix.** | Measured yield strength, ultimate strength, elongation and modulus, including orientation effects and both room-temperature *post-exposure* and *hot* tests. C415, C416 and ML377 retained tensile strength within roughly **5% through 30,000 hours at 200–225 °F**; after exposure at **275 °F for 15,000 hours**, candidate tensile-strength losses reached **25%**. RX818 was dropped after **5,000 hours** because a companion study found inadequate fracture toughness despite its high strength. NASA identified **200–225 °F** as the candidates’ upper service-temperature range. | Edahl and Domack, *Effect of Thermal Exposure on the Tensile Properties of Aluminum Alloys for Elevated Temperature Service*, **NASA/TP-2004-212988** (2004). Companion creep paper: Taminger *et al.*, “Evaluation of Creep Behavior of Emerging Aluminum Alloys for Supersonic Aircraft Applications,” *Proceedings of the 6th International Conference on Aluminum Alloys* (1998), pp. 1361–1366; the tensile report **does not itself supply creep-rupture allowables**. (jr2004effectofthermal pages 1-5, jr2004effectofthermal pages 5-8, jr2004effectofthermal pages 25-28, jr2004effectofthermal pages 28-32) |

**Temperature correction.** The documented long-exposure tensile campaign reached **135 °C**, not **177 °C**. A separate NASA hypersonic-structures discussion describes protecting a conventional aluminum airframe below **350 °F/177 °C**; that is a *structural temperature limit for a thermal-protection concept*, not evidence that the HSCT C415/C416 coupons were soaked at 177 °C for 60,000 hours. (jr2004effectofthermal pages 1-5, tenney1989materialsandstructures pages 1-2)

**MMPDS.** MMPDS is the appropriate source to check alloy-, temper-, form-, thickness- and direction-specific design properties and elevated-temperature effects before establishing an allowable. Its definitions distinguish design yield and ultimate stresses and statistical A-/B-basis properties. The MMPDS material retrievable here did **not** provide a verified numerical prolonged-exposure curve for the particular alloy/temper combinations; consequently, neither a specific MMPDS exposure time nor an allowable is asserted. A short-duration hot tensile value must not be substituted for strength *after* long exposure or for creep performance. (standard2003a.0glossary pages 1-4, jr2004effectofthermal pages 1-5, jr2004effectofthermal pages 25-28)

## Engine-adjacent, high-speed and additively manufactured applications

* **Nacelles, pylons, inlets and APUs:** These labels alone do not specify the metal temperature. A documented engine application is the **cast-2219 fan case**; it is **not** evidence for a 2219 compressor *wheel*. An industry materials survey instead identifies Inconel 718 in a nacelle area at temperatures up to **650 °C** and titanium in compressor structures, illustrating why the hot zone cannot automatically be assigned an aluminum allowable. The retrieved sources give no component-specific NASA/FAA/AFRL aluminum heat-soak matrix for a particular pylon, inlet or APU, so their service temperatures and exposure hours cannot responsibly be generalized. Boyer *et al.*, DOI **10.1557/mrs.2015.278**. (boyer2015materialsconsiderationsfor pages 7-8, boyer2015materialsconsiderationsfor pages 8-9)
* **Compressor wheels and impellers:** A published **2618-versus-titanium impeller study at 50,000 rpm** is a *structural/CFD calculation*, not a reported hot creep-qualification campaign; DOI **10.3390/engproc2023059221**. Industry-associated turbocharger-wheel work used overspeed spin tests, surface metrology, fatigue specimens and fracture origins to investigate machining marks, but its reported result does not establish an elevated-temperature qualification of 2618 or 2219 wheels. Do not transfer fan-*case* data to rotating impellers. (ravinath2024optimizingandanalyzing pages 1-2, tabriz2019theeffectof pages 1-10, boyer2015materialsconsiderationsfor pages 8-9)
* **Missiles and hypersonic vehicles:** NASA’s explicitly documented route for an aluminum hypersonic airframe is a **thermal-protection system keeping the conventional structure at or below 350 °F (177 °C)**; other routes are active cooling or a higher-temperature *non-aluminum* hot structure. The hypersonic exterior and an insulated aluminum substrate therefore do not share a test temperature. Tenney, Lisagor and Dixon, *Journal of Aircraft* (1989), DOI **10.2514/3.45868**. The sources examined do not establish an alloy-specific missile-aluminum exposure schedule. (tenney1989materialsandstructures pages 1-2, tenney1989materialsandstructures pages 5-6)
* **Printed high-temperature aluminum:** An Oak Ridge-associated study of LPBF **Al–10Ce–8Mn** used hot tensile testing over **150–400 °C**, with a **30-minute equilibration**, and separately tested material pre-exposed **200 hours at 400 °C**; approximately **10⁻⁴ s⁻¹** was its initial tensile strain rate. It measured tensile behavior, hardness and microstructure rather than aircraft-component certification. Plotkowski *et al.*, *Acta Materialia* (2020), DOI **10.1016/j.actamat.2020.07.014**. LPBF **AlSi10Mg**, stress-relieved **2 hours at 350 °C**, was tensile-tested at room temperature, **125, 250 and 450 °C**: 0°-build ultimate strengths were **280.2, 162.8 and 34.4 MPa** at room temperature, 250 and 450 °C, respectively—evidence of why a printed bracket near an engine needs a measured *part-metal* temperature. Lehmhus *et al.*, DOI **10.3390/ma15207386**. (plotkowski2020microstructureandproperties pages 2-3, plotkowski2020microstructureandproperties pages 3-4, lehmhus2022hightemperaturemechanicalproperties pages 1-2)
* **Prototype versus qualification:** An **AlSi10Mg aircraft-engine cooling heat-exchanger prototype** was designed, printed and described for engine testing; the available thesis does **not** establish a certified high-temperature alloy allowable. Separately, a **DOE/ORNL–industry automotive-piston materials project** tested LPBF **Al–9Ce–4Ni–0.5Mn–1Zr** coupons after **350 °C/8 hours** heat treatment. Its tensile tests ran from room temperature through **350 °C at 5 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹**; fully reversed **50-Hz** fatigue tests at **150, 250 and 350 °C** used **10⁷ cycles** as runout, yielding respective fatigue strengths of **115, 100 and 60 MPa**. This is valuable engine-adjacent aluminum evidence, **not a NASA/FAA aerospace-part qualification**. Perrone (2019), *Prototype Development of an Innovative Heat Exchanger for an Aircraft Engine Cooling System Made by Additive Manufacturing*; Plotkowski, Rakhmonov and Ferlet, DOE report DOI **10.2172/2573209**. (perrone2019prototypedevelopmentof pages 1-6, plotkowski2025additivelymanufacturinghightemperature pages 8-12, plotkowski2025additivelymanufacturinghightemperature pages 12-17)

## Crash, bird-strike and fan-containment testing

| Driver and documented test | Rate, impact condition and properties measured | Why it mattered; source |
|---|---|---|
| **Automotive crash-forming/body-sheet material** | Tensile split-Hopkinson tests on **AA5754 and AA5182** at **600, 1,100 and 1,500 s⁻¹**, compared with **3.3 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹** quasistatic tests; room- and elevated-temperature experiments measured stress–strain response and failure elongation. The paper describes an approximately **500 s⁻¹ nominal crash rate**, with higher local folding rates. | Flow-stress rate sensitivity was modest, but failure elongation increased markedly; fitted Johnson–Cook behavior therefore needs *ductility/failure* data as well as yield stress. Smerd *et al.*, *International Journal of Impact Engineering* (2005), DOI **10.1016/j.ijimpeng.2005.04.013**. These are sheet-alloy tests motivated by crashworthiness, not proof that the specimens were complete crash boxes. (smerd2005highstrainrate pages 1-3) |
| **FAA engine-fragment shielding/fan-containment model check** | **2024-T3** plates of **0.0625, 0.125 and 0.25 in.** were fired on with a **0.5-in. steel sphere**. Outcomes were ballistic limit, residual speed and *petaling versus plugging*. A Johnson–Cook model calibrated to **0.125 in.** overpredicted the thin plate’s ballistic limit and underpredicted the thick plate’s. | FAA pursued stress-state-, temperature- and rate-dependent **tabulated MAT_224** rather than treating one fitted Johnson–Cook failure parameter set as universally predictive. Cordasco, Emmerling and Du Bois, *11th European LS-DYNA Conference* (2017); related FAA report **DOT/FAA/AR-08/36**. (cordasco2017astatusreview pages 1-2, cordasco2017astatusreview pages 2-5, cordasco2017astatusreview pages 14-15) |
| **FAA coupon calibration for 2024-T351** | One **0.5-in.-thick plate** supplied multiaxial deformation/fracture specimens spanning about **10⁻⁴–11,000 s⁻¹** and **50–450 °C** in the FAA program account. An independently published 2024-T351 study covered **10⁻⁴–10⁴ s⁻¹** and **−50 to 450 °C**, with tension, compression and shear. | Measured plastic flow, fracture strain, orientation dependence and stress-state dependence; rates alone were insufficient because the 2024 study found little plastic-flow rate effect up to roughly **5,000 s⁻¹** and noncoincident tension/compression/shear curves. Sources: **DOT/FAA/TC-13/25, Parts 1–3**; Seidt and Gilat, DOI **10.1016/j.ijsolstr.2013.02.006**. The two quoted temperature ranges come from *different accounts* and should not be conflated. (cordasco2017astatusreview pages 5-8, cordasco2017astatusreview pages 14-15, seidt2013plasticdeformationof pages 1-2) |
| **NASA Glenn/FAA ballistic validation** | NASA **NASA/TM-2012-217625** tested Al-2024 small panels at **0.125, 0.25 and 0.5 in.** with a **0.5-in. cylindrical projectile**, targeting penetration speeds around **600–900 ft/s (180–275 m/s)**; larger panels were also struck obliquely. Recorded impact/residual velocities, penetration, backside strain and displacement using high-speed imaging and digital-image correlation. Later oblique Al-2024 experiments covered **0°, 30° and 45°** panel tilt. | Plate-level measurements tested transfer of coupon-calibrated failure laws to actual penetration geometries. Pereira *et al.*, **NASA/TM-2012-217625**, journal DOI **10.1061/(ASCE)AS.1943-5525.0000356**; Ruggeri *et al.*, **NASA/TM-2015-218484 / DOT/FAA/TC-15/7**. **Projectile speed is not specimen strain rate**; neither memorandum establishes bird-strike resistance of an aluminum fan blade. (pereira2014ballisticimpacttesting pages 5-8, pereira2014ballisticimpacttesting pages 8-11, ruggeri2015impactandpenetration pages 1-5, ruggeri2015impactandpenetration pages 7-9) |

**Regulatory and bird-strike boundary:** FAA identifies fan-blade-off containment under **14 CFR 33.94** as requiring an *engine test*; analysis is supplementary and needs validation. Bird ingestion/strike is a related but different load case: an illustrative *simulation* compares **2024-T3 and 7075-T6** plates under soft impacts of **117 and 147 m/s**, using assigned Johnson–Cook parameters. Those speeds are simulation inputs, **not measured coupon strain rates or FAA material qualification tests**. In particular, a Johnson–Cook fit should report the tested alloy/temper, temperature, plastic strain and rate range, failure stress states, and independent plate/component validation—not merely a parameter list. FAA conference paper above; Aslam *et al.*, DOI **10.1088/1742-6596/1707/1/012008**. (cordasco2017astatusreview pages 1-2, cordasco2017astatusreview pages 2-5, cordasco2017astatusreview pages 5-8)

**Evidence limit:** The documents establish strong examples of how programs *selected and evaluated* aluminum, and of how impact data improved predictive models. They do not jointly document a completed 60,000-hour HSCT soak, a verified MMPDS thermal-exposure allowable, or alloy-specific certified tests for every proposed nacelle, APU, missile, printed bracket, bird-strike component or 2219 impeller. Reporting those as completed qualification campaigns would exceed the available evidence. (jr2004effectofthermal pages 1-5, jr1993nasauvalightaerospace pages 226-226, standard2003a.0glossary pages 1-4, cordasco2017astatusreview pages 1-2)
