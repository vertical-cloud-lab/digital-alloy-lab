**Question (PRECEDENT):** Has anyone screened aluminum alloys for elevated-temperature strength or creep resistance with high-throughput methods, for example hot hardness or high-temperature nanoindentation on compositionally graded or combinatorial additively manufactured samples, small-punch creep tests, or multi-specimen creep frames?

Question: Has anyone screened aluminum alloys for elevated-temperature strength or creep resistance with high-throughput methods, for example hot hardness or high-temperature nanoindentation on compositionally graded or combinatorial additively manufactured samples, small-punch creep tests, or multi-specimen creep frames?

Yes. # High-throughput screening of aluminum alloys for hot strength and creep

**Yes—but the demonstrated approaches are mostly separate pieces of the proposed workflow.** Researchers have obtained multiple elevated-temperature creep curves from individual additively manufactured (AM) aluminum specimens, measured hot indentation properties locally within an aluminum alloy, and tested four aluminum-based small-punch creep specimens simultaneously. I did **not** find a published demonstration that combines a *compositionally graded AM aluminum-alloy library* with systematic hot-hardness mapping or long-duration creep screening across its compositions. That is a search-limited finding, not proof that no such study exists. (kulkarni2023creepbehaviorof pages 2-3, krizik2024creeppropertiesof pages 3-5, richter2008hotstagenanoindentation pages 2-3, shen2025highthroughputstudyon pages 2-4, michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 1-3)

**The closest AM-aluminum creep-screening examples are DIC-instrumented cantilever tests.** Kulkarni and colleagues performed two bending-creep tests on LPBF A205 at **200 °C**. Different stress-invariant positions along each beam, on both its tensile and compressive sides, supplied creep responses spanning **120–180 MPa**. Their minimum creep rates agreed with conventional uniaxial results below **170 MPa** and were within a factor of two above that stress. Thus, “high throughput” here means *multiple stress and loading-mode measurements per specimen*, not multiple alloy compositions tested concurrently. The paper also reports that an earlier study by Singh and colleagues found bending–uniaxial agreement for LPBF AlSi10Mg at **250 °C and 75–140 MPa**; I could verify those particulars in Kulkarni’s paper but could not inspect the original Singh article. Kulkarni’s Figure 5(a) directly compares the bending and uniaxial minimum-rate measurements. (kulkarni2023creepbehaviorof pages 2-3, kulkarni2023creepbehaviorof pages 5-7, kulkarni2023creepbehaviorof media 6a17e918)

**Actual parallel aluminum small-punch creep testing has also been demonstrated.** Krizik and colleagues tested a powder-metallurgy, oxide-network-strengthened Al–Al₂O₃ material (HITEMAL) at **573–673 K (300–400 °C)**. Their furnace had **four independently loaded measurement cells**, allowing four **8-mm-diameter, approximately 0.5-mm-thick discs** to run simultaneously; they tested two creep specimens per condition. They compared punch results with conventional tensile creep at **573 K** and proposed a material-specific stress correlation. This is substantive parallel testing, but of one aluminum-based composite rather than a graded alloy library. Punch force and deflection rate do not directly equal uniaxial stress and strain rate, and the discs fractured after relatively small, localized deformation—important constraints on ranking alloys from punch results alone. (krizik2024creeppropertiesof pages 1-3, krizik2024creeppropertiesof pages 3-5, krizik2024creeppropertiesof pages 5-7)

**Hot indentation and combinatorial composition screening have been shown separately.** Richter and colleagues indented individual phases and local Ni variations in an Al–Ni–Si piston alloy at **300, 500 and 650 K**, finding phase-dependent hot-hardness loss and indentation creep. This was a phase-resolved study of a specially prepared alloy, **not** a combinatorial AM build. Conversely, Shen and colleagues made a diffusion multiple containing **12 sampled Al–Mg–Si micro-alloy compositions** and measured hardness and 900-s indentation creep across it, but the *measurements were at room temperature*. Its high-temperature diffusion and aging steps must not be mistaken for hot indentation tests. (richter2008hotstagenanoindentation pages 1-2, richter2008hotstagenanoindentation pages 2-3, richter2008hotstagenanoindentation pages 4-5, shen2025highthroughputstudyon pages 2-4)

Finally, a **four-specimen, single-frame tensile-creep** design with separate DIC strain measurements has been validated at **650 °C**, but its demonstration material was **316L stainless steel, not aluminum**. It establishes a plausible apparatus strategy, not an aluminum-screening precedent. Overall, the literature supports a workflow in which combinatorial aluminum fabrication is followed by rapid local screening and then calibrated bending or parallel small-punch creep tests. The remaining evidentiary gap is a demonstrated, composition-resolved *elevated-temperature* aluminum screen that establishes how its rapid measurements predict conventional long-term creep performance. (singh2026anovelhighthroughput pages 1-3, singh2026anovelhighthroughput pages 3-5, michi2022towardshightemperatureapplications pages 1-3, shen2025highthroughputstudyon pages 2-4)
