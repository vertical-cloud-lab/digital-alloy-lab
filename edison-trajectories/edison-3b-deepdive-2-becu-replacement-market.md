## Executive assessment

A “critical minerals demand reduction” story for substituting beryllium copper (Cu–Be) with Be‑free copper alloys is defensible on three pillars: (i) **beryllium is federally designated as a U.S. critical mineral** (USGS 2022 list) and DOE-funded programs explicitly reference that list; (ii) the beryllium supply chain is **highly concentrated** (historically dominated by one U.S. mine/refiner and a narrow set of global processors/exporters) with **very low end-of-life recycling**; and (iii) **regulatory (OSHA) exposure controls** for beryllium are stringent, creating strong adoption pressure for Be‑free alternatives in manufacturing and additive manufacturing contexts. However, **Cu–Cr–Zr is not a drop-in substitute** for high-strength Cu–Be connector/spring use on strength and stress-relaxation performance; its strongest fit is in **high-conductivity thermal/electrical hardware and tooling/molds**. In contrast, **Cu–Ni–Sn (C72900/ToughMet®-type)** and **Cu–Ni–Si (Corson/Hyper‑Corson)** families have clearer commercial positioning as Cu–Be replacements for connectors/springs, though they have their own maturity and processing gaps.

## 4) Performance comparison: Be–Cu vs Cu–Cr–Zr vs Cu–Ni–Sn (and Cu–Ni–Si)

### Cu–Be (baseline for high-performance springs/connectors)
* A materials appendix lists Cu–Be **C17200** with composition “**1.9% Be, 0.20% Co**” and precipitation-hardened tensile strength **~1140–1310 MPa** and yield strength **~965–1205 MPa** (elongation 4–10%), and typical applications including **springs** and related elastic components. (kramar2018notesandlaboratory pages 74-76)
* A connector-focused Cu–Ni–Si paper cites typical Cu–Be performance as **~1200 MPa strength**, **~22% IACS conductivity**, and **~25% stress relaxation** after **150 °C/100 h** (presented as a limitation motivating alternatives). (lei2013effectofaluminum pages 1-2)

### Cu–Cr–Zr (CuCrZr / C18150 class)
Cu–Cr–Zr alloys show a consistent high conductivity + moderate-to-high strength trade space:
* ECAP + aging CuCrZr: **676 MPa** tensile strength with **73% IACS**; overaging increased conductivity to **78.1% IACS** with tensile strength dropping to **612 MPa**; literature “best” cited as **700 MPa / 77% IACS**. (liang2018amultiscalearchitectured pages 2-4, liang2018amultiscalearchitectured pages 1-2)
* Aging + cryorolling CuCrZr: reported **UTS ~712 MPa** with **~70.2% IACS**; other combinations in the same work include values such as **648 MPa / 79.8% IACS** (for Cu–1Cr–0.1Zr in cited work). (li2021ahighstrength pages 1-2, li2021ahighstrength pages 4-4)
* C18150 used in high-heat/accelerator studies is reported at **~75% IACS** “as received,” but strength values were not captured in the extracted excerpt. (arnauizquierdo2007highpowerrf pages 4-6)

Interpretation: CuCrZr can approach ~0.6–0.78 GPa UTS while retaining ~70–78% IACS, but it is not in the same high-strength class as C17200 precipitation-hardened strip/wire used for springs/connectors, and stress-relaxation performance for connector-like geometries was not quantified in the retrieved CuCrZr sources. (liang2018amultiscalearchitectured pages 2-4, li2021ahighstrength pages 1-2)

### Cu–Ni–Sn (spinodal-hardened C72900/ToughMet® type)
* Commercial Cu–15Ni–8Sn (C72900) products from Materion and Lebronze span ~**620–1380 MPa** (0.2% offset yield) and tensile strength up to **~1415 MPa** for some product variants; these are marketed into applications including **electronic connectors**, bearings/valves, and oil & gas components. (yang2023researchprogresson pages 2-3)
* Reviews explicitly position Cu–Ni–Sn alloys as substitutes for Cu–Be and report bulk industrial adoption for some electrical contact components (e.g., microswitch contact reeds), while noting additive manufacturing (LPBF) is still in the research stage and that processing/heat-treatment windows are sensitive. (guo2023areviewof pages 14-16)

Interpretation: on strength, Cu–Ni–Sn can overlap or approach Cu–Be in some products and is commercially mature enough to cite as a credible alternative; conductivity and standardized stress relaxation comparisons were not recovered as quantitative data in the extracted pages. (yang2023researchprogresson pages 2-3, guo2023areviewof pages 14-16)

### Cu–Ni–Si (Corson/Hyper‑Corson) as the “connector alloy” alternative (relevant for stress relaxation)
* A key review emphasizes that Corson-type Cu–Ni–Si alloys are already used for connector materials and can achieve ~**700 MPa yield strength** at ~**50% IACS**, with explicit industry targets for replacing Be–Cu being **800 MPa/50% IACS** or **950 MPa/30% IACS**. (altenberger2015materialpropertiesof pages 9-13)
* A thermomechanically processed Cu–Ni–Si–Al alloy achieved **UTS 1080 MPa**, **YS 985 MPa**, **28.1% IACS**, and **stress relaxation 9.83%** at **150 °C/100 h**, contrasted against Cu–Be’s cited ~25% under the same condition. (lei2013effectofaluminum pages 1-2)

Interpretation: connector/spring substitution is generally more credible with Cu–Ni–Si and Cu–Ni–Sn families than with CuCrZr, because stress-relaxation performance is central for contacts and CuCrZr evidence in this corpus is not yet connector-specific. (lei2013effectofaluminum pages 1-2, altenberger2015materialpropertiesof pages 9-13)

## 5) Current state of Be-free alternatives and gaps (CuCrZr and CuNiSn maturity)

### Cu–Ni–Sn maturity
* Multiple commercial Cu–Ni–Sn product families exist (ToughMet® 3/3AT/3TS; BrushForm® 158; Lebronze products), with very high strengths reported and explicit connector applications. (yang2023researchprogresson pages 2-3)
* Remaining gaps documented in reviews include segregation/processing cracking challenges, discontinuous precipitation control, and underdeveloped additive manufacturing routes (LPBF still research-stage). (guo2023areviewof pages 14-16, yang2023researchprogresson pages 17-19)

### Cu–Cr–Zr maturity
* CuCrZr/C18150 appears mature for high-conductivity applications (accelerator/fusion/thermal fatigue contexts) and is widely studied with many processing routes yielding reproducible strength–conductivity combinations in the ~600–780 MPa and ~70–78% IACS range. (liang2018amultiscalearchitectured pages 2-4, li2021ahighstrength pages 1-2, arnauizquierdo2007highpowerrf pages 4-6)
* Gap for “Be‑Cu replacement” narrative: for connector/spring service, the missing/weak evidence in this corpus is **stress relaxation performance** and the ability to reach C17200-like strength in thin strip/wire while maintaining conductivity; additionally, thermal cycles (e.g., HIP/aging) can reduce prime-aged strength in CuCrZr in some applications. (arnauizquierdo2007highpowerrf pages 4-6, li2021ahighstrength pages 2-4)

## 8) Health/safety and regulatory push toward Be-free

OSHA’s beryllium standard (29 CFR 1910.1024) provides a strong, quantifiable driver for substituting away from Cu–Be in manufacturing:
* **Action level (AL)**: **0.1 µg/m³** (8‑h TWA). (black2017bioenvironmentalengineeringguide pages 8-11, black2017bioenvironmentalengineeringguide pages 11-14, taruru2017berylliumlaboratoryanalysisthe pages 1-3)
* **PEL (8‑h TWA)**: **0.2 µg/m³**. (black2017bioenvironmentalengineeringguide pages 14-16, black2017bioenvironmentalengineeringguide pages 5-8, taruru2017berylliumlaboratoryanalysisthe pages 3-6)
* **STEL (15‑min)**: **2.0 µg/m³**. (black2017bioenvironmentalengineeringguide pages 8-11, black2017bioenvironmentalengineeringguide pages 5-8, taruru2017berylliumlaboratoryanalysisthe pages 3-6)
* The rule requires exposure assessment, engineering controls, regulated areas, housekeeping (wet/HEPA), and medical surveillance triggers at the action level; it exempts unprocessed “articles” and can exclude materials with <0.1 wt% Be if objective data show exposures remain below AL. (black2017bioenvironmentalengineeringguide pages 14-16, black2017bioenvironmentalengineeringguide pages 5-8, black2017bioenvironmentalengineeringguide pages 16-18)

Implication: the compliance burden and analytical sensitivity requirements under these low limits can be a compelling rationale for adopting Be‑free alloys, particularly in AM settings where powder handling and post-processing may elevate exposure risks. (taruru2017berylliumlaboratoryanalysisthe pages 3-6, taruru2017berylliumlaboratoryanalysisthe pages 1-3)
