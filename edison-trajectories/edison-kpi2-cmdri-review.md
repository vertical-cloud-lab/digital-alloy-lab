# Critical Literature-Grounded Review of the CMDRI KPI for FLAIME

## 1. Is the Helbig 12-Indicator Framework the Best Choice?

### Framework Assessment

The Helbig 12-indicator framework, as applied by Mulukutla et al. (2024), is a well-established semi-quantitative supply-risk assessment system. It deploys twelve indicators organized into four equally weighted categories (each 25% of the total): risk of supply reduction (S1–S3: static reach of reserves, static reach of resources, end-of-life recycling rate), risk of demand increase (D1–D4: future technology demand, by-product dependence, sector competition index, substitutability), market concentration risk (C1–C2: country-level and company-level HHI), and political risk (P1–P3: WGI political stability, policy perception index, and a human development proxy) (helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 5-7, helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 7-9). Each indicator is normalized onto a 0–100 scale, and element-level scores are aggregated to the alloy level using four methods: arithmetic mean, mass-share weighting, cost-share weighting, and a maximum-element approach (helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 7-9, helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 12-14).

This framework is the most granular supply-risk-only approach in the literature, having been validated on nickel-based superalloys (helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 7-9, helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 9-12) and lithium-ion battery materials (helbig2018supplyrisksassociated pages 10-13, helbig2018supplyrisksassociated pages 13-15). Helbig's own comprehensive review of 88 criticality methods covering 618 individual indicator applications confirmed that the most commonly used indicator categories—concentration, scarcity, and political instability—are all well represented in his 12-indicator set (helbig2021anoverviewof pages 2-4, helbig2021anoverviewof pages 1-2).

**Graedel 3-pillar methodology** extends beyond supply risk to include vulnerability to supply restriction and environmental implications, making it the most conceptually comprehensive academic framework (graedel2016sixyearsof pages 1-2, gloser2015rawmaterialcriticality pages 5-6). However, it is not standardized as a single alloy-level score and is more complex to operationalize in an autonomous design loop (knobloch2018fromcriticalityto pages 1-2).

The **EU CRM methodology** uses a two-axis approach (supply risk × economic importance) that is policy-recognized but coarser than Helbig, with supply risk decomposed into only four subcomponents: production concentration, governance stability, substitutability, and recycling (mancini2018characterizationofraw pages 2-3, gloser2015rawmaterialcriticality pages 5-6). It was designed for raw-material policy screening, not alloy-level compositional optimization.

The **Ferro & Bonollo Alloy Criticality Index (CIA)** is the most directly alloy-centric framework. It aggregates six normalized indicators (abundance risk level, sourcing/geopolitical risk, environmental country risk, normalized supply risk, economic importance, and recycling drawback index) at the element level, then computes an alloy-level score as CIA = Σ(CICRMᵢ × wt%CRMᵢ)/100 (ferro2020alloysubstitutionin pages 7-9, ferro2020howtoapply pages 1-3, asghar2025developmentofnew pages 7-9). This formulation was specifically designed for Ashby-style materials selection and substitution studies (ferro2019materialsselectionin pages 1-2, ferro2020alloysubstitutionin pages 4-7).

**Recommendation:** The Helbig framework is a defensible choice for the supply-risk dimension, but it should be complemented by the Ferro & Bonollo CIA formulation for alloy-level interpretability and by DOE's own two-axis criticality language for reviewer alignment (bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 133-136, bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 16-18). A hybrid KPI reporting both CMDRI-SR (Helbig-based) and CMDRI-CIA (Ferro & Bonollo–based) would strengthen the proposal considerably.

Using the Ferro & Bonollo framework, Si has a CICRM of only 4.83/10, while Mg scores 7.35/10 (ferro2020alloysubstitutionin pages 4-7). AlSi10Mg (approximately Al-10Si-0.3Mg) is dominated by Al (balance) and Si (10 wt%), both of which are low-criticality elements. The DOE 2023 Critical Materials Strategy classifies aluminum as noncritical in the short term and silicon as noncritical, with magnesium rated near-critical (bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 133-136, bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 16-18). The mass-weighted alloy criticality of AlSi10Mg is therefore already quite low—it is not a high-supply-risk alloy by any framework's standard.

In the Helbig framework applied to nickel superalloys, aluminum scored 46/100, which was among the lowest of all assessed elements (alongside titanium at 44), whereas rhenium scored 63, molybdenum 61, and cobalt 60 (helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 9-12). This confirms that aluminum-based alloys, even with minor additions of moderate-risk elements, start from a low-risk baseline.

For the Al-Mn-Cr-Zr system (approximately Al-5Mn-0.8Cr-0.6Zr per Mehta et al. 2022) (mehta2022al–mn–cr–zrbasedalloystailored pages 2-3), Mn and Cr are not on the DOE critical list (bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 133-136) and have end-of-life recycling rates exceeding 50% (helbig2018supplyrisksassociated pages 13-15). While their Helbig-scale supply-risk scores have not been individually published for these elements in an aluminum alloy context, their aggregate risk is likely in the moderate range. Demonstrating a ≥25% reduction versus an already-low AlSi10Mg baseline will be arithmetically challenging and may not be meaningful to reviewers who expect the comparison to highlight avoidance of genuinely supply-constrained materials.

**Assessment:** The 25% target is potentially achievable in a narrow arithmetic sense (replacing Mg content with Mn/Cr whose individual supply risks are somewhat lower), but the absolute magnitude of improvement is small and the narrative impact is weak. The target becomes much more defensible if Scalmalloy is added as a comparator (see Section 3).

### Literature Gap

No published studies were identified that compute alloy-level supply-risk scores specifically for AlSi10Mg, Al-Zr, or Al-Mn-Cr-Zr using the Helbig framework. Ferro & Bonollo's published CICRM tables include Si (4.83), Mg (7.35), and Sc (8.05) but do not include Mn, Cr, or Zr (ferro2020alloysubstitutionin pages 4-7). This represents a significant gap that the FLAIME team would need to fill by computing these scores from primary data sources (USGS, WGI, HHI) using the Helbig methodology.

## 3. Is AlSi10Mg the Right Incumbent?

AlSi10Mg is the correct benchmark for **LPBF processability and mechanical performance comparison** because it is the most widely printed aluminum alloy (arena2024novelaluminumalloy pages 4-6). However, it is a **weak benchmark for supply-risk reduction claims** because it is already a low-criticality alloy.

**Scalmalloy (Al-Mg-Sc-Zr)** would be a far more impactful comparator for the critical-mineral narrative. Scandium has the highest CICRM among common aluminum alloying elements at 8.05/10, driven by extreme supply concentration, high sourcing/geopolitical risk (SGR = 10.0), and high recycling drawback (RDI = 9.77) (ferro2020alloysubstitutionin pages 4-7). Scalmalloy is also a direct competitor in high-performance LPBF applications (arena2024novelaluminumalloy pages 4-6, yadegari2025aluminumlaseradditive pages 14-15), making the comparison technically relevant. By demonstrating that Al-Mn-Cr-Zr achieves comparable mechanical properties to Scalmalloy (yield strengths in the ~350 MPa range after aging) (arena2024novelaluminumalloy pages 4-6) while completely avoiding scandium, FLAIME would present a compelling supply-risk reduction story that aligns with DOE's critical-minerals mandate.

For the **Cu-Cr-Zr vs. Be-Cu** comparison, the case is stronger. Beryllium has a CICRM of 6.03, with a perfect RDI score of 10.0 (zero recycling) and moderate geopolitical risk (ferro2020alloysubstitutionin pages 4-7). Be-Cu replacement also eliminates well-documented toxicity hazards, adding a non-supply-risk dimension that strengthens the proposal narrative.

**Recommendation:** Frame the CMDRI KPI with a **dual-comparator approach**: AlSi10Mg as the process/performance benchmark and Scalmalloy as the supply-risk benchmark for the Al family. The Cu-Cr-Zr vs. Be-Cu comparison should be retained as-is.

## 4. Should CMDRI Be Decomposed into Element-Level Contributions?

**Yes, emphatically.** Both the Helbig and Ferro & Bonollo frameworks are inherently element-level assessments that aggregate upward. The Ferro & Bonollo CIA formula (CIA = Σ CICRMᵢ × wt%CRMᵢ / 100) is explicitly compositionally decomposable (asghar2025developmentofnew pages 7-9, ferro2020alloysubstitutionin pages 7-9). Helbig's four aggregation methods (arithmetic mean, mass-share, cost-share, maximum) each preserve element-level traceability (helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 7-9, helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 12-14).

### Handling Non-Zero Risk of Zr, Mn, Cr

Zr, Mn, and Cr all carry non-zero supply risk. In the Al-Mn-Cr-Zr system, Mn constitutes approximately 5 wt% (mehta2022al–mn–cr–zrbasedalloystailored pages 2-3), making it a significant contributor. Chromium (~0.8 wt%) and Zr (~0.6 wt%) are near the 0.5 wt% threshold below which Helbig typically excludes elements (helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 12-14). However, for FLAIME's purposes:

- **Mn** has high recycling rates (>50%) and is classified as noncritical by DOE (helbig2018supplyrisksassociated pages 13-15, bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 133-136), but it is flagged as a near-term concern in nuclear materials contexts due to projected peak production around 2020–2030 (hartmann2023strategicplandecrease pages 54-56, hartmann2023strategicplandecrease pages 1-5). Its Helbig-framework score is moderate.
- **Cr** was identified as peaking in production around 2020 in DOE nuclear-materials projections (hartmann2023strategicplandecrease pages 1-5), suggesting non-trivial long-term supply risk.
- **Zr** is not on the DOE critical list but is used in nuclear and aerospace applications that create competing demand.

The CMDRI should transparently report each element's contribution via a waterfall chart showing how alloy-level risk is composed. This prevents the perception that risk is merely shifted from one element to another. The Helbig 2020 study on superalloys demonstrates that the cost-share aggregation method can dramatically change alloy rankings when expensive minor elements dominate—a relevant consideration if Zr powder commands premium pricing (helbig2020supplyriskconsiderations pages 12-14).

### Priority Improvements for Phase I

The three most impactful changes for a Phase I proposal are:

1. **Add Scalmalloy as a secondary aluminum comparator** to make the supply-risk reduction story compelling. A 25% reduction versus Scalmalloy is both meaningful and easily achievable by eliminating Sc entirely.

2. **Decompose CMDRI into element-level contributions** with a transparent waterfall visualization. This directly addresses reviewer concerns about risk-shifting and demonstrates methodological rigor.

3. **Align KPI language with DOE's two-axis criticality framework** (importance to energy × supply risk), explicitly crosswalking CMDRI scores to DOE classifications (bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 133-136, bauer20232023criticalmaterials pages 16-18). DOE reports frame criticality through application-driven lenses (iams2025materialchallengesin pages 7-11), so connecting alloy-level scores to downstream energy applications (e.g., lightweighting for vehicles, heat exchangers for energy systems) will resonate with reviewers.

### Phase II Target Refinement

The Phase II target of "≥40% mean reduction across 3+ families" should specify the aggregation method and data vintage, given that Helbig's review found that normalization approaches that set bounds from the assessed material set can distort scores and make cross-study comparison unreliable (helbig2021anoverviewof pages 18-20). Committing to annual data updates and reporting multiple aggregation schemes would ensure Phase II results are robust and reproducible.

### Note on Mulukutla et al. (2024)

While the ArXiv preprint 2409.15391 by Mulukutla et al. (2024) was not directly retrievable in this search, the underlying Helbig 12-indicator methodology it employs is thoroughly documented in Helbig et al. (2018, 2020, 2021) as reviewed above. The FLAIME proposal should ensure that Mulukutla's specific adaptations—if any—to the Helbig framework for alloy-design contexts are clearly documented, particularly regarding element coverage, data vintage, and aggregation choice.

## Summary

The Helbig 12-indicator framework is a sound but incomplete foundation for the CMDRI KPI. It provides the most granular supply-risk assessment available, but it should be paired with the Ferro & Bonollo alloy criticality index for compositionally interpretable alloy-level scoring and explicitly mapped to DOE's two-axis criticality terminology. The proposed ≥25% SRmean reduction target versus AlSi10Mg is arithmetically fragile because AlSi10Mg is already a low-criticality alloy; adding Scalmalloy as a comparator would dramatically strengthen the narrative. Element-level decomposition, multiple aggregation reporting, and uncertainty analysis are essential for methodological credibility. The Cu-Cr-Zr vs. Be-Cu comparison is the stronger supply-risk story of the two alloy families and should be given commensurate visibility in the proposal.
