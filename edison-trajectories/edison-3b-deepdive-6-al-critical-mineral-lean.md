## Summary
A defensible “critical‑mineral‑lean alternative” claim for new printable aluminum (Al) alloys is strongest when (i) the Al alloy avoids or minimizes high‑supply‑risk elements (notably Co, Ni, REEs used in high-value applications, and Sc), (ii) retains strength/creep resistance and microstructural stability into the practical substitution window for many brackets/housings/powertrain parts (roughly 200–300 °C), and (iii) is compatible with scalable manufacturing (casting and/or LPBF) without extensive heat treatment (a cost and energy lever). Criticality depends on supply risk, economic importance, and lack of substitutes rather than crustal abundance alone, so substitution arguments should be linked to specific incumbent alloys and temperature/load regimes. (czerwinski2022criticalmineralsfor pages 2-4)

The most defensible Al “critical‑mineral‑lean” families supported by the retrieved evidence are: (1) Al–Ce casting and AM‑compatible alloys for powertrain/high‑temperature Al applications (which also create demand for low‑value Ce byproduct streams), (2) Sc‑free Zr‑strengthened printable Al–Mg–Zr (Addalloy‑class) and Al–Zr precipitation‑strengthened alloys as Scalmalloy/Al–Sc alternatives where peak strength can be traded for removing Sc, and (3) Al–Mn–Cr–Zr PBF‑LB alloys designed to be crack‑resistant and thermally stable using non‑Co/non‑Ni solutes. (sims2017highperformancealuminum–cerium pages 1-3, mehta2022al–mn–cr–zrbasedalloystailored pages 1-2, croteau2018microstructureandmechanical pages 1-2)

## 1) Al–Ce alloys (ORNL et al.): what they target and what they can replace
**Targeted applications.** ORNL’s Al–Ce alloys were explicitly positioned for transportation/powertrain castings such as automotive cylinder heads, turbocharger housings, impellers, and heavy‑duty diesel heads/blocks, where local temperatures can reach ~270 °C. (sims2016ceriumbasedintermetallicstrengthenedaluminum pages 1-3)

**Performance and temperature capability.** In Sims et al. (2017), Al–Ce alloys (6–16 wt% Ce) showed room‑temperature strengths approaching ~UTS 400 MPa and ~YS 320–340 MPa (in extruded conditions) and retained substantial strength at temperature (≈80% retention at 240 °C; >60% YS retention and >40% UTS retention at 300 °C), with microstructural stability reported above 500 °C due to stable Al11Ce3‑based intermetallic architectures. (sims2017highperformancealuminum–cerium pages 1-3)

**Substitution claim that is defensible from the sources.** The most direct substitution case supported here is not “replacing Ni/Co superalloys,” but (a) replacing conventional commercial Al casting alloys in 200–300 °C regimes, and (b) enabling replacement of heavier steels/cast irons in elevated‑temperature cast components. (sims2016ceriumbasedintermetallicstrengthenedaluminum pages 1-3, sims2016ceriumbasedintermetallicstrengthenedaluminum pages 10-12)

**Critical minerals angle.** The Ce story is best framed as *byproduct rare‑earth utilization* (and supply‑chain stabilization) rather than eliminating all REEs: Ce is abundant/low‑cost and can reduce reliance on more cost‑prohibitive additions like Sc for thermal stability in Al. (sims2016ceriumbasedintermetallicstrengthenedaluminum pages 1-3, sims2017highperformancealuminum–cerium pages 1-3)

## 2) DuAlumin‑3D (ORNL): motivation, composition, and application target
**Nominal composition and motivation.** DuAlumin‑3D is reported as Al‑9Ce‑4Ni‑0.5Mn‑1Zr (wt%). ORNL’s motivation was to leverage rapid solidification in LPBF to obtain a refined and thermally stable microstructure because most Al alloys lose substantial strength above ~200 °C; the ORNL report explicitly frames high‑strength elevated‑temperature Al as desirable to replace heavier and more expensive titanium alloys. (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 1-9, plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 9-12)

**Target applications and performance window.** A dedicated report evaluated DuAlumin‑3D for additive manufacturing of automotive engine pistons (and powertrain parts), testing tensile properties up to ~350 °C and discussing performance as attractive up to ~400 °C in an engine‑development context. (plotkowski2025additivelymanufacturinghightemperature pages 8-12, wang2025lowmassandhighefficiency pages 20-25)

**Critical‑mineral‑lean defensibility.** DuAlumin‑3D is *not* Ni‑lean (it intentionally contains ~4 wt% Ni). Thus, it is best used as evidence that printable Al can enter higher‑temperature regimes and displace Ti and some high‑temperature Al incumbents—not as a direct “Ni‑free alternative” exemplar. (plotkowski2025additivelymanufacturinghightemperature pages 8-12, plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 1-9)

## 3) Al–Mn–Cr–(Zr) printable alloys: Co‑free (and Ni‑free) strengthening for LPBF
A peer‑reviewed PBF‑LB study introduced Al–Mn–Cr–Zr alloys designed for crack resistance in AM while retaining solute in solid solution. Reported alloys include (wt%) Al‑4.8Mn‑0.47Zr, Al‑4.9Mn‑0.8Cr, and Al‑5.0Mn‑0.8Cr‑0.60Zr, reaching ~99.5% density; as‑printed hardness up to ~102 HV and aged hardness ~130 HV (678 K ageing). Hardness was reported retained to 523 K (~250 °C), consistent with a “moderate‑temperature structural” substitution regime. (mehta2022al–mn–cr–zrbasedalloystailored pages 1-2, mehta2022al–mn–cr–zrbasedalloystailored pages 2-3)

The defensible incumbent replacement claim, from the retrieved text, is primarily **replacement of crack‑prone conventional high‑strength wrought Al alloys in LPBF** (e.g., 6xxx/7xxx families that hot‑crack) rather than direct replacement of Ni/Co superalloys. (mehta2022al–mn–cr–zrbasedalloystailored pages 1-2)

## 4) Sc‑free Scalmalloy alternatives: Al–Mg–Zr / Al–Zr as Sc‑avoiding printable families
**Al–Mg–Zr (Addalloy‑class) performance.** Acta Materialia work demonstrated near‑fully dense LPBF Al‑3.60Mg‑1.18Zr and Al‑3.66Mg‑1.57Zr (wt%) with peak‑aged YS ~349–365 MPa and UTS ~382–389 MPa, with elongations ~17–24% (and one specimen achieving ~376 MPa YS, ~393 MPa UTS, ~27.8% elongation). Strengthening is by solid‑solution Mg plus L12 Al3Zr precipitation, and Zr also enables crack‑free builds through grain refinement via primary Al3Zr. (croteau2018microstructureandmechanical pages 6-8)

**Explicit Sc‑avoidance framing and comparison to Scalmalloy.** A dissertation summary of Addalloy™ (Al‑3.6Mg‑1.2Zr) reports ~353 MPa YS, ~386 MPa UTS, ~18.6% elongation and directly juxtaposes it against Scalmalloy® (Al‑4.5Mg‑0.5Mn‑0.7Sc‑0.4Zr) with higher reported strength (~520 MPa YS, ~530 MPa UTS). This supports the key policy‑relevant point: **Sc‑free printable Al alloys exist and can be positioned as “critical‑mineral‑lean” alternatives where a strength penalty is acceptable.** (roscher2022developmentofhighstrength pages 24-26)

**Al–Zr (without Sc) viability.** Elemental‑blend SLM work shows Al–Zr is printable and precipitation‑strengthened (L12 Al3Zr), but Al–Zr is measurably lower in hardness/strength than Al–Sc and Al–Sc–Zr variants; replacing part or all Sc with Zr is presented as a cost‑reduction pathway with performance tradeoffs. (glerum2020synthesisofprecipitationstrengthened pages 6-7)

## 5) Where Co/Ni incumbents overlap ≤300 °C and how to argue substitution without over-claiming
**Ni‑based superalloys at “not‑that‑hot” duty.** Alloy 718 (Pyromet 718) is explicitly used for aerospace fasteners, with operating temperature guidance up to 900 °F (~482 °C) and in some processing routes up to 1200 °F (~649 °C). This means Alloy 718 fasteners necessarily cover service envelopes that include ≤300 °C, even if many are specified for higher‑temperature margins; fasteners/brackets are therefore a plausible application class where printable Al alloys could substitute when the design temperature is in the ≤300 °C regime and strength/fatigue/corrosion requirements can be met. (voort1997metallographiccharacterizationof pages 1-3)

**Co‑based wear alloys as a boundary condition.** Stellite (Co‑based) alloys are used for wear/hardfacing and valve applications; sources show wear testing at 250 °C and typical use as wear‑resistant materials at elevated temperatures. This evidence is useful mainly to bound the substitution argument: Al alloys are unlikely to replace classic Co‑based hardfacing in severe wear/high contact‑stress service, but may replace surrounding structural hardware (brackets/housings) where wear is not the governing failure mode and temperatures are ≤300 °C. (kapoor2012hightemperaturehardnessand pages 14-19, hasan2016thebasicsof pages 1-2)

## 6) State of LPBF Al beyond AlSi10Mg (printable compositions with temperature targets)
A sustainability‑oriented review highlights that LPBF Al has historically been dominated by near‑eutectic Al–Si powders (AlSi10Mg class), while research and commercialization are expanding to tailored compositions and dispersion/grain‑refining strategies (e.g., Scalmalloy; A20X (Al–Cu–Ti with TiB2); Elementum 3D RAM‑modified Al6061/Al7050). The same review identifies Constellium Aheadd® HT1 (Al–Mn–Ni–Cu–Zr) with claimed service temperatures up to ~260 °C, which directly overlaps the substitution window of interest. (yadegari2025aluminumlaseradditive pages 14-15, yadegari2025aluminumlaseradditive pages 12-14)

## Quantification (Co/Ni displaced) and DOE “critical minerals” framing: current evidence limits
The retrieved sources did **not** provide credible system‑level quantification of “how much Co/Ni is used” in specific connector/bracket applications or how much could be displaced, nor did they provide a DOE program document explicitly framing printable Al alloy discovery as a critical‑minerals demand‑reduction lever. The strongest explicit motivation language retrieved is ORNL’s substitution framing of high‑temperature printable Al as a replacement for heavier and more expensive Ti alloys. (plotkowski2024highstrengthaluminum pages 1-9)

## Practical takeaways for a defensible “critical-mineral-lean Al alternatives” case
1. **For ≤300 °C structural/powertrain castings:** Al–Ce families have strong evidence for high‑temperature strength retention and explicit transportation/powertrain targets (e.g., cylinder heads/turbo housings up to ~270 °C). (sims2016ceriumbasedintermetallicstrengthenedaluminum pages 1-3, sims2017highperformancealuminum–cerium pages 1-3)
2. **For Sc elimination in high‑strength printable Al:** Al–Mg–Zr / Al–Zr (Addalloy‑class) are defensible Sc‑free alternatives to Scalmalloy/Al–Sc in applications that can tolerate lower peak strength in exchange for avoiding Sc. (croteau2018microstructureandmechanical pages 6-8, roscher2022developmentofhighstrength pages 24-26, glerum2020synthesisofprecipitationstrengthened pages 6-7)
3. **For Co/Ni avoidance in printable strengthening additions:** Al–Mn–Cr–Zr shows a credible pathway to crack‑resistant, thermally stable printable Al using non‑Co/non‑Ni solutes, with evidence of hardness retention to ~250 °C. (mehta2022al–mn–cr–zrbasedalloystailored pages 1-2, mehta2022al–mn–cr–zrbasedalloystailored pages 2-3)
4. **For incumbent Co/Ni displacement framing:** Focus on *components currently over‑specified in Ni/Co alloys for manufacturability/corrosion/fatigue margins but operating at ≤300 °C* (e.g., some fasteners/brackets) rather than claiming replacement of high‑wear Stellite hardfacing or high‑temperature turbine hot‑section superalloys. (kapoor2012hightemperaturehardnessand pages 14-19, voort1997metallographiccharacterizationof pages 1-3)
