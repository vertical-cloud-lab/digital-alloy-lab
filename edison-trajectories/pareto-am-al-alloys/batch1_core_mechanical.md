## Reported as-built properties (YS, UTS, elongation)
Across the studies with explicit as-built values, typical as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg is reported in the approximate bands YS ≈ 180–265 MPa, UTS ≈ 340–446 MPa, elongation ≈ 0.7–6% depending strongly on orientation and scan/border strategy (garciazapata2024effectsofbuilding pages 12-13, zhu2023microstructuralanalysisof pages 7-9).

* Example baseline (no explicit orientation reported): Zhu et al. measured as-built YS 231.1±0.7 MPa, UTS 442.2±1.7 MPa, EL 6.1±0.4% for an ambient platform; with 150 °C platform preheat YS increased to 253.4±0.7 MPa while EL decreased to 4.4±0.2% (interpreted as in‑situ aging/hardening during the build). (zhu2023microstructuralanalysisof pages 7-9)

* Orientation (build direction) strongly affects ductility and (sometimes) strength: García‑Zapata et al. reported, for *as-built with border scanning* (60 µm), Flat60: YS 236±6 MPa, UTS 429±17 MPa, EL 5.71±0.50%; Edge60: 238±5, 360±3, 3.58±0.13%; Vertical60: 260±2, 344±4, 1.80±0.06%. (garciazapata2024effectsofbuilding pages 12-13)

* Scan/path strategy effects (reported as border scanning): removing the border scan substantially reduced properties; for 60 µm builds without border scanning, Flat60WB: 178±3, 347±4, 3.98±0.11%; Vertical60WB: 188±4, 254±12, 0.70±0.07%. This shows that a scan strategy element (border scanning) can change UTS by ~+80–+100 MPa and increase elongation by ~+1–+2% absolute, depending on orientation. (garciazapata2024effectsofbuilding pages 12-13)

* Scan-strategy/parameter effects (reported as hatch/rotation “67SS”): a compilation table in Paul (2022) reports as-built AlSi10Mg properties for different parameter sets with a 67° scan strategy (67SS). For instance, 30LT/100HS/67SS‑H: YS 313.8 MPa, UTS 469.4 MPa, EL 5.6%, and for the same 67SS but different parameters/orientation, 60LT/190HS/67SS‑V: YS 213.9 MPa, UTS 340.3 MPa, EL 3.2%. Horizontal samples generally showed higher elongation than vertical in the same parameter family. (paul2022mechanicalanisotropyin pages 80-84)

* Orientation mapping across many spatial directions: Monkova et al. (2025) (ISO 6892‑1) reported that their lowest-strength orientation (“Y‑series”) had YS 243 MPa and UTS 348 MPa in the as-built state (elongation not present in the excerpt). (monkova2025apreliminarystudy pages 25-27)

## Reported heat-treated properties (YS, UTS, elongation)
### (1) Direct aging / T5-type treatments
Zhu et al. reported post-build T5 at 170 °C × 2 h with: ambient-platform build YS 266.8±1.8 MPa, UTS 464.7±3.5 MPa, EL 4.9±0.4%; and 150 °C platform build YS 263.9±0.7 MPa, UTS 451.3±0.8 MPa, EL 4.2±0.1%. This indicates modest strengthening vs as-built for the ambient-platform condition, but minimal benefit for the preheated-platform condition (consistent with in‑situ aging already occurring during the build). (zhu2023microstructuralanalysisof pages 7-9)

### (2) T6-type (solution + age) treatments; rapid vs conventional solution
Zhu et al. compared two T6 routes:
* RST‑T6 (520 °C × 10 min + 170 °C × 4 h): ambient-platform YS 251.3±0.2 MPa, UTS 318.8±2.7 MPa, EL 9.6±2.3%; 150 °C platform YS 250.1±0.2 MPa, UTS 324.0±1.4 MPa, EL 10.1±0.8%. (zhu2023microstructuralanalysisof pages 7-9)
* CST‑T6 (520 °C × 2 h + 170 °C × 8 h): ambient-platform YS 233.4±1.5 MPa, UTS 285.8±0.8 MPa, EL 10.8±1.1%; 150 °C platform YS 237.7±0.5 MPa, UTS 294.8±0.1 MPa, EL 11.7±0.4%. (zhu2023microstructuralanalysisof pages 7-9)

Compared to as-built, these T6 routes markedly reduced UTS (≈ 440 → 286–324 MPa) but increased elongation (≈ 4–6% → ≈ 10–12%), i.e., a strength–ductility tradeoff typical of Si-network coarsening/collapse and precipitation hardening behavior in AlSi10Mg. (zhu2023microstructuralanalysisof pages 7-9)

Independent evidence consistent with these magnitudes includes Liu et al. (2022), who reported as-printed ranges YS 295–199 MPa, UTS 439–363 MPa, EL 6.60–4.29% and, after T6, ranges YS 185–165 MPa, UTS 238–211 MPa, EL 10.80–9.27%, with reduced anisotropy after T6. (liu2022microstructureandmechanical pages 9-11)

### (3) Stress relief, HIP, and combined sequences (including orientation effects)
Ngo (2020) reported post-processing sequences (averaged) and also discussed orientation (XY vs Z) and defect sensitivity:
* SR-only: YS 199±26 MPa, UTS 298±10 MPa, EL 17.5±7.3%.
* SR+T6: YS 254±15 MPa, UTS 294±16 MPa, EL 4.9±2.4%.
* SR+HIP+T6 (HIP 102 MPa/510 °C/135 min; T6 530 °C/6 h solution + water quench + 160 °C/6 h aging): YS 260±4 MPa, UTS 300±38 MPa, EL 10.8±5.3%. (ngo2020effectsofbuild pages 81-86)

Orientation effect example in the same study: under SR-only, an XY specimen reached ~26.6% elongation while a Z specimen showed ~15.3% elongation, and Z-oriented samples in some conditions were specifically associated with lack-of-fusion defects on fracture surfaces (which depressed strength/ductility). (ngo2020effectsofbuild pages 81-86)

## Effect of build orientation and scan strategy (summary)
### Build orientation
* In as-built states, vertical/BD-related orientations often exhibit substantially reduced elongation relative to horizontal/flat orientations; e.g., García‑Zapata vertical vs flat (with border scan, 60 µm) EL 1.80% vs 5.71% (and UTS 344 vs 429 MPa). (garciazapata2024effectsofbuilding pages 12-13)
* Orientation-related anisotropy is also evident in Liu et al. (as-printed DRYS 38.52%, DRUTS 18.89%, DREL 42.37%), and was reported to decrease after T6. (liu2022microstructureandmechanical pages 9-11)
* Some datasets show parameter-dependent reversals in which vertical exhibits higher YS but much lower elongation (e.g., García‑Zapata at 60 µm with border scan: vertical YS 260 MPa vs flat YS 236 MPa, but vertical EL 1.80% vs flat EL 5.71%). (garciazapata2024effectsofbuilding pages 12-13)

### Scan strategy / scan-path modifications
* Border scanning (a scan strategy element) materially improves tensile properties versus “without border scan” in García‑Zapata et al., especially for UTS and elongation across orientations. (garciazapata2024effectsofbuilding pages 12-13)
* Rotation-angle / strategy comparisons in Dietrich et al. indicate that remelting scan strategies (re0, re90) produced lower and more variable tensile strengths and less favorable pore morphologies than single-scan strategies (conti/isl/wobb), while 67° was identified as the best rotation angle but with small, not significant differences among angles. The excerpt provides overall averages (Rp0.2 230.9 MPa; UTS 399.2 MPa; A20 3.1%) but not full per-strategy numeric breakdown. (dietrich2024influenceofscanning pages 11-13, dietrich2024influenceofscanning pages 13-16)
* A parameter/strategy compilation (67SS) shows that strength can vary widely with hatch spacing/layer thickness even at the same nominal scan rotation strategy, e.g., YS 189.6→313.8 MPa and UTS 323.0→469.4 MPa across 67SS conditions, with horizontal samples typically more ductile than vertical. (paul2022mechanicalanisotropyin pages 80-84)

## How Scalmalloy compares to AlSi10Mg (LPBF)

Across the extracted studies, Scalmalloy exhibits substantially higher strength than AlSi10Mg at comparable process route, particularly after aging: Scalmalloy YS/UTS commonly reaches ~470–535 MPa / ~507–560 MPa after aging, with elongation typically ~3–11%, whereas AlSi10Mg as-built is ~260–268 MPa YS with ~333–430 MPa UTS and very low elongation (~1.5–2%) in the cited primary studies. (ren2024studyonmicrostructure pages 8-10, janus2025microstructureandmechanical pages 14-15, janus2025microstructureandmechanical pages 12-14, aboulkhair2016themicrostructureand pages 5-6, megahed2022effectofheat pages 11-13)

Post-processing trends differ: Scalmalloy generally strengthens markedly with direct/peak/natural aging (at the cost of ductility), while AlSi10Mg often loses strength after T6/T6-like or HIP-based post-processing but gains ductility (e.g., elongation up to ~21–30% in cited examples) at a large strength penalty. (ren2024studyonmicrostructure pages 8-10, mehta2021laserpowderbed pages 7-10, mehta2021laserpowderbed pages 1-2, megahed2022effectofheat pages 11-13, merino2021multiplecomparativeheat pages 10-14)

## Scope and key limitation
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) of *unmodified* 2xxx Al–Cu alloys (e.g., AA2024, AA2219) is still constrained by hot/solidification cracking due to their wide solidification range and grain-boundary liquid-film failure during terminal solidification. The retrieved corpus contains multiple primary LPBF AA2024 studies and strong evidence for composition-modified (Zr-, Ti-, CaB6-, TiB2-assisted) Al–Cu(-Mg)(-Mn) alloys, but **direct, quantitative LPBF AA2219 mechanical-property papers were not obtainable in this run** (one key LPBF AA2219 + nano-TiO2 paper was flagged as unobtainable). Therefore, AA2219 is discussed mainly through general Al–Cu cracking physics and transferable mitigation approaches supported by Al–Cu evidence.

## Hot-cracking in LPBF 2xxx Al–Cu: mechanisms and solutions
### Mechanistic understanding (why 2xxx cracks)
Across AA2024 LPBF studies, cracking is consistently tied to (i) **wide solidification temperature range**, (ii) **columnar grain structures** that concentrate strain along grain boundaries, and (iii) **insufficient liquid feeding** in the mushy zone—leading to grain-boundary separation (hot tearing/solidification cracking) (xi2024circumventingsolidificationcracking pages 1-2, yao2023lpbfformed2024alalloys pages 1-2). In AA2024, Cu segregation to grain boundaries and high thermal gradients/time in a brittle temperature regime further promote liquation-type cracking and hot tearing (kumar2021additivemanufacturingof pages 16-22).

Most supported “solutions” and what they achieve:
1. **Parameter-window control (process optimization) can produce dense, crack-free AA2024, but with moderate properties.**
   - Xi et al. report **dense, crack-free AA2024** at **200 W and 100 mm/s** with **>98.1% densification** and as-built **~110 HV0.2, 300 ± 15 MPa UTS, ~3% elongation** (xi2024circumventingsolidificationcracking pages 1-2).
   - Yao et al. show that an **intermediate optimum energy density** (reported **741 J/mm³**) yields **99.77% density**, **330 ± 7 MPa UTS**, and **9 ± 0.6% elongation**, while too low or too high energy density is associated with cracking/defects (yao2023lpbfformed2024alalloys pages 1-2, yao2023lpbfformed2024alalloys media 41d84e26, yao2023lpbfformed2024alalloys pages 14-15).

2. **Grain refinement via alloy/nanoparticle additions is repeatedly identified as the more scalable route to crack suppression in Al–Cu.**
   - Zr (and Sc/Zr-type) additions promote **Al3Zr formation**, **heterogeneous nucleation**, and **equiaxed grains**, reducing hot-cracking tendency and enabling much higher tensile properties in Zr-modified 2xxx datasets (dixit2022laseradditivemanufacturing pages 15-16, zhang2023selectivelasermelting pages 1-2).

3. **Thermal management (preheating, reducing gradients) and scan-strategy design are supportive but usually insufficient alone.**
   - Elevated bulk temperatures/preheating are discussed as a way to reduce thermal gradients and crack driving forces; one Al–Cu–Mn–Mg–Zr study explicitly used **150 °C substrate preheat** while achieving high density and moderate tensile properties (zhang2023selectivelasermelting pages 2-4).
   - Shorter scan lengths and scan-strategy changes are suggested to reduce thermal stress accumulation (ellis2019additivepowdermanufacture pages 62-65, xi2024circumventingsolidificationcracking pages 1-2).

## Visual evidence (cracks vs. processing window)
Yao et al. provide a table of tensile properties versus energy density and microscopy images showing crack defects at non-optimal energy inputs; this supports the “intermediate-energy window” concept and links it to quantitative tensile outcomes (yao2023lpbfformed2024alalloys media 41d84e26).

## What this implies for AA2219 specifically
AA2219 (Al–Cu) shares the **same core cracking driver** (wide freezing range and mushy-zone feeding failure) emphasized for Al–Cu alloys in LPBF (xi2024circumventingsolidificationcracking pages 1-2). While AA2219-specific LPBF mechanical-property datasets were not available here, the strongest evidence-based, transferable pathways to crack mitigation are:
- **Move away from “unmodified AA2219” toward grain-refined chemistries** (e.g., Zr or Sc/Zr-type inoculation to promote equiaxed grains) (dixit2022laseradditivemanufacturing pages 15-16, zhang2023selectivelasermelting pages 1-2).
- **Use grain-refiner particles/nanoparticles** (Ti-based, CaB6, TiB2) shown to refine grains and reduce/eliminate cracking in Al–Cu(-Mg) systems (pourkhorshid2025laserbasedadditivemanufacturing pages 14-17, yao2023lpbfformed2024alalloys pages 1-2).
- **Use process optimization to stay in a crack-minimizing energy window**, recognizing that excessive energy can introduce porosity/keyholing and stress concentrators (yao2023lpbfformed2024alalloys pages 14-15, xi2024circumventingsolidificationcracking pages 1-2).

## Strategies used to overcome / mitigate solidification (hot) cracking

### 1) Composition modification to promote eutectic solidification and feeding (Si addition)
- For Al7075, Si additions are repeatedly reported to reduce cracking by **(i) lowering melting temperature/solidification range via eutectic formation, (ii) improving melt fluidity/feeding, and (iii) refining grains**; cracking was reported absent above a threshold Si level in compiled studies. (wang2020selectivelasermelting pages 35-37, dixit2022laseradditivemanufacturing pages 16-18)
- In a dedicated 7075+Si experimental study, **microcracks were completely suppressed at 5 wt.% Si** (and also suppressed at higher Si), but excessive Si led to brittleness/self-breakage and poor ductility (e.g., 9%Si: 1% elongation). (otani2020effectsofthe pages 4-6, otani2020effectsofthe pages 6-7)

### 2) Grain refinement by nanoparticle inoculation / powder-surface decoration (e.g., TiC)
- Powder “decoration” with **TiC nanoparticles** is used as a nucleating-agent approach to promote **equiaxed, refined grains** and suppress hot cracking in AA7075; in one study, **2.5 ma% TiC enabled crack-free samples** in their microstructural observations under optimized parameters, with large mechanical-property scatter reflecting remaining defects/process sensitivity. (heiland2021requirementsforprocessing pages 10-12, heiland2021requirementsforprocessing pages 12-15)
- The same work notes practical limits: **grain refinement alone may be insufficient** and the effective fraction of nanoparticles acting as nuclei can be very small (~0.1% estimated). (heiland2021requirementsforprocessing pages 12-15)
- A separate LPBF strategy uses a **synergistic grain-refining modification (TiC + TiH2)** where in situ L12-Al3Ti plus TiC increases heterogeneous nucleation and growth restriction, eliminating columnar grains and reducing hot cracks in Al–Zn–Mg–Cu alloys. (liu2022enhancedstrengthand pages 10-11)

### 3) Zr (and related) inoculation / microalloying to form Al3Zr nucleants
- Hydrogen-stabilized **Zr nanoscale nucleants** added to Al7075 powder were reported to form **Al3Zr** during melting, providing heterogeneous nucleation sites and promoting fine equiaxed grains; this was proposed to accommodate semisolid strain and prevent crack initiation/growth. (wang2020selectivelasermelting pages 35-37)
- Review-compiled data show markedly improved tensile properties for **7075+Zr (T6)**, consistent with a grain-refined, less crack-dominated microstructure. (wang2020selectivelasermelting pages 47-49)

### 4) Process optimization and scan/thermal management (beneficial but often insufficient alone)
- LPBF parameter optimization can raise density (>99%), but **hot cracks can persist in AA7075 despite high density**, indicating cracking is not removed purely by densification. (stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 1-6)
- **Baseplate preheating** reduces thermal gradients (G/R), but in AA7075, preheating to **300 °C did not change the fracture mode of cracking**, illustrating limitations of thermal management alone. (stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 27-31)

### 5) Post-processing: HIP (crack closure) vs microstructure/aging tradeoffs
- In AA7075, HIP conditions were selected to close pores/cracks by plastic flow and creep, but HIP was also reported as **ineffective at healing long cracks** and can drive the alloy toward an **overaged hardness state**, with segregation patterns and columnar grain shape largely unchanged. (stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 27-31, stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 1-6)

## Mechanistic basis for why cracking occurs (context for mitigation)
A key mechanistic picture for LPBF AA7075 is **liquid-film rupture along high-angle boundaries between columnar grains**, promoted by **microsegregation of Zn, Mg, Cu (and even minor Si) that stabilizes low-melting interdendritic films**; crack “healing” by liquid feeding is impeded by high strain rate during processing. (stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 27-31, stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 1-6)

## Quantitative thermal / thermal-stability properties
Across the retrieved AM literature, “thermal properties” are primarily reported as **phase equilibria / solidification metrics** and **high-temperature microstructural stability/strength retention**, rather than thermal conductivity/CTE.

### Eutectic temperature/composition and solubility (relevant to AM solidification)
- The Al–Ce eutectic is reported near **~10–12 wt% Ce** (zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3, plotkowski2017evaluationofan pages 1-6). A near-eutectic value of **~10.6 wt% Ce** with a eutectic temperature **644.5 ± 0.6 °C** is reported (hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3).
- Cerium has **extremely low solubility** in Al: **<0.01 wt%** (zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3) and reported **~0.005 wt% at the eutectic temperature** (hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3). This drives strengthening toward stable intermetallic dispersions (Al11Ce3) rather than precipitate sequences typical of age-hardenable Al alloys.

### Diffusion / coarsening resistance (key thermal-stability metric)
- A very low Ce diffusion coefficient in Al at **500 °C** is reported as **~5.9×10−18 m²/s** (zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3) and as **5.7×10−14 cm²/s** (hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3). (These values are both cited in the AM literature as evidence of slow Ce transport and coarsening resistance.)
- In LPBF contexts, high cooling rates are cited as **~10^5–10^6 K/s** (hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3), producing nanoscale Al/Al11Ce3 morphologies that are relatively resistant to coarsening.

### Quantitative high-temperature exposure / stability and strength-retention statements
- No observable coarsening after **300 °C for 24 h** is reported for laser-melted near-eutectectic Al–Ce (plotkowski2017evaluationofan pages 1-6) and similarly in the DED-relevant laser-melt study (plotkowski2017evaluationofan pages 10-15).
- Reported mechanical retention metrics for Al–Ce include:
  - **~80% retention of room-temperature mechanical properties at 240 °C** (zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3).
  - Literature cited in an AM-focused study: **>60% of room-temperature yield strength and >40% of tensile strength retained at 300 °C** (hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3).
- Longer-term high-temperature exposure statements summarized in a system-level reference include **recovery of room-temperature properties after 500 °C exposure for 1000 h**, and “room temperature properties remain stable” up to **400 °C** exposures in some Ce-containing alloys (weiss2020compositesandalloys pages 1-4). (These are not LPBF-specific but are used to motivate Al–Ce for AM high-temperature applications.)

### Thermal conductivity / CTE
- **Thermal conductivity and coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE)** values for AM Al–Ce alloys were **not located** in the retrieved primary AM papers and reviews. The available “thermal” data are predominantly **phase/eutectic temperatures, solubility/diffusion, and thermal exposure stability** (hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3, zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3, weiss2020compositesandalloys pages 1-4, plotkowski2017evaluationofan pages 1-6).

## Why Al–Ce is interesting for AM (mechanistic rationale)
The Al–Ce system is repeatedly highlighted as attractive for AM because it aligns with core AM constraints: **printability (cracking)**, **rapid-solidification microstructures**, and **high-temperature stability**.

1. **Printability and reduced hot-cracking susceptibility**
   - Near-eutectic Al–Ce has a **narrow solidification/freezing range** and eutectic solidification, which is associated with improved fluidity and reduced hot tearing/solidification cracking (plotkowski2017evaluationofan pages 1-6, zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 11-12, zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3).
   - Crack-free, high-density processing windows are reported for near-eutectic Al–Ce compositions in laser processing and LPBF contexts (hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3, zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 11-12).

2. **Rapid-solidification‑enabled nanoscale eutectics for strengthening**
   - LPBF produces **nanoscale Al/Al11Ce3** (ribbon/skeleton) eutectics enabling **Orowan-type strengthening**; this underpins the strength increase of LPBF Al–10Ce relative to cast material (zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 11-12, zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3).

3. **Exceptional thermal stability / coarsening resistance**
   - The combination of **very low Ce solubility** and **very slow Ce diffusion** supports a stable dispersion of Ce-containing intermetallics (Al11Ce3) with resistance to Ostwald ripening (hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3, zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3).
   - Laser-processed Al–Ce shows **no coarsening after 300 °C/24 h** (plotkowski2017evaluationofan pages 1-6), and the literature reports substantial elevated-temperature strength retention (e.g., at **240–300 °C**) (zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3, hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3).

4. **Cost/supply advantage relative to other rare-earth strategies**
   - Ce is described as **abundant** and **low cost** (often a by-product), motivating its use as an economically scalable alloying element for AM Al alloys (liu2024reviewoflaser pages 2-4, zhou2020laserpowderbed pages 1-3, hesselmann2022effectofprecipitationforming pages 1-3).

## Limitations of the available evidence
- The retrieved AM papers provided **robust tensile and hardness data**, plus diffusion/eutectic and thermal-exposure stability evidence, but **did not provide** thermal conductivity, CTE, heat capacity, or comprehensive fatigue datasets for AM Al–Ce alloys.
- Temperature-dependent tensile data for LPBF Al–Ce–Mg alloys are available primarily in plotted form (Figure 7) rather than as a fully tabulated dataset (sisco2021microstructureandproperties media 2595d5a8).

## Notes on the highest-ranked systems (why they rank highly)
1. **Al–Mg–Si–Sc–Zr (compositionally designed for SLM/LPBF)** shows the highest SDI in the retrieved evidence because it combines **moderate–high strength with very high ductility** (UTS 376 MPa, A 35%). The same study also reports a higher-strength but lower-ductility condition (UTS 497 MPa, A 11%), illustrating a tunable strength–ductility tradeoff via aging/processing state. (li2020developingahighstrength pages 13-14)

2. **Zr-modified AA6061 (LPBF)** ranks highly primarily due to **very high elongation in the as-built state** (UTS ~268 MPa, A ~26.5%), yielding a high SDI despite moderate strength. (mehta2021additivemanufacturingand pages 9-10)

3. **Sc/Zr precipitation-strengthened Al–Mg–Sc–Zr systems (Scalmalloy-type)** appear multiple times with strong SDI because they deliver **high UTS near ~480–520 MPa with double-digit elongation** when aged appropriately. Example: rapid aging at 330 °C × 1 h gives UTS 479 MPa and elongation 14.5%. (shen2021effectofheat pages 9-10)

4. **Nanoparticle-inoculated 2xxx (A2024–CaB6, LPBF)** achieves a strong balance (UTS 478 MPa, A 13.2%) by suppressing cracking and refining grains at sufficient CaB6 content; these values are directly confirmed in the paper’s tensile-property table/figure. (mair2022dependenceofmechanical pages 8-9, mair2022dependenceofmechanical media 6a21e270, mair2022dependenceofmechanical media 810c2999)

5. **TiB2-assisted Al–Cu–Mg (A20X, LPBF, T6)** achieves high SDI via ~485 MPa UTS at ~14% elongation after T6, consistent with grain refinement and precipitation-strengthening concepts in these Ti–B/TiB2-containing AM alloys. (belelli2021investigationontwo pages 9-12)

6. **7xxx modified with Si (e.g., 7075 + 5 wt.% Si)** shows very high strength (UTS 537 MPa) but somewhat lower ductility (9.7%), giving an SDI below the best 2xxx and Sc/Zr systems in this evidence set. (dixit2022laseradditivemanufacturing pages 15-16)

## Limitations (scope of “across all AM Al alloys”)
This ranking is **strictly bounded by the full-text sources successfully retrieved and the explicit (UTS, elongation) pairs extractable from them**. Several important AM Al alloy families (e.g., additional 7xxx variants, oxide-dispersion/nanocomposite systems, some 2xxx/6xxx/7xxx nanoparticle-reinforced reports, and non-LPBF routes like WAAM/DED/binder-jet for high-strength alloys) were identified in search but were **unobtainable or did not yield extractable tensile pairs in the available text**; therefore, they could not be included without risking uncited extrapolation.

Within these constraints, the table provides a transparent, evidence-traceable **best-reported strength–ductility balance ranking** for AM aluminum alloy systems.
