## 1. WAAM/DED-Arc aluminum: achieved tensile properties by common filler wire

### ER2319 (Al–Cu; 2319/2219-family)
WAAM 2319 (ER2319) can reach high aerospace-relevant strengths after aging/T6-type treatments. In a widely cited study, aging at 190 °C for 26 h produced up to ~324 MPa yield strength, ~452 MPa UTS, and ~8% elongation (measured perpendicular to build) when processing avoided columnar grains. (arana2022influenceofdeposition pages 1-2)

A more recent CMT-WAAM study reported solution-aging-treated 2319 tensile properties that varied with height/orientation: the best horizontal/top location achieved YS 325.5 ± 39.5 MPa, UTS 479.2 ± 14.2 MPa, EL 7.4 ± 2.4%, while vertical specimens had comparable YS (318.8 ± 4.8 MPa) but lower elongation (3.9 ± 0.7%). (pan2024highperformance2319aluminum pages 10-12)

For related Al–Cu WAAM (2219) using ER2319 wire, as-deposited strength was much lower (YS 118.6 MPa, UTS 261.3 MPa; elongation not reported in the excerpt), but a T6 condition increased strengths to YS 293.9 MPa and UTS 441.85 MPa (elongation not reported in the excerpt). (yang2023heattreatmentoptimization pages 8-11)

ER2319 is also used in hybrid/dilution concepts (e.g., ER2319 + ER5183 double-wire CMT) to address cracking; in one Al–Cu–Mg alloy example, T6 delivered very high strength but low ductility: horizontal YS/UTS/EL = 387/445/2.7%, vertical = 360/418/1.8%. (fan2022microstructureandmechanical pages 5-8)

**Practical interpretation:** ER2319 WAAM shows the highest *strength potential* among common commercial Al wires in WAAM when coupled to appropriate precipitation heat treatments, but elongation is often modest (and strongly position/orientation dependent) because pores and grain-boundary brittle phases are frequent crack initiators. (pan2024highperformance2319aluminum pages 10-12, arana2022influenceofdeposition pages 1-2)

### ER4043 (Al–5Si)
ER4043 WAAM typically exhibits low yield strength and UTS in the as-deposited state, consistent with a non-heat-treatable Al–Si weld-metal microstructure. A WAAM ER4043 wall showed ~82 MPa YS, ~164 MPa UTS, and ~13.8% elongation (reported as an average with horizontal/vertical nearly the same). (he2023improvementofmicrostructure pages 5-8)

A notable route to “high-elongation” WAAM 4043 is interlayer friction stir processing (FSP), which can eliminate pores and fragment/redistribute the Si-eutectic network. In the same study, WAAM + interlayer FSP raised elongation to 37.5% (horizontal) and 28.8% (vertical) with YS ~87–88 MPa and UTS ~147–149 MPa. (he2023improvementofmicrostructure pages 5-8)

**Practical interpretation:** ER4043 WAAM is best viewed as a *ductility-tunable* system: interlayer mechanical processing can deliver very high elongation, but absolute strength remains low (tens to low hundreds of MPa). (he2023improvementofmicrostructure pages 5-8)

### ER5356 (Al–Mg; 5356/AlMg5)
ER5356 WAAM commonly achieves moderate UTS (~230–275 MPa) with yield strength around ~110–135 MPa and elongation in the low-to-high teens. A CMT-based WAAM study (Table 6) reported per-specimen ranges: YS 109–134 MPa, UTS 230.57–274.88 MPa, elongation 11.14–18.61% (some anomalous tests were flagged in that dataset). (wieczorowski2023characterizationof5356 pages 8-9)

Several other ER5356 WAAM studies highlight anisotropy (often UTS-only in the provided excerpts): for example, an Al–5Mg WAAM-CMT study reported horizontal UTS 265.8 MPa versus vertical UTS 232.4 MPa, attributed to larger/more pores in the vertical direction. (geng2021investigationofmicrostructure pages 7-8)

**Practical interpretation:** ER5356 WAAM tends to provide a “middle ground” between ER4043 and ER2319: stronger than ER4043, typically more ductile than high-strength ER2319/T6 outcomes, but still below peak-strength LPBF Sc-containing alloys. (wieczorowski2023characterizationof5356 pages 8-9, geng2021investigationofmicrostructure pages 7-8)

## 2. Other WAAM aluminum filler wires/alloys (context)

### ER5183 / AA5083-family (Al–Mg–Mn)
A WAAM plate study using ER5183 wire reported strong orientation dependence: longitudinal L specimens ~143 MPa YS, ~295 MPa UTS, ~20% elongation; diagonal D ~123/263/16%; transverse T ~122/215/~7%. (tonelli2021aa5083(al–mg)plates pages 11-14, tonelli2021aa5083(al–mg)plates pages 10-11)

This indicates that WAAM Al–Mg systems can deliver both relatively high UTS (~300 MPa) and good ductility (~20%) in favorable orientations, but can lose substantial ductility when interlayer discontinuities align unfavorably with loading. (tonelli2021aa5083(al–mg)plates pages 11-14, tonelli2021aa5083(al–mg)plates pages 10-11)

### WAAM 7xxx-like Al–Zn–Mg–Cu (7055)
WAAM of a quaternary 7xxx-like alloy can be feasible but, as-deposited, reported properties were low and brittle: average YS 148.3 ± 15.9 MPa, UTS 230.7 ± 12.0 MPa, elongation 3.3 ± 1.0%. (dong2020wirearcadditive pages 31-38, dong2020wirearcadditive pages 38-43)

### 7075 wire-based deposition with nanoparticle-enhanced wire (TiC additions)
A wire-based additive/weld-deposition approach using TiC nanoparticle-enhanced 7075 wire showed poor as-welded/as-deposited properties (YS 246 ± 11 MPa, UTS 349 ± 27 MPa, EL 5 ± 1%), but post-deposition T73 largely restored properties close to parent material levels (YS 411 ± 6 MPa, UTS 462 ± 19 MPa, EL 9 ± 4%). (oropeza2020weldingandadditive pages 5-7)

## 3. How WAAM Al compares to LPBF Al

### LPBF AlSi10Mg (benchmark, widely used)
LPBF AlSi10Mg is typically *stronger than WAAM ER4043/ER5356 in the as-built state*, but ductility can be low unless heat treated. One representative dataset reports: as-built ~260 MPa YS, ~430 MPa UTS, ~2% elongation; after standard T6 ~180/290/9%; and after an alternative high-ductility heat treatment ~100/205/21%. (megahed2022effectofheat pages 11-13)

A broader literature compilation shows as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg ranges of roughly YS 165–322 MPa, UTS 318–460 MPa, elongation ~1.3–9%, with many heat-treated/T6 cases trading strength for ductility (e.g., lower YS/UTS with modestly higher elongation). (salandarirabori2022deformationratedependentbehaviora pages 29-32, salandarirabori2022deformationratedependentbehavior pages 29-32)

### LPBF Sc-containing Al alloys (Scalmalloy-like systems)
LPBF Al–Mg–Sc–Zr alloys (Scalmalloy-like) can exceed typical WAAM properties in strength while retaining usable ductility. One study reported as-built YS/UTS/EL of 321/404/21.3% (Z-oriented) to 352/412/23.6% (H-oriented), and after aging YS/UTS/EL of 527/551/11.2% (Z) to 535/560/11.1% (H). (ren2024studyonmicrostructure pages 8-10)

A review of as-built Scalmalloy data reports typical ranges of YS ~244–309 MPa, UTS ~315–438 MPa, elongation ~14–23% across multiple studies. (cerri2024mainheattreatments pages 5-7)

LPBF Al–Mn–Mg–Sc–Zr systems can reach even higher strengths after T5-type aging in one report (AB: 294/337/24%; T5: 484/507/15%; T6: 371/442/9%). (cao2025effectofheat pages 7-9)

## 4. Overall comparison and key takeaways

1. **Best-achieved strengths in WAAM Al among the common wires (ER2319/ER4043/ER5356):** ER2319 (heat treated) clearly leads, with demonstrated YS ≈ 324–326 MPa and UTS ≈ 452–479 MPa and elongation ≈ 7–8% in favorable cases. (pan2024highperformance2319aluminum pages 10-12, arana2022influenceofdeposition pages 1-2)

2. **Best-achieved ductility among the common wires:** ER4043 with interlayer FSP reached elongation up to ~37.5% (horizontal) at low strength (YS ~87 MPa, UTS ~147 MPa). (he2023improvementofmicrostructure pages 5-8)

3. **ER5356 typical “workhorse” behavior:** moderate strengths (YS ~109–134 MPa, UTS ~231–275 MPa) and elongation ~11–19% are achievable, with anisotropy often present. (wieczorowski2023characterizationof5356 pages 8-9, geng2021investigationofmicrostructure pages 7-8)

4. **WAAM vs LPBF:**
   - Against **LPBF AlSi10Mg**, WAAM ER2319 after heat treatment can be competitive in UTS and YS, but WAAM ER5356/ER4043 are generally much weaker. (pan2024highperformance2319aluminum pages 10-12, arana2022influenceofdeposition pages 1-2, wieczorowski2023characterizationof5356 pages 8-9, he2023improvementofmicrostructure pages 5-8, megahed2022effectofheat pages 11-13)
   - Against **LPBF Sc-containing alloys**, LPBF commonly achieves substantially higher strengths (e.g., aged YS ~527–535 MPa, UTS ~551–560 MPa) than the commercial-wire WAAM systems, while still keeping ~11% ductility. (ren2024studyonmicrostructure pages 8-10, pan2024highperformance2319aluminum pages 10-12, arana2022influenceofdeposition pages 1-2)

5. **Why WAAM properties vary widely:** the cited WAAM studies explicitly link tensile outcomes to porosity and brittle/continuous boundary phases (2319), and to pore elimination and Si-network fragmentation by FSP (4043), while Al–Mg WAAM results commonly show orientation-driven anisotropy due to interlayer discontinuities/pores. (pan2024highperformance2319aluminum pages 10-12, he2023improvementofmicrostructure pages 5-8, tonelli2021aa5083(al–mg)plates pages 11-14, tonelli2021aa5083(al–mg)plates pages 10-11)

**AlSi10Mg / Al–Si(-Mg).** EBM/PBF-EB AlSi10Mg is reported to have **low as-built strength but high ductility**. **AlSi10Mg / Al–Si(-Mg) (LPBF).** LPBF AlSi10Mg commonly achieves much higher as-built tensile strength than EBM/PBF-EB. (langebeck2020mechanicalpropertiesof pages 6-8)

1. **As-built strength**: For AlSi10Mg-family alloys, **LPBF > DED-LB ≳ EBM/PBF-EB** in the extracted datasets (e.g., LPBF UTS commonly 314–399 MPa vs EBM ~136–152 MPa; DED Al-12Si UTS up to 155.5 MPa). ## Query 3

**Question:** How does porosity type (gas porosity vs lack-of-fusion vs keyhole) and volume fraction quantitatively affect mechanical properties of LPBF aluminum alloys? Provide data on property degradation with increasing porosity.

**Task ID:** `75082cf9-f354-4703-9513-9a43f6f410fd`

**Status:** complete

## Scope and key porosity definitions (LPBF Al alloys)
LPBF porosity is commonly classified into (i) gas/entrapped-hydrogen pores (generally small and spherical), (ii) lack-of-fusion (LoF) porosity (large, irregular, low sphericity/high aspect ratio; often between scan tracks/layers and associated with unmelted powder), and (iii) keyhole pores (often larger and more spherical/elliptical, arising from keyhole cavity instability/collapse and vapor entrapment, typically deeper in the melt pool). (sola2019microstructuralporosityin pages 13-15, kan2022acriticalreview pages 1-3, snow2021flawsinpowder pages 43-47)

Quantitatively, one review describes gas pores as typically <~100 µm and spherical, while LoF pores are “much larger” with irregular shape (high aspect ratio/low sphericity) and keyhole pores are typically large and (often) spherical due to keyhole collapse. (kan2022acriticalreview pages 1-3)

## Quantitative property degradation with porosity fraction and pore type (AlSi10Mg example)
A pore-type-controlled LPBF AlSi10Mg study (Kramer et al., 2025) provides one of the clearest quantitative datasets separating porosity *type* and *volume fraction* and reporting tensile and fatigue outcomes. (kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 4-6)

### Primary quantitative dataset (Kramer et al. 2025): tensile and fatigue vs porosity level and pore type
In Kramer et al. (2025), measured porosity in fatigue specimens was:
- High-density/standard condition (HD; critical defects mostly oxide-related): **0.20%**
- Keyhole (KH): **2.06%**
- Lack-of-fusion (LoF): **10.35%** (kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 4-6, kramer2025impactofdifferent media 01d54061)

Corresponding tensile properties:
- **HD (0.20%)**: UTS **~425 MPa**, elongation **~6.3%**
- **KH (2.06%)**: UTS **~393 MPa**, elongation **~6.8%**
- **LoF (10.35%)**: UTS **~263 MPa**, elongation **~1.9%** (kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 4-6, kramer2025impactofdifferent media 01d54061)

**Property degradation with increasing porosity depends strongly on pore type**. Using HD (0.20%) as a low-porosity baseline, increasing porosity to 10.35% via LoF defects reduced UTS by ~162 MPa (≈38%) and elongation by ~4.4 percentage points (≈70% relative reduction). (kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 4-6)

Fatigue behavior (stress amplitude at 1×10^7 cycles) from the same study highlights that fatigue does *not* necessarily degrade monotonically with total porosity fraction:
- HD (0.20%): fatigue strength **~60 MPa** at 1×10^7 cycles
- KH (2.06%): fatigue strength **~80 MPa** at 1×10^7 cycles (i.e., *higher* than HD despite higher porosity)
- LoF (10.35%): **no specimen exceeded 4×10^5 cycles**, i.e., catastrophic fatigue debit relative to HD/KH (kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 11-12, kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 6-9, kramer2025impactofdifferent media 7ce582f2)

Mechanistic interpretation consistent with the data: **LoF porosity is most damaging** because irregular, tortuous, and potentially interconnected defects cause high stress concentration and large effective crack-starter size; **keyhole pores**, while increasing total porosity, can be more spherical/smaller (lower √area) and less crack-initiating than irregular oxide/LoF defects, yielding better endurance in this specific comparison. (kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 1-2, kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 6-9)

## Supporting quantitative trends (other LPBF AlSi10Mg studies)
### Tensile degradation in severe LoF regimes (process-induced)
In an LPBF AlSi10Mg process-parameter study, conditions associated with severe lack-of-fusion (low linear energy density, LED = 0.12 J/mm) showed a major tensile debit relative to a lower-porosity condition (LED = 0.15 J/mm):
- UTS decreased from **~475.2 MPa → ~422.6 MPa** (≈11% reduction)
- Elongation decreased from **~8.58% → ~3.32%** (≈61% reduction) (yang2020laserpowderbed pages 11-14)

Although porosity *fraction* was not explicitly reported in the extracted text for these conditions, the authors attribute the degradation to severe LoF pore formation and crack initiation at un-melted/macro-pore regions. (yang2020laserpowderbed pages 11-14)

### Fatigue sensitivity: pore morphology and extreme defects can dominate over bulk porosity
Afroz et al. (2025) emphasize that fatigue failure correlates strongly with **pore size/location/morphology** rather than bulk porosity fraction alone; they also report that porosity measured by XCT can change during cyclic loading (e.g., 0.03%→0.08% after tension–tension fatigue at R=0.1; 0.20%→0.09% after fully reversed fatigue at R=-1, consistent with pore opening vs closure). (afroz2025fatiguelifeof pages 10-11)

## Practical quantitative takeaways (LPBF Al alloys; strongest evidence for AlSi10Mg)
1. **LoF porosity causes the steepest property degradation**: in AlSi10Mg, raising porosity from ~0.2% (HD) to ~10% (LoF) reduced UTS by ~38% and elongation by ~70%, and eliminated high-cycle endurance (no samples reached 4×10^5 cycles in the cited dataset). (kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 4-6, kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 6-9)
2. **Keyhole vs gas porosity is generally less damaging in tension than LoF at comparable fractions**, because these pores are more spherical with lower stress concentration; however, fatigue behavior can be non-monotonic with porosity fraction because the controlling defect is often the single worst defect (√area-controlled), not the average porosity fraction. (kan2022acriticalreview pages 1-3, kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 11-12)
3. **Gas pores** (small, spherical; often a few µm and sometimes described as <5–6 µm when moisture/hydrogen-driven) are frequently considered less harmful to quasi-static properties than LoF, but the quantitative property-versus-gas-porosity-fraction slopes were not directly extractable from the current set of primary Al-alloy papers (most datasets either do not separate gas pores quantitatively or focus on defect size rather than fraction). (sola2019microstructuralporosityin pages 13-15, kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 1-2)

## Evidence limitations
The most direct quantitative “property degradation vs porosity fraction by pore type” evidence in the retrieved corpus is for **AlSi10Mg** (Kramer et al. 2025). Broader generalization across other LPBF aluminum alloys (e.g., 2xxx/6xxx/7xxx) likely holds qualitatively (LoF most detrimental), but additional pore-type-separated datasets would be required to report robust, alloy-general slopes (e.g., ΔUTS per 1 vol% porosity) across multiple aluminum systems using consistent porosity measurement methods (XCT vs Archimedes vs 2D metallography). (kan2022acriticalreview pages 6-8, kramer2025impactofdifferent pages 4-6)

## 1) LPBF Al–Si–Mg (AlSi10Mg/Al10SiMg): strength scales with cellular/sub-cell size plus nanoscale Si precipitates
### 1.1 Explicit additive strengthening model with Hall–Petch term using *cell size*
Hadadzadeh et al. decomposed yield strength of DMLS/LPBF AlSi10Mg as an additive sum of friction stress + Orowan + Hall–Petch + dislocation hardening:

\[\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + \Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{Orowan}} + \Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{HP}} + \Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{dislocation}}\] (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16)

with:
- Orowan (Al–Si binary system form): \(\Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{Orowan}} = \frac{\phi G b}{d_{Si}}\left(\frac{6V_{Si}}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}\) (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16)
- Hall–Petch (implemented with aluminum *cell size* \(d\)): \(\Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{HP}} = k/\sqrt{d}\) (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16)
- Dislocation hardening: \(\Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{dislocation}} = \beta M G b \sqrt{\rho_d}\) (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16)

This is a quantitative structure–property correlation in the requested form (Hall–Petch plus precipitate-size-dependent strengthening), albeit the extracted text did not include numerical values for \(k\), \(\sigma_0\), \(\phi\), or \(\beta\) (they are referenced as being tabulated in the paper). (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16)

### 1.2 Quantitative length scales and measured properties supporting the model
Hadadzadeh et al. report sub-micron cell sizes and nanoscale Si precipitates together with tensile properties, enabling mechanistic attribution:
- Representative tensile properties (recycled powder): YS/UTS ≈ 235/386 MPa (horizontal) and 210/392 MPa (vertical), elongation ≈ 7.2% (horizontal) vs 5.5% (vertical). (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 7-12)
- Cell sizes in virgin material: 0.77 µm (horizontal) and 0.86 µm (vertical). (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16)
- Nanoscale Si precipitate diameters: ~61–65 nm (recycled) (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 7-12) and 25–62 nm (virgin, depending on build direction). (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 16-20)
- Dislocation densities in virgin material: 1.14×10^14 to 3.05×10^14 m−2 (orientation dependent). (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16)

The authors report that the predicted vs. measured yield strengths agree within a few percent, supporting the combined role of cell size (Hall–Petch term), precipitate size/volume fraction (Orowan term), and dislocation density. (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16)

### 1.3 Empirical correlation of tensile properties with *sub-cell size* across LPBF parameter sets
Kempf & Hilgenberg measured sub-cell sizes in different LPBF AlSi10Mg batches and observed that smaller sub-cells correlate with higher strength/hardness:
- Example sub-cell sizes: fine/coarse sub-cells of 420/810 nm (batch #3) vs 700/1830 nm (batch #6). (kempf2020influenceofsubcell pages 4-5)
- As-built property ranges across batches: UTS 324–492 MPa, YS 180–292 MPa, elongation 1.9–5.6%, hardness 97–136 HV1. (kempf2020influenceofsubcell pages 5-6)
They explicitly report a correlation between sub-cell size and tensile strength/elongation (their Fig. 11), and attribute strength differences primarily to the sub-cell structure and associated Si-rich boundary precipitates (Orowan-type strengthening). (kempf2020influenceofsubcell pages 6-10, kempf2020influenceofsubcell pages 10-11)

### 1.4 Precipitate coarsening/spacing changes control local hardness, yield and ductility (especially MPB/HAZ)
Delahaye et al. analyzed how reheating produces a heat-affected zone with coarser and more widely spaced Si precipitates (and partially destroyed eutectic network), reducing hardness/yield/hardening compared with as-built cellular regions; Orowan looping is identified as the main strengthening/strain-hardening mechanism, and its effectiveness decreases as precipitates coarsen and spacing increases. (delahaye2019influenceofsi pages 13-16)

## 2) Cellular/dendritic spacing links processing (cooling rate) to local mechanical response (hardness/strength)
While not always expressed as a direct σ–spacing fit in the retrieved full texts, multiple studies quantify the LPBF solidification length scales that underlie Hall–Petch-like strengthening in Al–Si–Mg.

### 2.1 PDAS/SDAS vs cooling-rate correlations (microstructure–process)
Van Cauwenbergh et al. report a semi-empirical relation for primary dendrite arm spacing (PDAS):
- PDAS = a(G·R)^(−n), with a = 43.2 and n = 0.324 (cauwenbergh2021unravellingthemultiscale pages 7-8)
- Measured PDAS: 0.5 ± 0.1 µm in melt-pool centre vs 0.9 ± 0.2 µm at melt-pool boundary, corresponding to cooling-rate differences on the order of ~10^5–10^6 K/s. (cauwenbergh2021unravellingthemultiscale pages 7-8)
Ghio & Cerri used the same constants (k = 43.2, n = 0.324) in an SDAS vs cooling-rate power law and quantified how conditions with higher cooling rate and more overlap regions yield higher hardness. (ghio2021workhardeningof pages 6-8)

### 2.2 Hardness differences attributable to grain morphology and local solidification structure
Ghio & Cerri quantified local hardness differences:
- Equiaxed-grain molten-pool cross-section: 133 ± 10 HV15 vs columnar-grain cross-section: 107 ± 4 HV15. (ghio2021workhardeningof pages 6-8)
- Along-build hardness (centre region) decreases from bottom to top (aging/thermal history effects) with values such as 124 ± 3 → 109 ± 3 HV500 (SL-50) and 130 ± 2 → 114 ± 4 HV500 (SL-90). (ghio2021workhardeningof pages 8-10)

### 2.3 Representative cellular spacing values associated with high strength in LPBF Al10SiMg
Chou et al. report extremely fine cellular structure dimensions in pulsed-LPBF Al10SiMg:
- Al cell spacing 210 ± 65 nm and Si boundary width 39 ± 9 nm (chou2017microstructureandmechanical pages 3-6)
with high compressive strength (true compressive YS 380 MPa; true ultimate compressive strength 485 MPa). (chou2017microstructureandmechanical pages 3-6)

## 3) Sc/Zr-modified Al–Mg (Scalmalloy-type): grain size distribution and precipitate-rich melt-pool-boundary bands govern strength
Ekubaru et al. show that tuning *the volume fraction and distribution* of ultrafine grains (UFG bands at melt-pool boundaries) and associated Al3(Zr,Sc) precipitates changes tensile properties:
- UFG size is similar (~0.93–0.94 µm) but UFG volume fraction differs with scan strategy (e.g., ~57/56/47% across planes for X-scan vs 46/46/46% for XY-scan). (ekubaru2022effectsofscanning pages 4-7, ekubaru2022effectsofscanning pages 7-9)
- Precipitate volume fraction differs: 0.75% (X-scan) vs 0.59% (XY-scan). (ekubaru2022effectsofscanning pages 4-7)
- Tensile properties: X-scan YS/UTS = 271.5 ± 2.7 / 352.2 ± 1.4 MPa vs XY-scan 261.3 ± 1.0 / 343.8 ± 0.6 MPa; elongations ~29–31%. (ekubaru2022effectsofscanning pages 7-9, ekubaru2022effectsofscanning media 6b6e982f)
These data indicate that, even when mean UFG size is unchanged, increasing the *fraction* of UFG/MPB bands and precipitates increases yield strength, consistent with grain-boundary (Hall–Petch-type) and precipitation strengthening acting at melt-pool-boundary length scales. (ekubaru2022effectsofscanning pages 7-9, ekubaru2022effectsofscanning media 6b6e982f, ekubaru2022effectsofscanning media b79ce848)

## 4) What is missing for a full “Hall–Petch parameter” answer
Across the retrieved full texts, explicit fitted Hall–Petch coefficients (σ0 and k) for LPBF aluminum alloys were generally not present in the extractable text sections. The most quantitative Hall–Petch-style correlation available here is the explicit use of ΔσHP = k/√d with d taken as the LPBF cell size in the additive strength model of Hadadzadeh et al., but the numerical value of k is referenced as being tabulated rather than shown in the extracted text. (hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16)

## 5) Practical interpretation: dominant controlling microstructural features
1. **Cell/sub-cell spacing (~0.2–1.8 µm)** is the most consistently quantified length scale associated with strength/hardness in LPBF Al–Si–Mg; smaller cells/sub-cells increase strength via a Hall–Petch-like term and by increasing boundary Si precipitate density (Orowan). (chou2017microstructureandmechanical pages 3-6, hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 12-16, kempf2020influenceofsubcell pages 4-5)
2. **Nanoscale Si precipitate size (~25–65 nm) and its spacing/distribution**, especially differences between melt-pool centre vs boundary/HAZ, control Orowan strengthening and local ductility/fracture by changing slip resistance and void nucleation sites. (delahaye2019influenceofsi pages 13-16, hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 7-12, hadadzadeh2018strengtheningmechanismsin pages 16-20)
3. **Grain size distribution and melt-pool-boundary UFG band fraction (Scalmalloy)**—more MPB/UFG regions plus higher Al3(Zr,Sc) precipitate fraction yields higher YS/UTS even when mean UFG size is similar. (ekubaru2022effectsofscanning pages 4-7, ekubaru2022effectsofscanning pages 7-9, ekubaru2022effectsofscanning media 6b6e982f)
4. **Cooling-rate–spacing relations (PDAS/SDAS)** provide quantitative process→structure links and rationalize why parameter changes that raise cooling rate/overlap refine cellular spacing and increase hardness/strength. (cauwenbergh2021unravellingthemultiscale pages 7-8, ghio2021workhardeningof pages 6-8)

## System-by-system summary (peak aging temperatures and times)

### 1) AlSi10Mg (LPBF)
**Direct aging / T5-like kinetics:** In an LPBF AlSi10Mg study that explicitly mapped hardness vs. aging time (1–8 h) at 160/180/200 °C, the as-built + artificial aged condition reached peak hardness at **160 °C for 4 h** (130.6 ± 1.7 HV) and **180 °C for 4 h** (130.8 ± 1.9 HV); at **200 °C the peak occurred earlier (2 h)** (peak time given in the excerpt). (pu2024theinfluenceof pages 4-6)

**Solution + aging / T6-like behavior:** A rapid T6 approach for LPBF AlSi10Mg is reported as **510 °C for 10 min (solution) + 160 °C for 6 h (aging)**, and a direct T5 condition of **160 °C for 4 h** is used as a baseline. The work reports classical kinetics after solutionizing (hardness rises with time; peak time shifts shorter at higher aging temperatures), but the excerpt does not provide a single numerical “peak at X h” value for the rapid-T6 aging curve. (egidio2022anovelt6 pages 6-9, egidio2022anovelt6 pages 12-15)

**Implication for wrought comparison:** For LPBF AlSi10Mg, several studies emphasize that the **as-built condition can already show very high strength/hardness** due to the fine cellular α-Al + Si network, so additional aging may yield limited benefit or even degrade properties depending on the route (e.g., long aging at 177 °C up to 1000 h gave little advantage relative to as-built in one dataset). This contrasts with many wrought Al alloys where peak strength is typically targeted via a well-defined T6 sequence from a solutionized state. (merino2021multiplecomparativeheat pages 10-14)


### 2) Scalmalloy® / Al–Mg–Sc–Zr (LPBF)
**Direct aging is the dominant LPBF practice and is fast:** A commonly reported “datasheet-like” direct-aging condition is **325 °C for 4 h**, which is repeatedly cited as producing large strength gains (YS ~256–278 MPa → ~456–469 MPa; UTS ~349–350 MPa → ~512–521 MPa). (cerri2024mainheattreatments pages 1-2)

**Explicit multi-temperature kinetics (time-to-peak shifts strongly with temperature):** Long-duration hardness–time curves show that peak hardness depends strongly on aging temperature: **~128 h at 275 °C** to reach ~172 HV0.5, **~2 h at 325 °C** to reach/stabilize near ~165–171 HV, and a **very rapid ~5 min peak at 400 °C** (~165 HV0.5) followed by pronounced overaging at longer times. (ghio2025laserpowderbedfused pages 10-13, ghio2025laserpowderbedfused pages 13-15)

**Implication for wrought comparison:** LPBF processing is expected to **extend Sc solid solubility and increase dissolved Sc**, improving precipitation kinetics of Al3(Sc,Zr) (and enabling very short-time peak aging at elevated temperature). This “intrinsically supersaturated” LPBF starting point differs from typical wrought processing assumptions and helps explain why direct aging (without full solutionizing) is widely effective in LPBF Scalmalloy. (sharma2022advancementsinthe pages 31-32)


### 3) 2xxx-type Al–Cu(–Mg) (LPBF)
**Measured aging-kinetics (hardness vs time) exist for LPBF Al–Cu:** One LPBF Al–Cu alloy dataset reports clear T5 and T6 peak times: for **T5**, peak hardness was **~101 HV0.2 at 170 °C/16 h** and also **~101 HV0.2 at 190 °C/8 h**; for **T6**, peak hardness reached **146 ± 2 HV0.2 at 170 °C/6 h**. (tumulu2025laserpowderbed pages 70-75)

**Additional LPBF Al–Cu–(Ag) kinetics at fixed temperature:** An LPBF Al–Cu–(Ag) alloy development study solution-treated at **510 °C** and aged at **160 °C**, reporting peaks around **16–20 h** depending on composition (e.g., 187 VHN at 160 °C/16 h for one alloy). (sweny2018compositionalformulationsand pages 86-91)

**Implication for wrought comparison:** LPBF Al–Cu alloys commonly start from **fine, highly supersaturated microstructures due to solute trapping**, which can make precipitation hardening effective even with relatively short artificial-aging times (hours) at moderate temperatures; this differs from many wrought routes where solutionizing, quenching, and aging are tightly standardized and where the as-received (worked) microstructure/dislocation structure differs. (kotadiaUnknownyearkotadiahrgibbons pages 8-10, shakil2021microstructureandsmallscale pages 59-66)


### 4) 7xxx-type Al–Zn–Mg(–Cu) (LPBF)
**(i) Zr-containing 7xxx processed by LPBF with full kinetic curves:** For an LPBF Al–Zn–Mg–Cu–Zr alloy, artificial aging at **120 °C** gives:
- **T5-like** peak at **120 °C/30 h** (160 ± 13 HV0.1).
- **T6** peak after **475 °C/2 h solution + water quench + 120 °C/20 h** (218 ± 11 HV0.1). (chambrin2023influenceofheat pages 7-11, chambrin2023influenceofheat pages 11-13)

**(ii) Si-modified 7075 (LPBF) direct aging peaks:** For an Si-modified LPBF 7075 alloy, direct aging reached peak hardness after **24 h at 120 °C** and **8 h at 150 °C** (peak times reported; values not shown in the excerpt). (li2021investigationofsolidification pages 14-15)

**(iii) Si-added 7xxx via powder blending (LPBF) with peak among tested schedules:** A mixed-powder Al–Si–Zn–Mg–Cu (50% 7075 blend) study reports the best condition among those tested as **160 °C/6 h** (HV 152.9; σy ~350 MPa; UTS ~415 MPa). It also shows that a **300 °C/2 h “AlSi10Mg-style” stress relief** can severely reduce hardness (overaging/coarsening) in this 7xxx variant. (aversa2022laserpowderbed pages 9-11)

**(iv) Rare-earth/Zr-modified 7xxx with defined heat treatments:** For an LPBF Al–Zn–Mg–Cu–Si–Zr–Er alloy, reported heat treatments include **direct aging 150 °C/4 h**, **T6 = 520 °C/1 h + WQ + 175 °C/4 h**, and **RRA = 520 °C/1 h + WQ + 175 °C/4 h + 240 °C/0.5 h + 175 °C/4 h**; the excerpt identifies **175 °C/4 h as a peak-hardness condition (~131 HV)**. (zhang2023effectofpostheat pages 1-3)

**Implication for wrought comparison:** In LPBF 7xxx, several factors shift the response vs wrought: (1) processability constraints and chemistry changes (e.g., Zn/Mg volatility) can reduce achievable “wrought-like” T6 property levels; (2) as-built dislocation density may suppress GP-zone growth while providing nucleation sites for η, altering kinetics/pathways; and (3) common LPBF stress-relief temperatures can unintentionally overage 7xxx variants. These features make tailored lower-temperature/shorter-time aging (and careful solutionizing) more important than simply applying wrought schedules. (sharma2022advancementsinthe pages 31-32, aversa2022laserpowderbed pages 9-11)


## How LPBF aging responses differ from wrought (mechanistic synthesis)

1. **Starting condition is often more supersaturated in LPBF than wrought.** LPBF’s high cooling rates promote solute trapping/extended solid solubility and metastable phase formation, changing the driving force and kinetics for precipitation relative to wrought products that start from thermomechanically processed microstructures. (kotadiaUnknownyearkotadiahrgibbons pages 8-10)

2. **Direct aging (T5-like) is frequently effective in LPBF, but not uniformly beneficial.** In Scalmalloy and several 7xxx variants, strong hardening is obtained by direct aging from the as-built state with hours (or even minutes at high T) to peak. (ghio2025laserpowderbedfused pages 10-13, chambrin2023influenceofheat pages 7-11) In contrast, for AlSi10Mg, some datasets show limited benefit of extended aging vs. as-built properties, reflecting that the as-built Si-network/cellular microstructure can already confer high strength. (merino2021multiplecomparativeheat pages 10-14)

3. **Peak times can be shorter (or the peak can be broader/more stable) due to abundant heterogeneous nucleation sites.** LPBF microstructures (cell boundaries, high dislocation density, residual stresses) can accelerate precipitation or shift dominant pathways; for example, Scalmalloy shows rapid peak aging at 325 °C (~2 h) and extreme acceleration at 400 °C (~5 min) but also overaging at long times/high temperatures. (ghio2025laserpowderbedfused pages 10-13)

4. **Thermal exposures used for stress relief in LPBF can coincide with precipitation/overaging windows.** A 300 °C/2 h stress-relief condition can overage and reduce hardness in Si-modified 7xxx variants, highlighting that LPBF post-processing must be alloy-specific and not borrowed wholesale from other Al systems. (aversa2022laserpowderbed pages 9-11)

### 1) LPBF AlSi10Mg

#### SHPB compression (high strain-rate flow/strength)
Nurel et al. performed SHPB compression on SLM/LPBF AlSi10Mg (7 mm diameter disks; 3.5 or 7 mm thickness) over a broad dynamic strain-rate range (~700–7,900 s−1) in both as-built and T5 heat-treated conditions, and for loading parallel (Z) and perpendicular (X/XY) to the build direction. Reported compressive stresses at engineering strain 0.1 were 569 ± 8 MPa (Z, as-built), 514 ± 7 MPa (X, as-built), 427 ± 5 MPa (Z, T5-HT), and 405 ± 8 MPa (X, T5-HT), demonstrating directional dependence and a reduction in strength after T5 heat treatment. (nurel2018splithopkinsonpressure pages 32-40)

In the same SHPB program, post-peak softening/descent rates (reported for high strains) depended on orientation and heat treatment (Z as-built −421 MPa/strain; Z T5-HT −339 MPa/strain; X as-built −506 MPa/strain; X T5-HT −292 MPa/strain). Fracture observations included severe cracking in as-built specimens, major cracks often ~45° to disk axis, and more ductile fracture surfaces in T5-HT specimens. (nurel2018splithopkinsonpressure pages 32-40)

A separate Hopkinson-based study (Amir et al., “anisotropic behavior…”) reported dynamic compressive peak stresses at ~4000 s−1 of 606 ± 8 MPa (Z) and 522 ± 10 MPa (XY), again indicating build-orientation dependence under high-rate loading. (amirUnknownyeartheanisotropicbehavior pages 37-41)

Across the Nurel et al. SHPB range, the authors report no significant overall strain-rate sensitivity in the measured dynamic response (while still observing anisotropy within a mid-rate window), and note that T5 reduces but does not eliminate the anisotropy. (nurel2018splithopkinsonpressure pages 1-5, nurel2018splithopkinsonpressure pages 13-17)

#### Shock/plate-impact response (very high-rate elastic-plastic transition and dynamic tensile failure)
Specht & Brown measured the shock response of LPBF AlSi10Mg via plate-impact experiments to peak compressive stresses >13 GPa. They reported a linear Hugoniot fit in particle/ shock velocity space and a Hugoniot elastic limit (HEL) of ~0.25–0.30 GPa at elastic strain rates on the order of 10^5 s−1. (specht2021shockcompressionresponse pages 5-7)

They also reported spall (dynamic tensile) strength in the range 1.16–1.45 GPa for tensile strain rates roughly ~0.7×10^5 to 1.2×10^5 s−1, and noted that spall strength increases with tensile strain rate and peak compressive stress; they observed limited orientation dependence under their conditions. (specht2021shockcompressionresponse pages 1-2, specht2021shockcompressionresponse pages 8-9, specht2021shockcompressionresponse pages 7-8)

### 2) LPBF AlSi12

#### SHPB compression (temperature and post-build heat-treatment effects)
Ponnusamy et al. (2020) used SHPB compression on SLM AlSi12 at an applied strain rate of ~1×10^3 s−1 (as-built specimens) and evaluated elevated test temperatures and post-build heat treatments. For as-built specimens tested at RT, they reported a peak flow stress of 560 MPa. Elevated test temperature reduced peak flow stress by ~20% at 200°C (~448 MPa) and by ~50% at 400°C (~280 MPa), consistent with strong thermal softening. (ponnusamy2020dynamiccompressivebehaviour pages 12-17)

The same work also reported heat-treatment-induced softening relative to an as-built reference peak stress (reported as 525 MPa in the excerpt): a 200°C heat treatment reduced peak flow stress by 12%, and a 400°C heat treatment reduced peak flow stress by 45%. (ponnusamy2020dynamiccompressivebehaviour pages 17-21, ponnusamy2020dynamiccompressivebehaviour pages 21-25)

#### SHPB compression (lower strain-rate regime)
Earlier SHPB testing of SLM AlSi12 (Ponnusamy et al., 2018) was conducted at ~670–840 s−1 (variously reported around ~690–720 s−1 or nominal 700 s−1), at room temperature and 200°C. The extracted text primarily reports qualitative trends (elevated temperature reduces strength; rate effects are more pronounced at high temperature), and provides post-test hardness values after dynamic testing (150 HV horizontal, 156 HV inclined, 160 HV vertical). (ponnusamy2018highstrainrate pages 10-12, ponnusamy2018highstrainrate pages 1-2, ponnusamy2018highstrainrate pages 7-8)

### 3) Ballistic performance results (LPBF AlSi10Mg)
Kristoffersen et al. tested powder-bed-fusion (LPBF/PBF) AlSi10Mg plates (100 mm × 80 mm × 5 mm) under ballistic impact from 7.62 mm APM2 projectiles (full AP rounds) and from the hard core in a sabot (“CO” series). Using Recht–Ipson fits to initial–residual velocity data, they reported ballistic limit velocities v_bl for LPBF AlSi10Mg of 231.6 m/s (AP) and 225.5 m/s (CO), and found only marginal differences relative to a die-cast AlSi10Mg of the same chemistry (230.7 m/s for AP and 222.9 m/s for CO). (kristoffersen2020ontheballistic pages 1-5, kristoffersen2020ontheballistic media 9462bb6c)

Representative initial→residual velocity pairs from LPBF plate tests include AP: 606.2→564.8 m/s and CO: 663.5→619.7 m/s (initial→residual), illustrating that many tests were in the fully perforating regime. (kristoffersen2020ontheballistic pages 36-44)

Observed perforation mechanisms were described as ductile hole growth with petalling on entry/exit and limited fragmentation; the study emphasizes that despite microstructural differences and tensile anisotropy, the ballistic impact behavior of LPBF and die-cast AlSi10Mg plates was very similar in both appearance and fitted ballistic-limit curves. (kristoffersen2020ontheballistic pages 20-23, kristoffersen2020ontheballistic pages 1-5)

## Query 8

**Question:** What are the production costs and cost-performance tradeoffs for different AM aluminum alloy systems? Compare powder cost, machine time, post-processing costs, and overall part cost vs conventional manufacturing.

**Task ID:** `4d3f68de-2917-433d-81b7-1d324b2acc49`

**Status:** complete

## Executive synthesis
Production cost for AM aluminum is driven primarily by (i) feedstock form (powder vs wire) and alloying content, (ii) machine time (build rate, recoating overhead, utilization, batch packing), and (iii) post-processing (support removal, heat treatment/HIP, finish machining and inspection). Across the evidence collected here, LPBF costs remain substantially higher than conventional routes on a $/kg basis for “bulk” simple geometry, but can approach or beat CNC machining on complex parts when buy‑to‑fly (B2F) is unfavorable for subtractive manufacturing and when LPBF build volume is efficiently packed. (wang2024areviewon pages 13-15, sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies pages 15-16)

## 1. Powder cost: strong alloy dependence in LPBF, modest in WAAM
**LPBF powders show a large premium over ingot/wrought stock, and the premium is alloy dependent.** A review compiling market prices reports AlSi10Mg powder at 100–200 CNY/kg (≈USD 13.8–27.6/kg), 6061 powder at 700–800 CNY/kg (≈USD 96.6–110.4/kg), and 7075 powder at 400–450 CNY/kg (≈USD 55.2–62.1/kg), compared with ingot prices on the order of ~19–35 CNY/kg. (wang2024areviewon pages 11-13)

**Scalmalloy feedstock is extremely expensive relative to conventional wrought.** In a bracket case study, raw material inputs used were €250/kg for Scalmalloy (LPBF) versus €3/kg for Al 7175 stock used in CNC machining (Table 2). (sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies media 2a26174c)

**WAAM uses wire with feedstock prices closer to commodity aluminum forms than LPBF powders (but still above bulk).** A WAAM aluminum assessment used wire at 81.9 €/kg (±15%) and “bulk material” at 41.0 €/kg (±15%), emphasizing that feedstock production dominates cumulative energy and remains a key cost driver. (priarone2020multicriteriaenvironmentaland pages 3-4)

**Tradeoff:** Moving from AlSi10Mg to higher-performance alloys (6061, 7xxx, Scalmalloy) can increase feedstock cost by factors of ~2–10+ (and much more vs conventional stock), so cost-per-part competitiveness increasingly depends on geometry-enabled value (lightweighting, part consolidation) and material utilization (low B2F). (wang2024areviewon pages 11-13, sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies media 2a26174c)

## 2. Machine time and productivity: the dominant lever in LPBF
**LPBF throughput is often the limiting cost driver.** Typical single-laser LPBF build rate is reported around ~20 cm³/h in the review, with multi-laser/advanced approaches potentially much higher (>1000 cm³/h reported for advanced systems). (wang2024areviewon pages 13-15)

**Case study (AlSi10Mg) quantifies machine-rate sensitivity explicitly.** For an AlSi10Mg turbine wheel (20.618 cm³) a cost model used powder at €90/kg and a computed machine cost rate of **€452.54/h** (from €450k capex, 5000 h/year, 5-year amortization). Two parameter sets (BR 6.72 vs 12.48 mm³/s) cut total cost from **€955.49/part** to **€578.10/part** for a single-part build. (qamat2025laserpowderbed pages 98-102, qamat2025laserpowderbed pages 94-98)

**Batching/packing reduces effective machine time per part.** In the same study, moving to a 16-part build reduced per-part cost to **€519.08** (slower set) and **€289.67** (faster set), illustrating that recoating/setup/overheads amortize over more parts. (qamat2025laserpowderbed pages 98-102)

**Tradeoff:** For commodity alloys like AlSi10Mg, powder cost is often not the dominant term; machine time and utilization dominate. Process choices that increase layer thickness / build rate and improve packing density typically deliver larger cost reductions than modest changes in powder €/kg. (qamat2025laserpowderbed pages 98-102)

## 3. Post-processing: often mandatory and can rival build cost in WAAM; in LPBF it can be a large fraction for complex parts
**LPBF post-processing is generally required** (stress relief/heat treatment, support removal, powder cleaning, secondary machining). For thin-walled parts or internal channels, the review notes that **support removal and powder cleaning time can exceed printing time**, meaning post-processing can dominate lead time and labor even if machine time is optimized. (wang2024areviewon pages 13-15)

**LPBF AlSi10Mg case study models pre/post as a fixed fraction.** The turbine-wheel model cites a NIST range of **4–19%** of total production cost for pre/post and assumes the maximum (19%) in calculations, so post-processing costs scale with total cost rather than being independently optimized in that model. (qamat2025laserpowderbed pages 94-98)

**WAAM often requires substantial finish machining due to surface finish and accuracy limits.** A process-based WAAM cost model example (not aluminum-specific but illustrative for WAAM economics) reports deposition cost ≈€25.84/part and machining cost €13.50/part (total chain €39.34/part), and an aluminum WAAM assessment highlights tooling/fixturing cost ranges from ~€300–400 to >€5000 depending on part size. (dias2022economicandenvironmental pages 10-12, priarone2020multicriteriaenvironmentaland pages 3-4)

**Tradeoff:** WAAM gains cost-performance via kg/h deposition and lower equipment complexity, but frequently “gives back” cost in finish machining and fixturing; LPBF has better near-net shape capability but still often requires support removal, stress relief, and machining—particularly for tight tolerances and fatigue-critical surfaces. (priarone2020multicriteriaenvironmentaland pages 3-4, wang2024areviewon pages 13-15)

## 4. Overall part cost vs conventional: complexity and B2F drive the crossover
**LPBF can remain far more expensive than traditional manufacturing on a mass basis.** The aluminum LPBF review reports a supplier quote implying AlSi10Mg LPBF manufacturing cost ≈2100 CNY/kg (with quoted selling price ≈3500 CNY/kg), and states LPBF aluminum cost is **~2–120×** higher than traditional manufacturing depending on part complexity. (wang2024areviewon pages 13-15)

**However, a complex aerospace bracket case shows LPBF approaching or beating CNC machining at part level.** For a bearing bracket, CNC machining in Al 7175 is reported at **€1019.41/part** with **B2F 1:7**. LPBF using Scalmalloy is **€930.20/part** for a single-part, distortion-minimized strategy and **€496.76/part** for a multi-part (3-up) build strategy, with LPBF B2F ≈1:1.2. The study also reports stage costs including recoating (€295.99) and laser melting (€177.32), illustrating that machine/process time steps dominate LPBF economics. (sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies pages 15-16, sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies media 2a26174c)

**Tradeoff:**
- **Conventional (CNC from wrought)** is favored for low complexity and large simple parts, but becomes costly when B2F is poor (e.g., 1:7) because raw material loss and machining time dominate. (sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies pages 15-16)
- **LPBF** can win on complex parts when it (a) drastically improves B2F, (b) enables part consolidation, and (c) is batched to raise effective utilization—despite far higher powder cost, especially for premium alloys like Scalmalloy. (sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies pages 15-16, sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies media 2a26174c)
- **WAAM/DED** can be economically attractive for medium-to-large parts due to ~2.4 kg/h aluminum deposition and wire feedstock costs far below LPBF powders, but it typically requires significant finish machining and tooling; costs are strongly sensitive to deposition time and the build-to-finish ratio. (priarone2020multicriteriaenvironmentaland pages 3-4, cunningham2017costmodellingand pages 5-7)

## 5. Cost–performance tradeoffs by alloy system (what you “buy” with higher cost)
**AlSi10Mg (LPBF baseline):** Lowest powder cost among commonly LPBF-processed Al alloys in the cited pricing data; good printability; economics mostly governed by machine time and batching rather than feedstock, as shown in the turbine-wheel analysis. (wang2024areviewon pages 11-13, qamat2025laserpowderbed pages 98-102)

**6061 (LPBF):** Much higher powder cost than AlSi10Mg in the cited price compilation; without demonstrated throughput/qualification advantages in the evidence provided, this tends to worsen $/part unless 6061-specific property/qualification needs justify it. (wang2024areviewon pages 11-13)

**7xxx (LPBF vs CNC):** Powder cost premium exists but is smaller than 6061 powder in the cited review. Conventional machining of high-strength 7xxx aerospace parts can suffer severe B2F penalties; thus, the value proposition for AM is often B2F/material savings and complexity, though direct LPBF 7xxx part-cost data were not found in the collected snippets. (wang2024areviewon pages 11-13, sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies pages 15-16)

**Scalmalloy (LPBF):** Very high feedstock cost (€250/kg) but may enable high-performance lightweight designs and favorable B2F; the bracket example shows that build strategy (multi-part packing) can halve unit cost, indicating a strong cost–performance coupling between alloy selection, design, and production planning. (sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies pages 15-16, sargioti2026simulationdrivenbuildstrategies media 2a26174c)

**WAAM/DED Al (wire):** Strong economic potential for large structures because wire is far cheaper than powder and deposition is fast (2.40 kg/h), but the performance/cost tradeoff often shifts downstream to machining/QA because of surface finish and dimensional accuracy limitations. (priarone2020multicriteriaenvironmentaland pages 3-4, cunningham2017costmodellingand pages 5-7)

## Limitations of the retrieved evidence
The evidence base here contains strong quantitative data for AlSi10Mg LPBF economics and several quantitative comparisons for Scalmalloy LPBF vs CNC and for WAAM general economics, but it does **not** include a detailed, apples-to-apples post-processing cost breakdown (e.g., € for HIP vs heat treatment vs machining) across all aluminum alloy systems, nor does it provide explicit LPBF per-part cost models for 6061 and 7xxx alloys. Where data are missing, the table marks “not reported” rather than extrapolating. (wang2024areviewon pages 11-13, wang2024areviewon pages 13-15)

## Query 9

**Question:** What thin-wall and size-dependent mechanical properties have been measured for LPBF aluminum alloys? How do properties change with wall thickness below 2mm? Provide quantitative data.

**Task ID:** `9b0e6677-1220-4daf-a905-5668f81eb40c`

**Status:** complete

## Scope
Thin-wall/size-dependent mechanical-property datasets for LPBF aluminum alloys are currently dominated by AlSi10Mg, with measurements spanning: (i) thin-wall tensile specimens with variable wall thickness, (ii) flat tensile coupons with variable printed thickness, (iii) small-diameter rods/struts (relevant to thin ribs and lattice struts), and (iv) ultra-thin wall tracks where hardness and roughness are quantified.

## What thin-wall and size-dependent properties have been measured (LPBF aluminum alloys)
Measured properties reported with explicit feature-size dependence include:
- **Tensile properties**: yield strength (YS), ultimate tensile strength (UTS), elongation at fracture (ductility). (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 1-2, fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 1-2, fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 2-5, dong2018studyofsize pages 6-9)
- **Elastic modulus (E)**: reported vs strut diameter for AlSi10Mg rods/struts. (dong2018studyofsize pages 6-9)
- **Microhardness (HV)**: measured for thin walls and as a function of wall thickness in AlSi10Mg thin-wall studies. (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 1-2, zhang2021investigationformacro pages 8-10, poncelet2021criticalassessmentof pages 10-12)
- **Surface roughness (Ra)** of thin-wall vertical surfaces (process-parameter dependent; not a thickness sweep). (poncelet2021criticalassessmentof pages 8-10)
- **Defect metrics linked to size effect**: relative density (thin-wall thickness series), pore size ranges, and μCT porosity fraction vs strut diameter. (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 5-8, dong2018studyofsize pages 6-9)

## Quantitative changes with wall thickness below 2 mm (AlSi10Mg)
### 1) Flat tensile coupons, printed thickness 0.5–2.0 mm (as-built AlSi10Mg)
Fahimi et al. tested as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg tensile specimens with nominal thicknesses **0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 mm**. They observed a **systematic degradation in strength and ductility with decreasing thickness**, with the strongest deterioration at **0.5 mm**. (fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 2-5, fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 7-9)
- They report that comparing **2 mm vs 0.5 mm** shows a **>2× increase in elongation** for 2 mm specimens (i.e., 0.5 mm has less than half the elongation). (fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 2-5, fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 7-9)
- They also report **>50% reduction** in tensile properties (UTS and elongation) when comparing **2 mm to 0.5 mm** specimens. (fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 1-2)
- A key metrology point: the **nominal 0.5 mm** samples had **average measured thickness 0.58 mm**, and using **nominal vs measured cross-section** produces a “huge difference” in calculated stress, meaning apparent strength changes at the thinnest sizes can be biased by dimensional error/roughness. (fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 5-7)
Figure-based summary (values are approximate bar read-offs because no numeric table is provided): YS and UTS increase monotonically from 0.5→2 mm, and elongation increases similarly. (fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy media a4541d60, fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy media 716542c6)

### 2) Thin-wall tensile specimens, wall thickness series 0.5–5.0 mm (AlSi10Mg)
Zhang et al. report thin-wall AlSi10Mg properties with explicit points at **0.5 mm** and **5.0 mm** (same process parameter set). (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 1-2)
- **0.5 mm wall**: UTS **250.9 MPa**, YS **143.7 MPa**, elongation **5.31%**, microhardness **116.8 HV**, relative density **99.86%**. (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 1-2)
- **5.0 mm wall**: UTS **364 MPa**, elongation **12.04%**, relative density **99.21%**. (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 1-2)
Thus, from 0.5→5 mm, UTS increases by **~45%** and elongation by **~127%**. (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 1-2)

Within the thickness range that includes **<2 mm**, Zhang et al. also report that **relative density reaches a minimum at 1.5 mm** (relative density **93.60%**) and then increases for thicker walls; they associate thin-wall sections with more/larger pores (e.g., **10–50 μm** pores on 0.5 mm top surfaces vs **10–70 μm** reported for 1.5 mm with many positions showing >50 μm). (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 5-8)
This supports a non-monotonic “quality window” in some thin-wall builds where an intermediate thickness (here ~1.5 mm) can show worse densification/defects than the thinnest wall under the chosen scan strategy. (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 5-8)

Microhardness vs thickness in Zhang et al. is reported as non-systematic in the excerpt, but at least one sub-2 mm point is given:
- **0.8 mm wall**: **105.6 HV** (lowest reported). (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 8-10)

### 3) Small struts/rods, diameter 1–5 mm (proxy for thin ribs/struts)
Dong et al. measured μCT porosity and tensile properties of SLM AlSi10Mg rods with nominal diameters **1–5 mm**; these are highly relevant to thin-wall/filigree LPBF designs (e.g., ribs, thin columns, lattice struts). (dong2018studyofsize pages 6-9)
- **Porosity fraction increases strongly with decreasing size**: **1 mm: 1.87%**, **2 mm: 1.26%**, **3 mm: 0.57%**, **4 mm: 0.12%**, **5 mm: 0.10%**. (dong2018studyofsize pages 6-9)
- Mechanical properties become “bulk-like” only for sizes **≥4 mm**, stabilizing at approximately **E ~70 GPa**, **elongation ~5.5%**, **UTS ~345 MPa**, **YS ~220 MPa**. (dong2018studyofsize pages 6-9)
- Reducing size from **5 mm to 1 mm** decreases **E and elongation by >30%** and decreases **UTS and YS by ~24%**. (dong2018studyofsize pages 6-9)

## Ultra-thin walls: hardness/roughness trade-offs (sub-mm scale)
Poncelet et al. studied LPBF AlSi10Mg thin walls (including “ultra thin walls” / single-wall tracks) primarily as a function of **scan/contour parameters** (not a thickness sweep), but provides quantitative values representative of sub-mm thin-wall conditions:
- Hardness values: **~136 HV** (bulk hatching), **119 HV** (thin wall, contour-focused), **109 HV** (single-wall track). (poncelet2021criticalassessmentof pages 10-12)
- Vertical surface roughness examples: **Ra 4.2 μm** (TW-CF), **Ra 8.6 μm** (TW-BF), and a reported “best” TW condition **Ra 5.9 μm** (TED 341 J/m, 90 μm offset). (poncelet2021criticalassessmentof pages 8-10)
They note a process trade-off where pushing toward keyhole-like regimes can reduce apparent roughness but increases subsurface porosity and reduces hardness. (poncelet2021criticalassessmentof pages 1-2, poncelet2021criticalassessmentof pages 8-10)

## Overall answer to “How do properties change with wall thickness below 2 mm?”
Across independent AlSi10Mg datasets, reducing feature thickness/cross-section into the **sub-2 mm** regime generally causes:
1. **Lower UTS/YS and much lower elongation** in the thinnest coupons (0.5–1 mm), with reported **>50% reductions** in tensile properties when comparing **2 mm vs 0.5 mm** in one study. (fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 1-2, fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 7-9)
2. **Higher porosity and worse effective load-bearing area** for smaller struts/rods (e.g., **1.87% porosity at 1 mm** vs **0.10% at 5 mm**) and corresponding drops in E/UTS/YS/elongation (E and elongation **>30% lower**, UTS/YS **~24% lower** from 5→1 mm). (dong2018studyofsize pages 6-9)
3. **Greater sensitivity to dimensional error and surface roughness**, making **measured vs nominal cross-sectional area** especially important at **~0.5 mm** thickness (0.5 mm nominal measured 0.58 mm in Fahimi et al.). (fahimi2024anexperimentalstudy pages 5-7)
4. **Process-strategy dependence**: thin-wall series can show **non-monotonic density/defect minima** at intermediate thickness (e.g., **93.60% relative density at 1.5 mm** in Zhang et al.), so “thinner always worse” is not universal unless scan strategy is controlled. (zhang2021investigationformacro pages 5-8)

### Key quantitative highlights by alloy family

#### 1) Al–Li alloys for LPBF/SLM/DED

* **SLM Al–Cu–Li–Mg–Ag–Sc–Zr (2024)**: As-printed SLM Al–Li variants spanning 1–3 wt.% Li show high strength with strongly Li/defect-dependent ductility. For ~1 wt.% Li: YS 413 ± 16 MPa, UTS 461 ± 12 MPa, elongation 14 ± 1% (hardness 135 ± 3 HV). For ~2 wt.% Li: YS 481 ± 38 MPa, UTS 515 ± 28 MPa, elongation 5 ± 3% (hardness 160 ± 3 HV). For 3 wt.% Li: YS 478 ± 1 MPa, UTS 495 ± 17 MPa, elongation 1 ± 1% (hardness 148 ± 4 HV). The 3 wt.% Li alloy also had substantially lower densification (~92%), consistent with its very low ductility. (shao2024microstructureandmechanical pages 12-14, shao2024microstructureandmechanical media db348c34)
* **LPBF AA2195 (2020)**: In the as-fabricated state, YS 207.7 ± 1.5 MPa, UTS 340.7 ± 2.9 MPa, elongation 17.2 ± 0.9%. After a short T6 aging treatment (170 °C × 6 h), strength increased to YS 521.3 ± 14.4 MPa, UTS 587.0 ± 7.2 MPa, with elongation 6.9 ± 0.3%. (qi2020ahighstrength pages 8-9)
* **DED/LMD Al–Li (2020)**: A laser melting deposition study reported a through-build hardness gradient (~121 HV at top, rising to ~134 HV at ~30 mm depth, and decreasing to ~98 HV near bottom). Tensile behavior was reported as impaired by microcracks; the longitudinal UTS was described as comparable to a hot-rolled Al–Li reference value of 244 MPa, but YS and elongation for the AM material were not numerically stated in the retrievable excerpt. (jiao2020microstructureevolutionand pages 19-22)

#### 2) Al–Ti systems (Ti additions / in-situ Al3Ti in Al matrices)

* **SLM nano-TiC/AlSi10Mg with in-situ D022-Al3Ti (2021)**: Relative to SLM AlSi10Mg baseline (YS 254 MPa, UTS 437 MPa, elongation 5.72%, hardness ~113 HV0.05), the TiC-containing composite (with in-situ Al3Ti nanoparticles) achieved YS 338 MPa, UTS 456 MPa, elongation 2.97%, hardness ~131 HV0.05. The strength increase is consistent with reported grain refinement and dispersion strengthening by TiC and in-situ Al3Ti. (fan2021insituformation pages 7-8)

#### 3) Al–Ni systems (Al–Ni–Cu and Al–Si–Cu–Ni)

* **LPBF Al–Si–Cu–Ni (2024)**: As-built tensile properties were UTS 437 ± 6 MPa, YS 344 ± 4 MPa, elongation 1.51 ± 0.07%. A bending strength of 766 ± 30 MPa was also reported (fracture displacement 0.84 ± 0.03 mm). (shi2024solidificationmechanismof pages 9-11, shi2024solidificationmechanismof pages 7-9)
* **SLM Al–Ni–Cu (2021)**: A selective-laser-melted Al–Ni–Cu alloy (powder with ~3.8–4.2 wt.% Ni and ~1.9–2.2 wt.% Cu) was reported to reach UTS 725 MPa in the as-printed state but with very low ductility. After solution treatment (530 °C × 1 h) and aging (175 °C × 6 h), the alloy reportedly retained strength >500 MPa while improving ductility to >10%, but detailed numeric YS/UTS/EL and fatigue-life values were not present in the retrieved excerpt. (chang2021microstructuremechanicalproperties pages 1-2, chang2021microstructuremechanicalproperties pages 9-12)

#### 4) Compositionally complex / high-entropy aluminum-containing alloys

Two AM modalities are represented in the retrieved evidence: (i) solid-state severe-plasticity AM (AFSD) with compression data, and (ii) powder-bed (SLM/EBM) tensile datasets compiled in a 2024 review.

* **AFSD Al0.35CoCrFeNi (2024)**: In compression, AFSD material exhibited maximum stress of ~1074 MPa at 0.001 s−1 and ~1057 MPa at 1 s−1 (feedstock comparator values were ~575–576 MPa and ~690 MPa, respectively). An average annealed feedstock hardness of ~175 HV was reported. (mcclelland2024microstructureandmechanical pages 6-9)
* **Powder-bed processed AlxCoCrFeNi-family (review-compiled, 2024)**: Examples of SLM tensile properties include Al0.3CoCrFeNi with YS ~730 MPa, UTS 896 MPa, elongation 29%, and Al0.5CoCrFeNi with YS 579 MPa, UTS 721 MPa, elongation 22% (hardness 262.5 ± 5 HV). EBM AlCoCrFeNi showed orientation dependence: for 0° build orientation, yield ~1015 ± 52.5 MPa and strength ~1668.3 ± 71.5 MPa with elongation ~26.4 ± 6.7%, and for 90° build orientation, yield ~944.0 ± 55.4 MPa and strength ~1447.0 ± 135.8 MPa with elongation ~14.5 ± 5.3%. (gupta2024advancesinnickelcontaining pages 5-7, gupta2024advancesinnickelcontaining pages 8-9)

### Notes on “latest” and evidence limitations

* The newest primary (non-review) tensile datasets retrieved here are from **2024** for Al–Li (SLM) and Al–Ni (LPBF), and from **2024** for Al-containing HEA via solid-state AM (AFSD) but in compression rather than tensile loading. (shao2024microstructureandmechanical pages 12-14, shi2024solidificationmechanismof pages 9-11, mcclelland2024microstructureandmechanical pages 6-9)
* **Fatigue**: Although fatigue testing was a focus of the SLM Al–Ni–Cu study, the retrieved pages did not contain numerical S–N results needed for quantitative reporting; only qualitative statements and limited test-condition numbers were visible. (chang2021microstructuremechanicalproperties pages 2-5, chang2021microstructuremechanicalproperties pages 9-12)
* **Al-rich HEAs/CCAs**: Several powder-bed AlxCoCrFeNi values above are **review-compiled** rather than extracted from the underlying primary articles within this run; they are included because they provide explicit numerical YS/UTS/elongation/hardness for AM-built Al-containing HEAs, but should be treated as secondary until cross-validated in the original papers. (gupta2024advancesinnickelcontaining pages 5-7, gupta2024advancesinnickelcontaining pages 8-9)
