# Why LPBF Dominates AM Aluminum Deployment (and the Role of Ultrasonic Atomization)

This document compiles Edison Scientific LITERATURE results explaining why powder-bed laser fusion (LPBF/SLM) has reached industrial deployment for aluminum alloys while powder-blown directed energy deposition (DED), wire-arc additive manufacturing (WAAM), and electron-beam melting (EBM) have not. It also addresses the role of feedstock form (gas atomization, ultrasonic atomization, wire) in enabling small-batch novel-composition powder production for alloy discovery.

Question: Why have powder-bed laser fusion (LPBF/SLM) aluminum alloys reached industrial deployment while powder-blown directed energy deposition (DED), wire-arc additive manufacturing (WAAM), and electron beam melting (EBM) of aluminum have not? Compare the technological readiness and qualification status of these AM processes for aluminum alloys. What are the specific advantages of LPBF for aluminum (resolution, surface finish, microstructural refinement, alloy compositional flexibility, defect control)? What are the disadvantages of DED/WAAM for aluminum (oxidation, porosity, coarse microstructure, limited resolution)? How does feedstock form (gas-atomized vs. ultrasonic-atomized fine powder vs. wire) affect the achievable property envelope and the ability to design new alloy compositions? Why is fine, spherical, low-oxygen powder a prerequisite for high-performance AM aluminum alloys, and what role does ultrasonic atomization play in enabling small-batch novel-composition powder production for alloy discovery?

Industrial deployment of aluminum AM has converged on LPBF (PBF‑LB/M, SLM) because it combines (i) the finest geometric capability, (ii) rapid solidification that refines microstructure and expands the usable alloy set via tailored chemistries, and (iii) a comparatively mature qualification ecosystem (process monitoring, parameter control, coupon→subcomponent building‑block qualification). In contrast, powder‑blown DED and WAAM inherit welding-like oxidation/hydrogen porosity and coarse microstructures, and EB‑PBF/EBM for Al remains constrained by lower resolution/rougher surfaces and a limited industrial material set (largely Al–Si), which slows qualification and broad industrial uptake. (popov2021powderbedfusion pages 18-19, arena2024novelaluminumalloy pages 2-4, imran2024advancementsin3d pages 9-10)

### 1) Technological readiness and qualification status for aluminum alloys
**LPBF aluminum is the highest-readiness route** among the compared Al fusion AM processes. A cross-industry review explicitly assigns SLM/LPBF high TRLs (e.g., aerospace **TRL 5–7**, tooling **TRL 9**, medical **TRL 9–10**), reflecting broad industrialization of powder-bed laser processes compared with other metal AM routes. (popov2021powderbedfusion pages 18-19)

LPBF aluminum also has comparatively **more developed qualification practice**: industrial PBF platforms offer commercial in‑situ monitoring modules that record melt-pool/process data “at each spot, each layer, and each part,” supporting traceability and lowering QA cost—important for aerospace-style qualification. (calignano2019ametalpowder pages 11-13)

At the alloy+component level, PBF‑LB aluminum is already being advanced through **certification-oriented qualification workflows** (coupon characterization → subcomponent validation) for aeronautical applications, including a novel PBF‑LB alloy (Al‑HS1) demonstrated for compliance with industrial standards and airworthiness requirements. (arena2024novelaluminumalloy pages 1-2, arena2024novelaluminumalloy pages 2-4)

**DED/WAAM aluminum remains less mature for qualified production** largely because its lower accuracy and near-net-shape nature create larger dependence on post-processing and make property/defect repeatability harder to guarantee. A review explicitly states DED/WAAM is **less widespread than PBF** because of lower accuracy and post-processing needs, even though it offers high deposition rates and large-part capability. (popov2021powderbedfusion pages 18-19)

**EB‑PBF/EBM aluminum is industrially narrower**: it can process Al–Si alloys but is described as having **lower resolution/higher surface roughness**, smaller working volumes, and a **limited industrial material set**, which collectively reduces its attractiveness for high-performance Al components that require fine features and tight tolerances. (popov2021powderbedfusion pages 18-19)

### 2) Specific advantages of LPBF for aluminum
**(a) Resolution and surface finish**
LPBF is preferred when fine features, dimensional tolerance, and surface quality matter. Reviews describe LPBF as producing complex parts with “good surface quality” and “excellent dimensional tolerance,” and as capable of “small features and high surface precision.” (martucci2023ongoingchallengesof pages 1-3, wang2024areviewon pages 5-7)

A process–feedstock analysis ties LPBF’s fine resolution to its fundamentals: minimum feature size is bounded by laser spot diameter and layer thickness; spot sizes **<40 µm are not common**, and conventional Al powders (15–50 µm) make powder layers **<30 µm** difficult—hence the value of controlled fine powders for maximizing resolution. (balbaa2021roleofpowder pages 1-4)

**(b) Microstructural refinement via rapid cooling**
LPBF aluminum undergoes extremely rapid thermal cycles: cooling rates reported as **10^4–10^6 K/s** (and “above 10^5 K/s”) produce refined microstructures (e.g., ~**500 nm** cellular features and ~**10 µm** grain sizes cited in reviews of LPBF Al alloys). (wang2024areviewon pages 5-7, martucci2023ongoingchallengesof pages 3-4)

This microstructural refinement is an enabling reason LPBF can deliver high strength in printable Al systems (e.g., AlSi10Mg, Scalmalloy and newer tailored Al alloys) while maintaining reproducibility needed for industrial qualification. (arena2024novelaluminumalloy pages 2-4, wang2024areviewon pages 5-7)

**(c) Alloy compositional flexibility (tailored powders, microalloying, particle additions)**
LPBF aluminum is increasingly enabled by **tailored alloy development**, because many conventional high-strength Al alloys are difficult to print. Reviews emphasize the need to design new Al alloys for PBF‑LB/M. (martucci2023ongoingchallengesof pages 1-3)

Evidence includes specific microalloying/particle strategies: adding **1 vol% ZrH2 nanoparticles** significantly refined microstructure and **eliminated hot cracking** in both Al7075 and Al6061 processed by PBF‑LB/M, and a broader set of refiners/AM‑tailored compositions (e.g., Sc-bearing systems and engineered dispersoid-forming compositions) are discussed as routes to printability and performance. (yadegari2025aluminumlaseradditive pages 14-15)

**(d) Defect control: controlled environment + process monitoring + parameter sensitivity**
Aluminum’s high affinity for oxygen and oxide-film formation is a central defect driver, making atmosphere control critical. A powder-bed melting study states it is “mandatory” to precisely control and maintain low oxygen during processing to avoid negative impacts on tensile strength and fatigue resistance, and it identifies multiple oxygen sources (powder, equipment, gas supply). (bauer2016effectofprocess pages 1-4)

LPBF also offers a mature pathway to **defect control through monitoring and traceability**: commercial LPBF QA modules acquire melt-pool/process data per spot/layer/part. This is a key differentiator for qualification compared with open-arc/open-deposition processes that are harder to instrument consistently. (calignano2019ametalpowder pages 11-13)

### 3) Disadvantages of powder-blown DED and WAAM for aluminum
**(a) Oxidation/oxide film entrapment and hydrogen porosity**
WAAM/DED aluminum is strongly limited by the stability and entrapment of the oxide film (Al2O3), which must be removed before welding to reduce porosity risk; oxide-film entrapment in the melt pool is explicitly identified as a mechanism leading to porosity. (rodriguezgonzalez2023wirearcadditive pages 9-10)

Hydrogen porosity is also central: hydrogen is highly soluble in liquid Al and poorly soluble in solid, so it is trapped upon solidification; hydrogen contents **>1 mL/100 g Al** can generate excessive fusion-zone porosity (with residual hydrogen **>1–5 mL/100 g** cited as problematic). (rodriguezgonzalez2023wirearcadditive pages 9-10)

In powder-blown DED of Al7075, porosity could be reduced in single tracks (from **2.1%** to **0.09 ± 0.07%**) by increasing laser power, but larger builds still had higher porosity attributed to the **oxide skin on preceding tracks**, underscoring the scaling/track‑to‑track oxide problem. (langebeck2020mechanicalpropertiesof pages 1-3)

**(b) Coarse microstructure and inferior mechanical behavior vs LPBF**
DED has a lower cooling rate than PBF, producing **coarser grains**; a review explicitly states this is why DED mechanical properties are **generally inferior to PBF**. (imran2024advancementsin3d pages 9-10)

WAAM similarly produces heterogeneous and anisotropic microstructures; columnar grain growth between layers is noted, and precipitation/dissolution effects in age-hardenable Al families (2xxx/6xxx/7xxx) can degrade strength in the deposited + HAZ microstructure. (ozel2023areviewon pages 5-6, derekar2018areviewof pages 1-6)

**(c) Limited resolution/surface finish and heavy post-processing dependence**
DED/WAAM are intrinsically near-net-shape. A DED review reports substantially poorer geometric outcomes than PBF: DED tolerances **<0.25 mm** and surface roughness **<25 µm** with typical deviations ~**0.5 mm**, whereas PBF can reach precision **up to 0.05 mm**. (imran2024advancementsin3d pages 9-10)

Process-class comparisons reinforce that wire-based DED/WAAM is orders of magnitude coarser in geometric capability than powder-bed processes (e.g., DED wire-fed layer thickness ~**3000 µm** vs PBF‑LM **10–50 µm**, with minimum feature diameters ~**16,000 µm** vs **75–100 µm** cited in one comparison). (guarinoUnknownyearphdschoolin pages 22-25)

### 4) Feedstock form controls the property envelope and alloy-design space
**Powder-bed processes demand powder-bed physics; wire processes inherit welding physics.**
For LPBF, powder particle size directly governs minimum layer thickness and thus achievable feature size; typical Al powder size (15–50 µm) limits layer thickness to ~≥30 µm, and smaller powders (1–20 µm and even submicron) are a route to thinner layers and finer features. (balbaa2021roleofpowder pages 1-4)

Finer powders also change energy coupling: decreasing particle size increases specific surface area and can **enhance laser absorptivity** and consolidation kinetics (less heat required to melt particles), but very fine powders can become cohesive/agglomerated and introduce handling/ignition risks. (balbaa2021roleofpowder pages 1-4, balbaa2021roleofpowder pages 4-8)

Wire-fed WAAM avoids some powder handling issues and is often cheaper, but the geometric/property envelope is inherently narrower (thicker beads, coarser features) and the alloy space is constrained by wire availability and weldability; reviews note many high-strength Al families (notably **6xxx/7xxx and many 2xxx**) are problematic in WAAM due to weldability/hot cracking and melt-pool instability. (ozel2023areviewon pages 5-6)

### 5) Why fine, spherical, low-oxygen powder is a prerequisite for high-performance AM aluminum
Powder-bed fusion requires uniform thin layers and stable melting; therefore powder **sphericity and low contamination** are fundamental.

Spherical powders are preferred because they increase packing density and flowability needed to create a continuous, uniformly thick powder layer; flowability depends on particle size distribution and also on **oxides and moisture presence**. (trevisan2018studyandcharacterisation pages 63-66)

Aluminum powder is highly oxygen-reactive: oxide layers are commonly present on Al powders and can alter laser absorption and melt behavior; incorporated oxides can become internal defects that reduce mechanical and fatigue properties. (trevisan2018studyandcharacterisation pages 63-66)

Moisture/oxygen pickup in Al powders measurably degrades LPBF performance: experiments on powder spreadability found AlSi10Mg particularly susceptible to moisture and oxygen pickup, causing reduced spreadability and relative density; moisture is linked to flowability problems, porosity formation and enhanced oxidation. (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 1-2)

At the process-atmosphere level, a powder-bed melting study emphasizes it is **mandatory** to control and maintain low oxygen during Al processing to avoid negative impacts on tensile strength and fatigue resistance, showing that oxygen control is an explicit prerequisite for qualified performance. (bauer2016effectofprocess pages 1-4)

### 6) Role of ultrasonic atomization in enabling small-batch, novel-composition powders for alloy discovery
A key barrier to Al alloy discovery for LPBF is that gas atomization is capital-intensive and typically optimized for high-volume production of standard compositions. Multiple sources therefore motivate **small, flexible powder-production routes** to evaluate new or expensive alloys. (dietrich2016anewapproach pages 1-3)

Ultrasonic atomization (UA) and ultrasonic plasma atomization (UPA) address this by enabling **composition-flexible, small-to-medium-scale powder production** with high sphericity and controlled PSD:

* UA “allows flexibility in alloy composition” due to its arc-melting-based principle, and was concluded to “perfectly meet the requests for alloy development for powder bed fusion AM” in a study explicitly positioned around alloy-development powders. (hinrichs2021flexiblepowderproduction pages 1-2)
* Ultrasonic powder atomization configurations can use plasma/arc/induction melting, enabling processing of a wide range of materials; operation in pure argon with **O2 <100 ppm** was reported in an Al-matrix composite UA study, supporting low-oxygen processing environments for reactive compositions. (jedynak2024processabilityofaluminummatrix pages 1-3)
* A broader UPA review describes ultrasonic plasma atomization as able to integrate **alloying and fine powder production in a single process** and emphasizes minimized contamination and high sphericity powders (reported **>0.9** sphericity), which is well aligned with rapid-turn, small-batch powder production for exploring new AM alloy chemistries. (sojoodi2025integrationofcircular pages 1-2)

### 7) Why LPBF aluminum is industrial, while DED/WAAM and EBM aluminum lag
Taken together, the evidence supports a coherent explanation:

1. **Qualification maturity and controllability:** LPBF has an established monitoring/traceability ecosystem and demonstrated building-block qualification workflows for Al alloys; DED/WAAM remain more variable and post-processing-dependent. (calignano2019ametalpowder pages 11-13, arena2024novelaluminumalloy pages 2-4, popov2021powderbedfusion pages 18-19)
2. **Geometric capability:** LPBF achieves much finer features and higher precision than DED/WAAM and EB‑PBF, enabling high-value aerospace/industrial designs (thin walls, internal channels, lattices) where Al’s lightweight advantage matters most. (imran2024advancementsin3d pages 9-10, guarinoUnknownyearphdschoolin pages 22-25, popov2021powderbedfusion pages 18-19)
3. **Microstructure/property envelope:** LPBF’s high cooling rates enable microstructural refinement and, with tailored alloy chemistries, high strength/fatigue potential; DED/WAAM’s lower cooling rates and welding-like defects (oxide/hydrogen porosity, hot cracking) constrain consistent high performance. (wang2024areviewon pages 5-7, imran2024advancementsin3d pages 9-10, rodriguezgonzalez2023wirearcadditive pages 9-10)
4. **Feedstock prerequisite and alloy discovery:** LPBF performance depends on fine, spherical, low-oxygen powders and strict oxygen control; ultrasonic atomization provides a path to produce such powders at smaller scale with composition flexibility, accelerating alloy development for LPBF in ways that wire-based routes cannot easily match. (bauer2016effectofprocess pages 1-4, trevisan2018studyandcharacterisation pages 63-66, hinrichs2021flexiblepowderproduction pages 1-2)

Figure evidence supporting the powder-size→surface-quality linkage in LPBF is provided by Balbaa et al., who report comparative surface topography/roughness differences between fine (D50≈9 µm) and coarse (D50≈40 µm) powders in LPBF AlSi10Mg, illustrating that powder characteristics measurably affect surface quality and densification behavior.
