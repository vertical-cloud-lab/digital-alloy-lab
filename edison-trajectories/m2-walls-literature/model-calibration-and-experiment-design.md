**Eagar–Tsai calibration for 316L.** Whalen, Ma, and Balachandran (2021; DOI: 10.1007/s40192-021-00238-z) performed Bayesian inference on a modified Eagar–Tsai (E-T) conduction model using approximately 30 316L single-track melt-pool depth measurements combined from the Bertoli et al. and Kamath et al. datasets (layer thicknesses 30 and 50 µm). The calibrated parameters were apparent absorptivity and powder-bed porosity; beam radius was specified (average of 55 and 52 µm from the source studies) rather than inferred (whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 9-12, whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 4-7). The likelihood assumed 15 µm depth standard deviation for measurement variability, with weakly informative normal priors: mean absorptivity of 0.68 for SS316L, mean porosity of 0.40, both constrained to [0, 1] (whalenUnknownyearusingbayesianinferencea pages 3-4). MCMC was used to generate posteriors. The fitted power-dependent absorptivity was A = 0.148 ln(P) − 0.0879 (R² = 0.77) and porosity φ = 0.0015P + 0.1626 (R² = 0.96) (whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 16-19). The principal remaining discrepancy is that conduction physics underpredicts melt-pool depth once keyholing sets in, and isolated experimental conduction-mode tracks were misclassified as keyhole (whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 16-19). No separate Kennedy–O'Hagan model-discrepancy Gaussian process was used in that work. The calibration was also performed for Ti-6Al-4V and IN718, with analogous prior absorptivities of 0.74 and 0.67, respectively (whalenUnknownyearusingbayesianinferencea pages 3-4). **This is strongly supported as the primary Bayesian E-T calibration for 316L.**

**Texas A&M (TAMU) framework.** Zhang et al. (2021; DOI: 10.1016/j.addma.2021.102018) calibrated the E-T model against single-track experiments using a Kennedy–O'Hagan-style framework: a GP surrogate trained from 1,000 E-T simulations captured forward-model behavior, and a GP discrepancy function δ(x) absorbed systematic model–experiment differences (zhang2021anefficientframework pages 4-5). Materials included Ni-5Nb (40 single tracks; absorptivity 0.51, thermal conductivity 70.4 W/(m·K), specific heat 636.19 J/(kg·K)), NiTi (60 tracks; absorptivity 0.56), and AF9628 (absorptivity approximated from published 316L measurements) (zhang2021anefficientframework pages 12-14, zhang2021anefficientframework pages 9-10). Calibrated width and depth errors for one case were 3.6% and 4.05% MAPE, respectively (zhang2021anefficientframework pages 10-12). Errors increased for linear energy density below 300 J/m, requiring additional experiments in that regime (zhang2021anefficientframework pages 10-12, zhang2021anefficientframework pages 9-10). Keyholing was classified by width-to-depth thresholds: W/D ≤ 1.2 for Ni-5Nb and W/D ≤ 2 for NiTi (zhang2021anefficientframework pages 14-16, zhang2021anefficientframework pages 10-12). Experiments were on a 3D Systems ProX 200 DMP (1070 nm fiber laser, max 260 W) under argon (zhang2021anefficientframework pages 7-9).

**FE model calibration with GP emulator.** Fody, Leser, and Narra (2025 preprint; DOI: 10.2139/ssrn.5185945) calibrated a Goldak-inspired 3D FE thermal model for Ti-6Al-4V on an EOS M 290. Three parameters were inferred: effective absorptivity (aeff), normalized heat-source depth (Ds), and normalized heat-source radius (Rs), with uniform priors on [0.01, 100], [0.8, 100], and [0.4, 10], respectively (fody2025probabilisticcalibrationof pages 8-12). Data comprised 29/25/29 melt-pool cross-section images per condition at three P–v pairs. A heteroscedastic GP emulator was trained with 6 Latin-hypercube seeds plus 32 active-learning evaluations per calibration (38 total training points), and sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) generated 40,000 posterior samples (fody2025probabilisticcalibrationof pages 15-20, fody2025probabilisticcalibrationof pages 8-12). A strong identifiability challenge required imposing a maximum-temperature constraint: without it, absorptivity, radius, and depth were highly correlated, admitting a continuous range of similarly likely posteriors (fody2025probabilisticcalibrationof pages 15-20). With the constraint, all 29 observations fell within the 95.4% posterior-predictive contour; surrogate-to-FE validation MAPE was 0.53% (fody2025probabilisticcalibrationof pages 15-20, fody2025probabilisticcalibrationof pages 23-27). Withholding ~25% of melt-pool sections produced qualitatively similar calibrations, suggesting the data budget could potentially be reduced (fody2025probabilisticcalibrationof pages 23-27).

**Practical implication for the planned build.** These studies consistently show that ~25–60 single-track or cross-section melt-pool measurements per alloy or condition are sufficient for Bayesian calibration, but the remaining discrepancy is dominated by the physics absent from conduction models (Marangoni convection, vapor depression, absorptivity transitions). Measuring both melt-pool width and depth (not just one) substantially improves parameter identifiability.

**Texas A&M automated framework.** Sheikh et al. (2023 preprint, DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2304.04113; 2024 peer-reviewed, DOI: 10.1038/s41524-024-01436-x) constructed printability maps using CALPHAD-derived properties (Thermo-Calc databases including TCHEA5 and TCNI11) fed to the E-T model, with Gladush–Smurov correction for keyhole depth (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 3-5, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 14-15). They evaluated 12 combinations of two LOF criteria, two balling criteria, and three keyhole criteria (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 8-10). For 316 stainless steel, evaluated against 83 literature single-track data points on a 20×15 P–v grid (beam diameter 80 µm, layer thickness 30 µm, hatch spacing 70 µm), the best combination achieved 83.96% overall accuracy (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 10-14). The highest LOF-specific accuracy was 87.95% (LOF1–KH3–Ball2) (sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 8-10). For NiTi, the best criterion set (Eq. 4 for LOF, Eq. 6 for balling, Eq. 10 for keyhole) achieved 84–91% accuracy across three compositions (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 20-25). **Balling was consistently the weakest classification**, largely because experimental balling points tended to fall near boundaries with other defect regimes (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 20-25). The ensemble probabilistic map weights each of the 12 combinations by its accuracy using a softmax function, so that more accurate criteria contribute more strongly (sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 5-6).

**Gan et al. Keyhole number.** Gan et al. (2021; DOI: 10.1038/s41467-021-22704-0) defined the dimensionless Keyhole number Ke as a function of absorbed power ηP, density ρ, heat capacity Cp, thermal diffusivity α, scan speed Vs, spot radius r0, and the liquidus-to-substrate temperature difference (Tl − T0) (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 1-2). The regime boundaries are: conduction mode for Ke < 1.4, transition mode for 1.4 ≤ Ke ≤ 6.0, and keyhole mode for Ke > 6.0 (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 1-2, gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 2-3). The normalized keyhole aspect ratio follows e* = 0.4(Ke − 1.4) (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 1-2). For stability: keyhole fluctuations are small and porosity is nearly absent for Ke < 16; the keyhole becomes chaotic with significant porosity and spatter risk for Ke > 30 (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 3-5, gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 2-3). A porosity-volume-fraction scaling law using Ke together with normalized energy density achieved R² = 0.92 across six independent literature datasets and ten process-parameter sets (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 3-5). **This criterion applies to quasi-steady-state track interiors only**—not to scan starts, stops, or turning points (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 6-7). This is strongly supported by synchrotron X-ray imaging and high-fidelity multiphysics simulations.

**316L-specific defect criteria.** Smith et al. (2024; DOI: 10.1007/s11837-024-06946-z) demonstrated that nine parameter sets at constant VED = 85 J/mm³ on a 3D Systems DMP Flex 350 with 316L produced systematically different defect populations, definitively showing that VED alone is not a sufficient predictor (smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 1-2, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 10-11). They proposed two modifications: (i) a semi-elliptical interpass LOF criterion using measured melt-pool width and depth (replacing semicircular assumptions), and (ii) a laser-shock-pressure criterion for spatter-induced intrapass LOF at high P and high v (smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 1-2, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 3-5). The modified criteria better captured defects at the low-P/low-v and high-P/high-v extremes, respectively (smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 10-11). **Weakly supported: no overall classification accuracy metric was reported in the accessible portions of that paper.**

Wang, Ning, and Liang (2021; DOI: 10.3390/app112412053) provided analytical threshold predictions for Ti-6Al-4V (LOF ~110 J/mm³), IN718 (LOF ~160 J/mm³), and 316L (properties: density 7633 kg/m³, thermal conductivity 25 W/(m·°C), specific heat 594 J/(kg·°C), absorptivity 0.35), using geometric LOF (H²/W² + L²/D² ≤ 1), capillary balling (πw/l > 1), and normalized-enthalpy keyholing criteria (wang2021analyticalpredictionof pages 6-7, wang2021analyticalpredictionof pages 4-6).

### 3. Experiment Design for Separating Absorptivity from Spot Size and Heat Accumulation

**Absorptivity measurements for 316L.** Trapp et al. (2017; DOI: 10.1016/j.apmt.2017.08.006) performed in situ calorimetric absorptivity measurements on bare polished 316L discs and ~100 µm powder-coated layers at 30–540 W and 100–1500 mm/s, using a 60 ± 5 µm Gaussian spot at 1070 nm (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 2-3). Key findings: bare-plate absorptivity in conduction mode was approximately 0.29–0.31; at the keyhole threshold (~70 W at 500 mm/s, or ~48 ± 10 W at 100 mm/s and ~108 ± 20 W at 1500 mm/s), absorptivity rose sharply and saturated at approximately 0.78 (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 3-4, trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 6-7). Powder layers initially exhibited about 2× the bare-plate absorptivity, but this dropped sharply once the powder was fully melted; above the keyhole transition, powder and bare surfaces showed broadly similar absorptivity behavior, with saturation around 0.7–0.8 for steel (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 7-8, trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 1-2). This is a strongly supported, highly cited (662 citations) primary source.

**Spot size / defocus experiments.** Metelkova et al. (2018; DOI: 10.1016/j.addma.2018.08.006; 366 citations) systematically varied defocus on 316L at ±8 mm beyond the Rayleigh length, producing beam diameters of ~205 and ~262 µm, combined with P = 400–800 W and v = 50–250 mm/s (metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 3-4). At constant P and v, changing defocus from +8 to −8 mm increased melt-pool depth by 204% while width changed only 22%; at lower power, the depth difference was smaller (+81%) (metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 6-8). They reported a potential productivity increase of 840% (reaching 18.8 mm³/s) using a defocused hull-core strategy (metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 1-3). Tenbrock et al. (2020; DOI: 10.1016/j.jmatprotec.2019.116514; 179 citations) used top-hat beam profiles at 100, 150, and 200 µm spot diameters with 140–300 W in 316L LPBF, explicitly noting that larger raw beam diameters shorten the Rayleigh length, requiring careful beam characterization (tenbrock2020influenceofkeyhole pages 2-3). Sow et al. (2020; DOI: 10.1016/j.addma.2020.101532; 254 citations) compared 80 µm Gaussian and 500 µm top-hat spots: the large spot produced shallow conduction-mode pools, suppressed vaporization and spatter, achieved near-zero porosity, and provided a 20–50% build-rate increase, while the 80 µm spot showed 0.3–0.5% porosity from keyhole instabilities (sow2020influenceofbeam pages 7-8, sow2020influenceofbeam pages 1-3).

**Practical strategy for separating effects.** To deconfound absorptivity from beam size and from heat accumulation, the literature supports:  
(i) Varying defocus at fixed P and v to isolate beam-diameter effects (Metelkova 2018);  
(ii) Constant P/v lines at different speeds to separate linear energy density from interaction time and intensity;  
(iii) Bare-plate single tracks versus powder-on-plate single tracks to isolate powder effects (Trapp 2017);  
(iv) Single tracks versus multi-track walls versus cubes to isolate heat accumulation from neighboring tracks and layers (Zhang et al. 2021 used single tracks for calibration, then density coupons for validation).

### 4. Should a Second Spot Size Be Added to the First Build?

The literature strongly supports that spot size is a physically independent variable—not merely a secondary effect of VED—because it determines local intensity, Rayleigh length, keyhole onset threshold, and melt-pool aspect ratio independently of P and v (tenbrock2020influenceofkeyhole pages 2-3, metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 3-4, sow2020influenceofbeam pages 7-8). Metelkova et al. showed that in 316L LPBF, defocus alone can change melt-pool depth by over 200% at constant P and v (metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 6-8). Normalized enthalpy ΔH/hs, which explicitly includes beam diameter, provides a more physically continuous relationship with melt-pool depth than VED across different speeds (metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 3-4, metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 6-8).

**Recommendation (moderately supported by synthesis of multiple studies):** The first build of 84 walls at a fixed 130 µm spot, with 60 P–v combinations spanning the full conduction-to-keyhole range, provides strong constraints on the E-T or Thermo-Calc AM model at that one spot size. However, without a second spot size, absorptivity and effective beam radius are confounded in any calibration: the same melt-pool geometry can be reproduced by trading one for the other (fody2025probabilisticcalibrationof pages 15-20). Given the practical constraint that the Concept Laser M2 has limited defocus control (tenbrock2020influenceofkeyhole pages 2-3), **the safest strategy is to save a dedicated defocus build for the second build**, where it can be properly characterized with beam-caustic measurements and the initial E-T calibration can be used as a prior. If a small defocus experiment (e.g., 5–10 walls at a 50–80 µm or ~200 µm defocused spot) can be added to the first build without compromising the core design, this would provide early data on spot-size sensitivity. However, this requires verifying that the defocused spot profile is known and stable (tenbrock2020influenceofkeyhole pages 2-3).

### 5. Batch Bayesian Optimization and Active Learning for LPBF

**McCarthy (2025 thesis, Ti-6Al-4V m-LPBF).** The initial design used a central composite design (CCD) with factorial, axial, and center points. Each subsequent active-learning iteration selected 24 unique feature vectors plus 8 center replicates from the CCD (mccarthy2025surrogatebasedmultiobjectiveoptimization pages 61-65). The acquisition function was a greedy predictive-variance criterion (Zhao et al.'s weight information gain/expected model change maximization), applied to 1,000 randomly subsampled candidates per iteration (mccarthy2025surrogatebasedmultiobjectiveoptimization pages 61-65). The study was constrained to three total prints/builds (one initial + two active-learning iterations) due to cost and timeline (mccarthy2025surrogatebasedmultiobjectiveoptimization pages 61-65). Center replicates were analyzed with a fixed-effect one-way ANOVA to test whether batch significantly affected relative density (mccarthy2025surrogatebasedmultiobjectiveoptimization pages 50-56). Non-dominated solutions from NSGA-II optimization were not experimentally validated (mccarthy2025surrogatebasedmultiobjectiveoptimization pages 12-16).

**Pawlak (2025, AZ31 PBF-LB/M; DOI: 10.3390/app152412968).** A total of 180 samples were produced and evaluated for relative density and build rate (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 1-2). Three initialization strategies were compared: Latin Hypercube Sampling (V1), balanced-marginal selection (V2), and prior fractional-factorial sampling (V3), each with 20 initial samples (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 6-8). DGEMO and TSEMO algorithms then proposed batches of 10 parameter sets per MOBO iteration for three additional iterations (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 6-8). V3 (fractional-factorial initialization) achieved the highest hypervolume; TSEMO converged faster early while DGEMO explored more broadly (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 1-2, pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 8-11). Critically, **60–80% of proposed candidates in some batches were duplicates** due to design-space discretization, limiting effective search diversity and causing early saturation of hypervolume improvement (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 11-13). The author noted that conventional PBF-LB/M campaigns typically investigate 10–20 parameter sets to identify a viable process window (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 1-2).

**Zhang, Parnell, Brabazon, and Benavoli (2021; DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2107.12809).** In a binder-jetting 316L example, the initial design used a Taguchi L27 orthogonal array (27 settings, 4 replicates each), and BO selected batches of 2 new settings at a time (zhang2021bayesianoptimisationfor pages 14-16). They reviewed acquisition functions including EI, qEI, UCB, KG, entropy search, Thompson sampling, and their batch extensions (qKG, dKG, bUCB) (zhang2021bayesianoptimisationfor pages 8-11). One cited AM study initialized with a Latin-hypercube batch of 200 samples in 5D, followed by 35 MCMC-generated samples (zhang2021bayesianoptimisationfor pages 8-11).

**Multi-fidelity BO (Dou 2025).** A multi-fidelity framework combining inexpensive and expensive thermal simulations used UCB acquisition for high-fidelity sampling and maximum posterior standard deviation for low-fidelity sampling, with a fixed high-to-low fidelity ratio r = m/n (typically n = 1 high-fidelity evaluation per iteration) (dou2025smartprocessdesigna pages 88-92).

### 6. Thermo-Calc AM Module and CALPHAD-Coupled Models

The Thermo-Calc AM Module provides CALPHAD-based thermophysical properties (liquidus, solidus, latent heat, density, thermal conductivity, heat capacity, surface tension, viscosity) and reportedly includes both steady-state and transient melt-pool calculations. The TAMU automated framework (Sheikh et al. 2023/2024) uses Thermo-Calc databases including TCHEA5, TCAL7, and TCNI11 to supply these properties, which are then fed to the E-T analytical model for melt-pool prediction and printability-map generation (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 3-5). Sjöström, Durga, and Lindwall (2022; DOI: 10.3389/fmats.2022.797226) linked Simufact Additive thermal simulations with Thermo-Calc microscale models (DICTRA) for 18Ni300 maraging steel, predicting micro-segregation and martensite-start temperature as functions of process parameters (sjostrom2022linkageofmacro pages 1-2). Sargent et al. (2021; DOI: 10.3390/met11040570) used Thermo-Calc 2020b for CALPHAD-based non-equilibrium solidification modeling of SS316L, with material properties interpolated at 700 K: density 7.9 g/cm³, thermal conductivity 20.2 W/(m·K), and specific heat 470 J/(kg·K) (sargent2021integrationofprocessing pages 5-7).

**Important caveat:** No peer-reviewed independent validation of the Thermo-Calc AM Module's built-in printability-map predictions against experimental defect data was identified in the accessible literature. The available studies use Thermo-Calc primarily as a property provider rather than as an end-to-end validated printability tool. This is a **weakly supported gap**: the module may have been validated internally or in unpublished industrial work, but independent experimental benchmarks are lacking.

### 7. Recommendations for the Planned 84-Wall Build and Subsequent Bayesian-Optimization Builds

Based on the synthesized literature, the following design recommendations emerge:

**(a) First build (84 walls, 130 µm spot).** The 60 Sobol-sequence P–v combinations spanning P = 150–370 W and P/v = 0.09–0.55 J/mm (VED ~33–204 J/mm³) provide excellent coverage of the conduction-to-keyhole transition for a single spot size, consistent with the TAMU approach of 40–60 single-track experiments per alloy (zhang2021anefficientframework pages 9-10). The scrambled Sobol sequence is a space-filling quasi-random design well-suited for GP surrogate training. The 21 replicated pairs (walls printed in both orientations on the rotated fourth panel) serve dual purposes: quantifying recoater-direction effects and providing variance estimates essential for the likelihood in Bayesian calibration (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 3-4). The control walls at the production parameter set provide anchor points for model validation.

**(b) Melt-pool observables.** Measuring both melt-pool width and depth from the etched last-layer cross-sections is critical for identifiability, as demonstrated by Fody et al.'s finding that absorptivity, depth, and radius are non-identifiable from width–depth data alone without additional constraints (fody2025probabilisticcalibrationof pages 15-20). Measuring porosity fraction in each wall core (the planned >0.2 mm from faces, >1 mm from base/top exclusion) provides the defect classification needed for printability-map validation.

**(c) Calibration approach.** Use the wall porosity and melt-pool boundary data to: (i) fit apparent absorptivity and powder-bed porosity in the E-T model following Whalen et al.'s MCMC approach, with weakly informative priors centered at A ≈ 0.35–0.68 for 316L (whalenUnknownyearusingbayesianinferencea pages 3-4, wang2021analyticalpredictionof pages 6-7); (ii) add a GP discrepancy function following Zhang et al.'s Kennedy–O'Hagan framework to capture systematic model–experiment differences (zhang2021anefficientframework pages 4-5); (iii) classify each wall as defect-free, LOF, keyhole, or balling to benchmark the Thermo-Calc AM Module's printability predictions and the Gan et al. Ke criterion (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 1-2).

**(d) Second and later builds (Bayesian optimization).** The McCarthy (2025) precedent of 24 unique conditions + 8 center replicates per batch across 3 builds is a practical template (mccarthy2025surrogatebasedmultiobjectiveoptimization pages 61-65). With 84 wall positions available per build, the budget is generous: consider allocating ~60 BO-selected conditions, ~15 replicates at a few key settings (for variance estimation and batch-effect testing), and ~5–9 hold-out points from the first build's well-characterized settings (for validating the BO surrogate's predictive accuracy). The Pawlak (2025) finding that duplicate proposals can reach 60–80% in discrete spaces argues for including a distance-based penalization in the acquisition function or using a continuous-relaxation strategy (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 11-13). The first build's 60 calibrated settings serve as an informative initialization for the GP surrogate—superior to cold-start approaches (pawlak2025impactofinitialization pages 8-11).

**(e) Second spot size.** Defer the second spot size to Build 2 or 3, once the 130 µm model is calibrated and the actual defocused beam profile can be measured with a beam-caustic diagnostic. Even 10–15 walls at a well-characterized ~200 µm defocused spot would break the absorptivity–spot-size degeneracy (tenbrock2020influenceofkeyhole pages 2-3, metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 3-4). The normalized enthalpy ΔH/hs, which explicitly includes beam diameter, should be used rather than VED for cross-spot-size comparisons (metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 1-3, metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 6-8).
