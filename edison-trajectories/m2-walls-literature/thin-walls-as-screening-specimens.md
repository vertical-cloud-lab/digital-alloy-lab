## Representativeness of Thin LPBF Walls for Bulk-Material Parameter Screening

### Overview

Thin walls (~1.3 mm) printed in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) can provide useful relative rankings of process parameters but are subject to at least six systematic effects that can bias porosity and melt-pool depth relative to bulk material printed with the same P, v, hatch spacing, and layer thickness. The literature strongly supports the conclusion that **thin-wall results should be treated as screening data, not as absolute bulk-equivalent measurements**, and that finalist settings should be validated in wider cubes. ### 1. Heat Accumulation in Thin Walls and Small Cross-Sections

Heat accumulation is the best-documented concern. In LPBF 316L stainless steel on an SLM 280 HL (single 400 W Yb fibre laser, Ar, 100 °C plate preheat, 50 µm layer, 120 µm hatch, 275 W, 700 mm/s, VED ≈ 65 J/mm³), Mohr et al. measured a progressive rise in the pre-exposure (freshly recoated) surface temperature from ~100 °C at the base plate to ~545 °C at the top of a 114.5 mm cuboid when the inter-layer time (ILT) was only 18 s (mohr2020effectsofinter pages 3-4, mohr2020effectsofinter pages 6-8, mohr2020effectsofinter pages 8-9). At 65 s ILT the top reached ~230 °C, and at 116 s it was ~170 °C (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 9-10). These measurements were confirmed by a validated FEM model by Chaudry et al. (2022), which reproduced the layer-by-layer temperature rise to within ~7 °C for short ILT and ~30 °C for medium ILT (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 7-9, chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 9-10).

The consequences for melt-pool depth and porosity are significant. Melt-pool depth in the upper section of the short-ILT cuboids was 13–19% larger than at intermediate or long ILT for the same VED (mohr2020effectsofinter pages 8-9). At 275 W, 560 mm/s (high VED, ~82 J/mm³) and 18 s ILT, porosity in the upper section reached 1.1–2.7% (mostly spherical keyhole pores), compared with 0.1–0.2% in the lower section and ≤0.3% at longer ILT (mohr2020effectsofinter pages 8-9). Sub-grain size also increased from 458 µm² (low VED) to 1386 µm² (high VED) for short ILT at the top of the build, while hardness dropped from 221 HV1 to 176 HV1 within the same specimen (mohr2020effectsofinter pages 6-8, mohr2020effectsofinter pages 8-9). These ILT-dependent effects were also correlated with the ratio of area exploitation (RAE): the short ILT of 18 s corresponded to an RAE of only ~1%, representative of a single small part on a large platform (mohr2020effectsofinter pages 8-9).

For the user's build, each thin wall (~1.27 × 12.7 mm cross-section, ~7.6 mm tall) has a very short per-layer exposure time, but each comb part has seven walls plus a base, and twelve combs share the build. The effective ILT therefore depends on how many walls and how many parts are scanned before the recoater returns—a quantity that the Concept Laser M2 controller determines from the exposure order and can vary with parameter set. With 12 combs and ~84 walls, the layer time should be moderate, but higher-power / lower-speed walls that take longer to scan will experience a different ILT than fast, low-power walls on the same layer.

Piazza et al. (2020) independently demonstrated on miniature 316L parts (60–70 W, 25 µm layers) that melt-pool depth decreased linearly with build height in both cylindrical and conical geometries, attributed to heat accumulation raising the substrate temperature (piazza2020theeffectsof pages 3-7, piazza2020theeffectsof pages 7-9). They also confirmed that geometry affects the magnitude of the effect: the cross-sectional-area variation in a cone produced lower overall porosity than in a constant-section cylinder, because conical layers conduct heat differently (piazza2020theeffectsof pages 7-9, piazza2020theeffectsof pages 9-11).

**Strength of evidence:** Strong for 316L on SLM 280 HL; directly applicable in principle but quantitatively transferable to the Concept Laser M2 only with caution (different optics, chamber flow, build strategy). The referenced data are in Mohr et al. (2020, doi:10.1016/j.addma.2020.101080, Figs. 3, 7, 8, Table 1) and Chaudry et al. (2022, doi:10.1007/s40964-022-00282-x, Figs. 10, 12, 13).

### 2. Short Hatch Vectors, Scanner Turnaround, and Skywriting

In a ~1 mm-wide core (after contour exclusion), transverse hatch vectors are approximately 1 mm long. Each vector spends a large fraction of its duration in the acceleration and deceleration zones of the galvanometer mirrors. Martin et al. (2019) used in-situ synchrotron X-ray imaging at 316L-relevant conditions to show that during turnaround, the vapor depression deepens as the galvanometer decelerates and the laser lingers near-stationary. The depression peaked approximately 100 µs after the reversal and approached steady state only after ~1000 µs. As the scanner accelerated away into a pre-heated region, the depression rapidly collapsed and trapped gas, creating spherical pores. In their experiments, **87% of observed pores formed within 200 µm of the turnaround point** (martin2019dynamicsofpore pages 1-2). At a programmed speed of 1000 mm/s, 89% of pores formed in under 500 µs (martin2019dynamicsofpore pages 4-4).

**Skywriting** (turning the laser off at the end of each vector so that it does not expose the turnaround) does not reliably eliminate these pores: the abrupt transition from keyhole-mode exposure to zero power causes its own rapid depression collapse and can produce an end-of-track pore (martin2019dynamicsofpore pages 6-6). The proposed mitigation is smooth, controlled power modulation that avoids keyhole formation and prevents rapid pressure changes in the melt pool (martin2019dynamicsofpore pages 6-6).

Kim et al. (2021) systematically varied galvanometer acceleration and laser on/off delay on the NIST AMMT (IN625, 285 W, 960 mm/s, 0.11 mm hatch, 40 µm layers) and characterized the resulting pores by XCT. They identified two types of error: **following error** (galvo cannot physically follow the commanded acceleration, so actual track positions drift inward and do not reach the contour) and **synchronization error** (laser power switches too early or too late relative to galvo position). The galvo acceleration limit for the AMMT was ~10⁶ mm/s², and the calibrated position/power delay was 1.21 ms (plus ~0.05 ms for the laser to reach full power) (kim2021characterizingtheeffects pages 6-7, kim2021characterizingtheeffects pages 7-9). With a 50× higher commanded acceleration, tracks oscillated within the core and never reached the contour, leaving an annular lack-of-fusion zone (kim2021characterizingtheeffects pages 7-9). Revanur (2024) separately reports pipeline delays of 250–500 µs and point-exposure delays of 10–50 µs in LPBF galvo systems (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 83-86).

For the user's build, a ~1 mm transverse vector at ~1350 mm/s requires only ~0.74 ms of steady travel—comparable to the total galvo response/delay budget. The fraction of each vector spent in acceleration/deceleration transients is therefore much larger than in a 12.7 mm longitudinal vector. This means transverse-vector layers will have more turnaround-related defects per unit volume than longitudinal-vector layers. The Concept Laser island scanning strategy (5 × 5 mm islands, vectors rotated 90° between adjacent islands, islands shifted between layers) further multiplies turnaround events (zeng2015optimizationofsupport pages 44-49). At 1.27 mm wall width, the core is narrower than a standard island, so the software must either assign a single stripe or treat the wall as a narrow feature. The exact behavior should be verified from the exported build file or CL WRX logs.

**Strength of evidence:** Strong (Martin et al. 2019, doi:10.1038/s41467-019-10009-2, in Nature Communications with 922 citations; Kim et al. 2021, doi:10.1016/j.addma.2021.102372). Machine-specific galvo and delay parameters are not transferable from the AMMT to the Concept Laser M2, but the physics is universal.

### 3. Contour–Hatch Interaction

In the user's build, contours at 100–120 W and 130–220 mm/s with a 50–70 µm spot produce P/v ≈ 0.45–0.92 J/mm—substantially higher linear energy density than the core hatch P/v = 0.274 J/mm at the production setting. The focused, slow contour is therefore more likely to operate in keyhole mode, and the contour–core interface is a known location for porosity.

Du Plessis (2019) studied 316L (EOS parameters, XCT) and found that increasing the hatch-to-contour offset from the nominal 0.015 mm to 0.05 mm introduced visible subsurface porosity near the part edges; at 0.10 mm offset the porosity was severe (plessis2019effectsofprocess pages 3-7). Englert et al. (2022) found, in their XCT analysis of hatching versus concentric strategies, that keyhole pores were concentrated specifically between the perimeter and the hatching region (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 6-8). Kim et al. (2021) described near-surface pores close to the contour–core boundary, attributing them in part to laser control errors (following and synchronization errors) that truncate or extend tracks at the interface (kim2021characterizingtheeffects pages 3-4, kim2021characterizingtheeffects pages 4-6).

In a 1.27 mm wall, contour regions on both faces may each penetrate ~0.1–0.2 mm inward (melt-pool half-width plus heat-affected zone), consuming 0.2–0.4 mm of the total width. After excluding 0.2 mm from each face, the remaining core window is only ~0.87 mm wide. This is narrow enough that contour-induced thermal effects (preheating, remelting, keyhole remnants) may still reach the center, especially at higher contour powers. No primary source was found that quantifies the exact contour-free depth for the stated parameters; the user should map pore density as a function of distance from each face to establish this empirically.

**Strength of evidence:** Moderate. The mechanism is well supported, but quantitative width of the contour-affected zone has not been reported for the specific Concept Laser M2 parameters. Key references: du Plessis (2019, doi:10.1016/j.addma.2019.100871, Fig. 8) and Kim et al. (2021, doi:10.1016/j.addma.2021.102372).

### 4. Published High-Throughput Parameter Screening with Miniature Specimens

The closest published precedent is the NIST combinatorial LPBF approach by Weaver et al. (2021), who built a single specimen of IN625 containing 24 columns of 4 mm × 3 mm cross-section, each with a different combination of five laser powers and four scan speeds (60 unique conditions with post-processing variants). Porosity in the miniature combinatorial regions was below 0.03%, comparable to that of a larger 20 × 20 mm regular sample (weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 1-5). However, hardness was lower in the combinatorial regions, indicating a geometry or thermal-history effect, and **the authors explicitly cautioned that combinatorial-sample information is not equivalent to regular-sample information and that validation against bulk specimens is necessary** (weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 33-37). They recommended thicker porous dividers or unmelted-powder gaps between regions to reduce inter-column thermal coupling.

Fowler et al. (2024) at Sandia National Laboratories used 3 × 3 × 3 mm Kovar (Fe-29Ni-17Co) cubes in linear arrays on a Renishaw AM400 (50 µm layers), with 100–200 unique parameter sets per build plate, and characterized them by automated EBSD after surface grinding and dry electropolishing. They uncovered an **unexpected, strong dependence of microstructure on location within the build** even for identical laser parameters (fowler2024highthroughputmicrostructuralcharacterization pages 1-2, fowler2024highthroughputmicrostructuralcharacterization pages 2-4, fowler2024highthroughputmicrostructuralcharacterization pages 4-5). This finding underscores that plate-position effects (thermal environment, gas flow, spatter) can be as important as the intentionally varied parameters and must be blocked or randomized.

Agrawal and Thoma (2022) demonstrated high-throughput surface characterization for 316L process screening, turning off contour and upskin parameters to isolate core behavior. The user's choice to keep contours fixed is a defensible alternative but adds the contour-interaction confound discussed above.

**Strength of evidence:** Strong for the conclusion that miniature specimens can rank parameters, but moderate-to-weak for the claim that they reproduce absolute bulk density. Weaver et al. (2021, doi:10.1016/j.matdes.2021.109969, Figs. and Tables therein) is the most directly relevant reference.

### 5. Orientation Relative to Recoater and Gas Flow

The user's fourth panel is rotated 90° so that walls run across the recoater rather than along it, repeating 18 settings plus the control. This is a valuable blocking strategy. Several mechanisms can produce orientation-dependent results:

- **Recoater collision.** Tall, narrow walls that distort or swell above the powder plane can interfere with the recoater blade. Ti-6Al-4V thin walls showed substantial distortion even with supports, and unsupported walls could cause build failure (wu2020exploringthefabrication pages 8-10, wu2020exploringthefabrication pages 5-7). Walls perpendicular to the recoater present a leading edge on every stroke, while parallel walls may guide the blade along their length. The resulting powder-layer thickness variation can be asymmetric around the wall.

- **Gas-flow direction.** Spatter ejected from the melt pool is preferentially transported by the cross-flow. When scanning is opposite to the gas flow, larger particles decelerate and may redeposit on the same or adjacent walls; when aligned, spatter is more effectively carried away (cen2024modelingandsimulation pages 7-10, revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 18-22). Gas velocity decreases from inlet to outlet, creating a spatial gradient of spatter deposition and plume interaction (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 18-22). No primary study was found that quantitatively compares porosity in thin walls parallel versus perpendicular to the recoater or gas flow for 316L on a Concept Laser M2.

- **Plate-position and optical effects.** Laser incidence angle, focus offset, and spot ellipticity vary with distance from the optical axis. These can alter melt-pool depth and width systematically across the plate. The Fowler et al. (2024) observation of location-dependent microstructure at identical parameters is consistent with such effects (fowler2024highthroughputmicrostructuralcharacterization pages 2-4).

**Strength of evidence:** Mechanistic reasoning is strong, but quantitative orientation-specific data for the user's geometry and machine are absent. The user's paired-orientation design is itself a valuable contribution to the field. Key reference: Wu et al. (2020, doi:10.1007/s00170-020-05827-4).

### 6. Minimum Wall Thickness and Measurement Area for Porosity

No universally standardized minimum section area, pore count, or sampling protocol exists for LPBF metallographic porosity (sola2019microstructuralporosityin pages 11-12, sola2019microstructuralporosityin pages 16-18). Sola and Nouri (2019) emphasize that microscopic porosity measurements lack standardized imaging methods and are biased by magnification, focus, sampling location, and the number of images analyzed (sola2019microstructuralporosityin pages 11-12). Shahabi et al. (2022, doi:10.1016/j.matchar.2022.112027) developed a statistical framework for determining data requirements for porosity characterization in LPBF; this paper was unobtainable for detailed review but is the key primary reference for this question.

In the user's design, after excluding 0.2 mm from each face and 1 mm from the top and bottom, the analysis window in each wall is approximately 0.87 mm × 5.6 mm ≈ 4.9 mm². This is comparable to but smaller than the 3 × 4 mm = 12 mm² regions used by Weaver et al. (2021), where porosity was <0.03% (weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 1-5). In the dense regime (<0.1%), a 4.9 mm² section at SEM resolution (sub-micrometer pixels) may contain only a few countable pores, making the area-fraction estimate highly uncertain. At higher porosity (>0.5%), the section may contain enough pores for a reasonable estimate.

Choi et al. (2024) addressed this by measuring all four longitudinal machined faces of Ti-6Al-4V fatigue bars, noting that multiple faces approach the bulk pore-size distribution better than a single section (choi2024opticalmicroscopyon pages 3-6). For the user's walls, measuring both halves of the bisected wall (mirror sections) would double the sampled area.

**Practical guidance:** The user's exclusion zones (0.2 mm from faces, 1 mm from base and top) are reasonable but should be justified empirically by plotting pore density versus distance from each boundary. The 0.2 mm face exclusion should be verified against the actual contour melt-pool boundary. All reported porosity values should include the analyzed area, pixel size, minimum detectable pore size, number of pores detected, and a confidence interval (e.g., Clopper–Pearson or bootstrap). Convergence can be assessed by progressively enlarging the analysis window and observing stability.

**Strength of evidence:** Moderate. The statistical framework exists (Shahabi et al. 2022) but was not fully reviewed. The qualitative guidance is well supported by multiple sources. Key additional reference: Sola and Nouri (2019, doi:10.1002/amp2.10021).

### Summary Assessment

Thin LPBF walls of ~1.3 mm thickness can serve as an efficient screening tool for ranking process parameters by porosity and melt-pool depth, as demonstrated by analogous miniature-specimen approaches (weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 1-5, weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 33-37, fowler2024highthroughputmicrostructuralcharacterization pages 2-4). However, they are **not** unconditionally representative of bulk material because:

1. **Heat accumulation** raises the temperature of thin, tall features and shifts the melt-pool regime toward deeper/wider pools and potentially into keyhole porosity—especially at high P/v and short ILT (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 7-9, mohr2020effectsofinter pages 8-9).
2. **Short scan vectors** in a ~1 mm core compress the steady-state fraction of each track and amplify turnaround-pore mechanisms (martin2019dynamicsofpore pages 4-4, martin2019dynamicsofpore pages 6-6, martin2019dynamicsofpore pages 1-2).
3. **Contour–core overlap** occupies a significant fraction of the 1.27 mm width, mixing contour and core porosity signatures (plessis2019effectsofprocess pages 3-7, englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 6-8, kim2021characterizingtheeffects pages 3-4).
4. **Orientation and position on the plate** introduce systematic biases from recoater interaction, gas-flow-dependent spatter, and optical-axis effects (wu2020exploringthefabrication pages 8-10, fowler2024highthroughputmicrostructuralcharacterization pages 2-4, cen2024modelingandsimulation pages 7-10).
5. **Small measurement area** limits the statistical precision of porosity estimates in the dense regime (sola2019microstructuralporosityin pages 11-12, weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 1-5).

The user's experimental design already incorporates several strong mitigations: scrambled Sobol sampling, repeated controls, two orientations, and SEM imaging. Additional recommended steps include (a) archiving the actual CL WRX scan files so that vector lengths, exposure order, and ILT can be reconstructed; (b) mapping pore density versus distance from each face to identify the contour-free zone; (c) comparing the production-set control walls with the production-set base blocks; and (d) validating finalist settings in wider (≥10 × 10 mm) density cubes before using the results to calibrate the Thermo-Calc AM Module or to seed Bayesian optimization.
