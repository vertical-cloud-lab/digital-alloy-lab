## Best Practice for Preparing and Imaging Cross-Sections of LPBF 316L Thin Walls: Porosity and Melt-Pool Depth Measurement by SEM

### 1. Cutting, Mounting, Grinding, and Polishing

**Cutting.** Cross-sections should be produced using precision diamond saws with water cooling or lubrication to minimize heat damage and surface deformation (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 4-5, sala2025theroleof pages 4-5). Abrasive cut-off wheels, while faster, produce a wider kerf and more subsurface deformation; if used, they must be selected specifically for austenitic stainless steels and followed by sufficient grinding to remove the heat-affected zone (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 4-5). Wire-cutting (EDM) has also been used to extract sections more than 5 mm from build surfaces, with section thicknesses of approximately 2 mm (zheng2021defectanalysisof pages 4-6). For the described thin-wall geometry (1.27 mm thick), precision diamond sawing is preferred because it produces a narrow kerf (~0.3–0.5 mm) with minimal subsurface deformation, which is critical when grinding must stop at a defined centreline plane. Protecting surfaces from clamping damage with rubber or metal shims is recommended (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 4-5).

**Conductive hot mounting and edge retention.** For SEM imaging, conductive mounts are essential. Westin et al. report positive experience with phenolic resin (PhenoCure™) and epoxy-based (EpoMet® G) mounting compounds (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 4-5). Cold-mount epoxies provide lower shrinkage and better edge retention for microstructure analysis but require several hours to cure and are softer, which may reduce polishing efficiency (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 4-5). For the top edge of a thin wall where melt-pool depth is measured in the last layer, edge retention is critical; conductive epoxy mounting or use of a nickel-plating or copper-plating technique before mounting can protect the edge from rounding during grinding and polishing. Chamfering or rounding of mount edges before grinding extends SiC paper and polishing cloth life (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 7-9).

**Grinding and polishing sequence.** The recommended automated preparation sequence, adapted from Westin et al. (doi:10.1007/s40194-025-02286-x, Tables 3–4) for 25 mm diameter embedded stainless steel specimens on a Struers Tegramin 30, is as follows:

| 2 | SiC P1000 paper | Water | 2:00 | 35 | 300 | 150 |

This protocol was developed for DED-arc stainless steel welds and AM deposits and has proven suitable for austenitic stainless steels with varying microstructures (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 7-9). Ronneberg et al. used a similar approach for LPBF 316L (200 W, 750 mm/s, 110 µm hatch, 50 µm layer, chessboard strategy on a Concept Laser): SiC grinding papers, diamond suspensions down to 1 µm, and final polishing with 0.06 µm (60 nm) colloidal silica suspension, followed by electrolytic etching (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3). Vallejo et al. used SiC grinding papers and diamond paste polishing with a 0.05 µm colloidal silica final finish on LPBF 316L built on an SLM 125HL (200 W, 800 mm/s, 0.12 mm hatch, 0.03 mm layer) (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 4-5).

**Controlling material removal depth.** When grinding to a centreline plane, one approach is to mark the target plane with a permanent pen on the outer surface and account for expected grinding consumption (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 4-5). Inspection every 30 seconds during hand grinding—checking that the surface is flat, fully exposed, and free of misaligned scratches—is recommended (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 7-9). For automated preparation, the planar grinding step (SiC 500 or grinding stone) is repeated until all samples in the holder are planar before proceeding.

### 2. How Polishing Changes Measured Porosity

**Smearing.** Mechanical grinding and polishing can plastically deform metal over pore openings, partially or fully closing small voids and causing the measured porosity to underestimate the true value. This effect is widely recognized (kan2022acriticalreview pages 1-3, bahshwan2026fastcharacterizationof pages 1-3). Bahshwan (doi:10.3390/ma19214527) noted that "artifacts such as smeared pores can arise due to polishing" in LPBF 316L, though the magnitude was not quantified (bahshwan2026fastcharacterizationof pages 1-3). Xavier et al. (doi:10.1007/s00170-019-04597-y) similarly noted that grinding and polishing "may affect the sizes and shapes of pores due to metal smearing" (sola2019microstructuralporosityin pages 11-12). Colloidal silica (OP-S) has a mild chemical–mechanical attack that helps remove the deformed layer, but over-polishing can create relief at phase boundaries and alter contrast (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 9-10).

**Comparison with X-ray CT and Archimedes.** Methods do not always agree, and the direction of discrepancy is not universal. In one LPBF 316L comparison by Zheng et al. (doi:10.3390/coatings11121562), optical metallography measured approximately 3.70% porosity, Archimedes gave 3.88%, and XCT yielded 3.00% for the same high-porosity sample, with the difference partly attributed to XCT's inability to resolve defects smaller than ~5 µm (zheng2021defectanalysisof pages 14-17). In a low-porosity LPBF 316L study by Yusuf et al. (doi:10.3390/met7020064), Archimedes measured ~0.82% average porosity while XCT measured ~0.61% (yusuf2017investigationonporosity pages 1-3). Tajalli et al. (doi:10.1007/s13632-026-01349-9) compared OM, Archimedes, and XCT for 316L-2.5Cu and found close agreement for dense samples but divergence for samples with localized defect concentrations (tajalli2026multimethodanalysisof pages 12-15). XCT is generally considered more representative for three-dimensional pore characterization but has resolution limits; Archimedes provides only bulk density without pore-level information and is sensitive to surface defects (aydogan2024reviewofin pages 17-19, tajalli2026multimethodanalysisof pages 1-2). **Strongly supported conclusion:** no single method is unbiased, and cross-validation is essential (kan2022acriticalreview pages 1-3).

**Checking for artifacts.** Repeating the final polishing step and re-imaging can reveal whether apparent pore closure changes with additional material removal. Comparison with Archimedes density on the same specimen provides an independent bulk check. When feasible, CT scanning before sectioning provides a ground-truth reference (kan2022acriticalreview pages 1-3).

### 3. Imaging Porosity: Optical vs. SEM BSE/SE, Magnification, Area, and Standards

**Imaging mode.** Ronneberg et al. used a Hirox RH-2000 digital microscope for porosity characterisation at a resolution of 2.61 µm/pixel and separately used a Hitachi S-3400N SEM with backscattered electron (BSE) detection at 13 kV accelerating voltage and ~8 mm working distance for microstructural examination of etched samples (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3). BSE imaging on unetched, polished surfaces provides strong atomic-number contrast between the steel matrix and voids (appearing black), making segmentation straightforward. SE imaging also shows pores via topographic contrast but can be confused by polishing scratches. For the Thermo Fisher Apreo C (a field-emission SEM), BSE imaging at 10–15 kV is generally preferred for porosity because it provides flat, composition-sensitive contrast with minimal topographic noise.

**Magnification and pixel size.** Vallejo et al. used 100× optical magnification with five micrographs per section for porosity quantification (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 4-5). Ronneberg et al. used 2.61 µm/pixel and stitched mosaics covering at least 1000 mm² per section (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3). For SEM, comparable coverage requires lower magnification (≤100×) with automated stage tiling and stitching. The minimum resolved pore size is fundamentally limited by the pixel size: Ronneberg et al. filtered out objects smaller than 25 µm² (< 4 pixels), corresponding to an equivalent circular diameter of ~5.6 µm (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3). As a practical minimum, a pore should span at least 3–4 pixels in each direction to be reliably segmented.

**Area required for statistical significance.** Ronneberg et al. imaged >1000 mm² per section orientation for LPBF 316L at ~0.3–0.7% porosity to attain adequate statistics (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3, ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 3-5). For the described thin walls (~1.27 × 7.6 mm cross-section = ~9.7 mm² per wall), the analysable core area (≥0.2 mm from faces, ≥1 mm from base and top) is roughly 0.87 × 5.6 mm ≈ 4.9 mm² per wall, so seven walls per mount ≈ 34 mm² of core area. At very low porosity (~0.1%), this small area may contain few pores, increasing statistical uncertainty. ASTM E1245 (doi:10.31399/asm.hb.v09.a0003789) specifies automatic image analysis of inclusions or phases with measurement of area fraction and uncertainty, requiring a sufficient number of fields to reduce the 95% confidence interval to an acceptable fraction of the measured value (brown2016microstructureanalysisfor pages 11-15). **Weakly supported estimate:** for 0.1% porosity with ≤20% relative uncertainty, areas of several hundred mm² or more may be needed; at 1–5% porosity, tens of mm² can suffice (extrapolated from Ronneberg's practice of >1000 mm² for ~0.5% porosity).

**Number of sections per specimen.** Ronneberg et al. examined both horizontal and vertical sections for each build orientation to capture anisotropy and used the geometric mean of area-fraction porosity from two orthogonal sections to estimate volumetric relative density (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3, ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 3-5). For the thin-wall geometry where a single lengthwise centreline section is taken, one section per wall is the minimum; repeating on a parallel section offset by ~0.3 mm would check for spatial variability.

### 4. Segmentation and Pore Classification

**Thresholding.** Grey-level thresholding is the standard approach. Mohr et al. used a fixed threshold of 136 (on an unspecified grey scale) for all cross-sections, applied via IMS Client software (mohr2020effectsofinter pages 4-6). Ronneberg et al. used ImageJ's "Analyse Particles" function (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3). The PoreAnalyzer open-source tool (doi:10.3390/app11136086) and the RAPID tool (doi:10.3390/ma19214527) provide automated workflows for batch processing of optical micrographs with threshold tuning and morphological operators (ellendt2021poreanalyzer—anopensourceframework pages 2-4, bahshwan2026fastcharacterizationof pages 1-3). Otsu's method or manual threshold selection with visual verification are common; the key is consistency across all images.

**Pore classification criteria.** The following table summarizes literature-derived heuristics for distinguishing lack-of-fusion (LoF), keyhole, and gas pores based on 2D (circularity, aspect ratio) and 3D (sphericity) shape descriptors:

Key sources: Kan et al. (doi:10.1007/s10853-022-06990-7) describe gas pores as generally <100 µm and spherical, LoF pores as large and irregular, and keyhole pores as large and spherical (kan2022acriticalreview pages 1-3). Nudelis and Mayr (doi:10.3390/met11121912) proposed a 3D CT-based classification using sphericity ≥0.6, compactness ≥0.4, and BB-factor ≥0.6 for spherical pores, with diameter classes of ≤100 µm, 100–200 µm, and >200 µm (nudelis2021anovelclassification pages 4-7). Ellendt et al. (doi:10.3390/app11136086) used circularity ≥95% for gas pores and aspect ratio >5 for cracks in 2D optical analysis (ellendt2021poreanalyzer—anopensourceframework pages 2-4). These are heuristics rather than universal boundaries and should be validated for the specific process window.

### 5. Etching to Reveal Melt-Pool Boundaries

**Why porosity must be imaged before etching.** Chemical or electrolytic etching preferentially attacks grain boundaries, cell walls, and segregation-enriched melt-pool boundaries, creating grooves or relief that can be confused with pores and alter existing pore boundaries (zheng2021defectanalysisof pages 10-14). In low-energy samples, etching can make pores "almost invisible" by modifying surrounding contrast, while in over-melted samples it can create corrosion trenches within melt pools that mimic voids (zheng2021defectanalysisof pages 10-14). Therefore, porosity must be measured on unetched, polished surfaces first, followed by etching for microstructural examination.

**Which etchant is best for melt-pool boundaries?** Electrolytic 10% oxalic acid is the most widely used for LPBF 316L melt-pool boundary revelation, with conditions varying from 5 V / 90 s (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3) to 15 V / 60 s depending on the laboratory. The voltage and time control the attack intensity: lower voltage/longer time gives gentler, more controlled revelation. Ronneberg et al. specifically noted good results at 5 V / 90 s for revealing both melt-pool boundaries and the cellular solidification structure (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3). Mixed-acid reagent (HCl:acetic:HNO₃ = 3:2:1 by volume, 40–60 s immersion) was used by Vallejo et al. for detailed melt-pool and cellular analysis (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 4-5). Beraha II color etchant (10–30 s) was used by Mohr et al. for melt-pool depth determination and cell-size measurement (mohr2020effectsofinter pages 4-6). Aqua regia is aggressive and difficult to control for melt-pool boundary work. Kalling's No. 2 is mentioned by Krakhmalev et al. (doi:10.3390/met8080643) alongside electrolytic oxalic acid for LPBF 316L (krakhmalev2018microstructuresolidificationtexture pages 2-3), but explicit comparative optimization was not reported.

**Strongly supported:** electrolytic 10% oxalic acid at room temperature is the most commonly reported and best-validated etchant for LPBF 316L melt-pool boundaries. **Weakly supported:** mixed-acid immersion and Beraha II are viable alternatives; glyceregia and Carpenter's are not represented in the retrieved LPBF 316L literature.

### 6. Measuring Last-Layer Melt-Pool Depth and Width

**Which pools count.** Mohr et al. (doi:10.1016/j.addma.2020.101080) measured melt-pool depths by analysing ten melt pools of the last exposed layer on etched cross-sections, using Part 4 (the upper 10 mm) at separation plane II, for each combination of parameters (275 W, 560–933 mm/s, 50 µm layer, 120 µm hatch, SLM280 HL) (mohr2020effectsofinter pages 4-6). Soares (2026) averaged eight measurements per single track for width and depth, with measurement uncertainty of approximately ±5–40 µm combining track-to-track variability and operator-dependent boundary identification (soares2026processoptimizationofa pages 81-86, soares2026processoptimizationof pages 81-86). Vallejo et al. set the initial layer of the build so that the final top LPBF layer had no repeated laser melting, enabling clean measurement of melt-pool width and depth from that layer (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 2-4).

**Section angle distortion.** When a cross-section is cut at an angle θ to the scan direction, the apparent melt-pool width on the section is stretched by a factor of 1/cos(θ). For a 67° scan rotation between layers (common in LPBF), a longitudinal centreline section will be aligned with the scan direction of some layers and oblique to others. Schmid et al. (doi:10.1007/s40964-021-00173-7) noted that rotated scan directions (e.g., 67° between layers) make melt pools appear distorted and more difficult to analyse; they therefore used a 90° scan rotation for their automated melt-pool measurements (schmid2021anewapproach pages 1-3). For the described thin walls (12.7 mm long, sectioned at mid-length), if the last-layer scan direction runs along the wall length, the centreline section is transverse to the scan tracks and shows their true depth and width. If the scan runs across the wall thickness (1.27 mm), the section is along the scan direction and reveals elongated melt-pool profiles; depth is still measurable but width is not. Measuring only pools whose scan direction is approximately perpendicular to the section plane (within ±15–20°) is advisable.

**How many pools to average.** Based on the literature, 8–10 melt pools from the last layer is the minimum for a reliable average (soares2026processoptimizationofa pages 81-86, mohr2020effectsofinter pages 4-6). For a 12.7 mm long wall with 0.09 mm hatch spacing, the last layer contains ~140 tracks, so selecting 10–20 clearly resolved, non-edge pools is feasible. The measurement should exclude contour tracks (which use different parameters) and any pools at the wall ends where turn-around effects occur.

### 7. Realistic Hands-On Time

**Per mount (grinding and polishing).** The automated sequence in Table 3 (Westin et al.) totals approximately 27 minutes of machine time per batch of up to 6 samples (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 7-9). With cleaning between steps, sample inspection, and re-starts if needed, a realistic hands-on estimate is 45–60 minutes per batch including setup and cleanup. For 24 mounts in batches of 6, this is approximately 4 batches × 1 hour = 4–5 hours of polishing, plus cutting and mounting time (~20 min per mount for hot mounting, partially parallelisable). Total metallographic preparation: approximately 2–3 full working days for 24 mounts.

**Per SEM session.** Acquiring a stitched BSE mosaic of a ~10 mm² area at ~1 µm/pixel (comparable to the core analysis region of one wall) on a Thermo Fisher Apreo C requires approximately 15–30 minutes per wall section including setup, focus, and acquisition. With 7 walls per mount, one mount requires approximately 2–4 hours for porosity imaging. For 24 mounts, this represents approximately 48–96 hours of SEM time, or roughly 6–12 full 8-hour days of instrument time. Post-etching re-imaging for melt-pool depth (last layer only, smaller area) adds approximately 15–20 minutes per wall, or roughly 2 hours per mount for 7 walls. **Weakly supported:** these estimates are derived from procedure parameters rather than explicit time-motion studies.

### Summary of Key Artefacts That Bias Results

1. **Pore smearing during polishing** underestimates porosity by plastically closing small pores (kan2022acriticalreview pages 1-3, bahshwan2026fastcharacterizationof pages 1-3). Mitigation: adequate colloidal silica final polish; re-polish check; Archimedes or CT cross-validation.

2. **Pull-out** of loosely bonded particles (e.g., in lack-of-fusion regions) can create artificial voids that overestimate porosity. Using gentle polishing loads (20–30 N per sample) and diamond rather than alumina abrasives reduces this risk.

3. **Edge rounding** at the top of the thin wall reduces the measurable area for last-layer melt-pool depth and can cause apparent shallowing of pools. Edge retention by conductive epoxy mounting or electroless nickel plating before mounting is essential (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 4-5).

4. **Colloidal silica residue** can continue attacking the surface if not promptly removed, creating a "blurry white" appearance on ferrite and altering contrast (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 9-10). The final 30 seconds of the OP-S step should use water only (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 7-9).

5. **Section angle relative to scan direction** stretches the apparent melt-pool width by 1/cos(θ) (schmid2021anewapproach pages 1-3). Only pools with scan direction approximately perpendicular to the section should be measured for width.

6. **2D stereological bias** means that a single cross-section systematically underestimates the true 3D pore volume for non-spherical pores and can make irregular pores appear more circular than their 3D shape (cerezo2024tomographyoflaser pages 7-9).

7. **Etching artefacts** create grooves at melt-pool and cell boundaries that can be mistaken for pores if segmentation is applied to etched images (zheng2021defectanalysisof pages 10-14). Porosity must always be measured before etching.

8. **Insufficient area** for image analysis at low porosity levels (≤0.1%) increases statistical uncertainty. At least several hundred mm² should be imaged at ~0.5% porosity; at 0.1%, more area or multiple sections are needed (ronneberg2020revealingrelationshipsbetween pages 2-3).
