## The Thermo-Calc Additive Manufacturing (AM) Module: Physics, Validation, Absorptivity, Experimental Calibration, and Model Comparisons

### 1. Module Physics and Capabilities

**Important caveat.** The Thermo-Calc AM Module is a commercial product, and its internal algorithms are described primarily in vendor documentation rather than in peer-reviewed papers. The peer-reviewed literature retrieved here documents Thermo-Calc's CALPHAD property calculations being coupled with external melt-pool models (chiefly the Eagar–Tsai analytical solution and the Gladush–Smurov keyhole-depth correction), rather than independent validation of the commercial AM Module's own thermal solvers. This distinction must be kept in mind throughout.

**CALPHAD property calculations.** Published CALPHAD-coupled AM frameworks use Thermo-Calc's Property, Equilibrium, and Scheil calculation models to obtain temperature-dependent thermophysical properties including liquidus and solidus temperatures, latent heat of fusion, density, thermal conductivity, specific heat, thermal diffusivity, enthalpy, dynamic and kinematic viscosity, and surface tension (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 3-5, sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 7-10). The thermodynamic databases documented in published workflows include TCHEA5 (for high-entropy alloys), TCAL7 (for aluminium alloys), TCNI11 (for nickel alloys), and TCFE (for steels) (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 3-5, sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 7-10). Rule-of-mixtures estimates complement CALPHAD-derived values for quantities such as boiling temperature and molecular weight (sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 7-10).

**Heat-source models.** According to vendor documentation (as described in the user's question), the AM Module offers steady-state, transient, and transient-with-steady-state-heat-source calculation modes, with Gaussian, double-ellipsoid (Goldak), conical, and keyhole heat-source options. In the peer-reviewed literature, however, the most commonly documented thermal model coupled with Thermo-Calc properties is the Eagar–Tsai (E-T) analytical model, which uses a moving Gaussian surface source on a quasi-steady semi-infinite substrate and predicts melt-pool length, width, and depth from the liquidus-temperature isotherm (sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 7-10, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 14-15). Because E-T systematically underpredicts depth once keyholing begins, the Gladush–Smurov model is applied as a corrective keyhole-depth estimate (sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 14-15, sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 3-5). Both omit fluid-flow effects including Marangoni convection, evaporation, and recoil pressure (sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 14-15). No peer-reviewed paper was found that independently validates the AM Module's own keyhole model, its treatment of Marangoni flow, evaporation, or recoil, or its powder-layer effective-property scheme.

**Outputs.** The documented framework outputs include melt-pool width, depth, and length; and—by batch calculation across power–speed space—printability maps delineating lack-of-fusion, keyholing, and balling regions. Published printability-map frameworks use two lack-of-fusion criteria, two balling criteria, and three keyholing criteria, combined across three thermal models (E-T, scaled E-T, and a neural-network surrogate) to give twelve model–criterion combinations (sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 5-7). Thermal gradient G, solidification velocity R, and cooling rate are described as standard AM Module outputs in vendor documentation but are not independently documented with quantitative validation numbers in the retrieved peer-reviewed papers.

### 2. Published Validations and Applications

**Alloys and machines.** Peer-reviewed CALPHAD-coupled printability-assessment frameworks that explicitly use Thermo-Calc for property calculations have been demonstrated on Co-Cr-Fe-Mn-Ni (Cantor) high-entropy alloys, Ni-5 wt.% Nb, AF9628 low-alloy steel, Ni-Ti shape memory alloys, and stainless steel 316L (sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 3-5). These frameworks were validated against experimental single-track and multi-track data on commercial LPBF systems including EOS M280 machines (gan2021benchmarkstudyof pages 1-2, li2023statisticalparameterizedphysicsbased pages 7-10).

**NIST AM Bench benchmark data.** The most rigorous publicly available single-track validation datasets come from the NIST AM Bench programme. The 2018 AM Bench (AMB2018-02) produced single-track melt-pool width, depth, length, and cooling-rate measurements on IN625 bare plates using both the AMMT research testbed (170 µm D4σ spot) and an EOS M270 commercial machine (100 µm D4σ spot), at three parameter cases: 150 W / 400 mm/s, 195 W / 800 mm/s, and 195 W / 1200 mm/s (lane2020measurementsofmelt pages 1-3, lane2020measurementsofmelt pages 6-8). The 2022 AM Bench Challenge 3 extended this to IN718 single tracks and multi-track builds, with an in-house AM-CFD code achieving first-place predictions in multiple challenge categories (li2023statisticalparameterizedphysicsbased pages 7-10, amin2023physicsguidedheat pages 10-14). The 2025 AM Bench (Challenge 06) further expanded to IN718 powder-bed single tracks with 285 W, 0.96 m/s, 72 µm spot, at 80 µm and 160 µm powder-layer heights, with high-fidelity CFD predictions within approximately 5% of experimental width and depth (kumar2026laserpowderbed pages 10-14, kumar2026laserpowderbed pages 14-17). These datasets are openly available and should be the first comparison target for the AM Module.

**Quantitative agreement.** For analytical Eagar–Tsai models with CALPHAD properties, melt-pool width predictions in the conduction regime achieve mean absolute errors below 10% for NiTi (rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsand pages 84-89, rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsanda pages 84-89). The Gladush–Smurov keyhole-depth correction reduces depth errors to below 20% (rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsand pages 84-89). For bare-plate single tracks of IN625, IN718, and 316L, a conduction model with independently fixed absorptivity (not fitted to the same depths) reproduced conduction-regime depth and half-width to within a few percent, but increasingly underpredicted depth once keyholing began (hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 1-2, hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 2-4). High-fidelity CFD models calibrated against NIST data reported approximately 2% depth errors for lower-energy IN718 conditions and 2–8% for higher-energy conditions (kumar2026laserpowderbed pages 14-17). No comparable module-specific numbers were found for the Thermo-Calc AM Module itself—this is the key validation gap.

**316L-specific validations.** For 316L, King et al. (2014, DOI: 10.1016/j.jmatprotec.2014.06.005) provided 28 single tracks on a 50 µm powder bed in argon with two beam sizes, designed as 14 paired conditions spanning the conduction-to-keyhole threshold using the normalized enthalpy ΔH/h_s (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 6-11, king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 1-6). Zhang et al. (2024, DOI: 10.3390/mi15020170) produced single tracks on both bare plate and powder for 316L at speeds from 0.52 to 2.20 m/s and validated a CFD model with cross-sectional width/depth and surface morphology (zhang2024understandingmeltpool pages 7-9). Smith et al. (2024, DOI: 10.1007/s11837-024-06946-z) evaluated nine parameter sets at a constant volumetric energy density of 85 J/mm³ with hatch spacings of 60–180 µm, 30 µm layers, and 316L on a Concept Laser Mlab system, demonstrating that VED alone is an inadequate defect predictor and proposing modified lack-of-fusion and spatter-induced porosity criteria (smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 3-5, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 7-9).

Key findings:

- **Conduction mode:** The flat 316L melt-surface absorptivity at 1070 nm is approximately 0.28–0.30. Unmelted powder initially has absorptivity about twice the flat-surface value (~0.6), but it drops sharply once the powder melts and the surface smooths (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 7-8, patel2021meltingmodesin pages 59-63, trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 4-6).

- **Keyhole mode:** As recoil pressure opens a vapor depression, absorptivity rises through multiple internal reflections and saturates near 0.70–0.78 (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 7-8, trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 4-6). The transition power depends on scan speed and spot size; at 1500 mm/s and 60 µm spot, the onset occurs at higher power than at 500 mm/s (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 4-6).

- **Absorptivity scaling:** Ye et al. (2019, DOI: 10.1002/adem.201900185) proposed the asymptotic exponential A_e = 0.70[1 − exp(−0.66·β·A_m·L*_th)], where β is the normalized enthalpy and L*_th the normalized thermal diffusion length, to collapse multi-alloy melt-pool depth data onto a single curve (ye2019energycouplingmechanisms pages 7-9). This provides a physics-based, speed- and power-dependent absorptivity rather than a single constant.

- **Diagnostic caution:** Hong et al. (2026, DOI: 10.3390/ma19153290) showed that when absorptivity is fixed to an independent value (0.27–0.34 for 316L), a conduction model reproduces conduction-regime depth to within a few percent; however, every keyhole-classified track requires an apparent absorptivity above unity, demonstrating that absorptivity fitting cannot replace missing keyhole physics (hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 1-2, hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 2-4).

- **Bayesian inference for 316L:** Whalen et al. (2021, DOI: 10.1007/s40192-021-00238-z) used Bayesian inversion of a temperature-dependent, powder-corrected E-T model for 316L and found that inferred apparent absorptivity depends strongly on laser power and only marginally on scan speed. At high power, inferred values approach unity, partly compensating for the missing vapor-depression physics (whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 1-4, whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 4-7, whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 7-9).

### 4. What Experimental Data Would Best Help Calibrate and Refine the Module

**Single tracks on bare plate.** The NIST AM Bench strategy provides the strongest template: single-track experiments on bare polished substrates with characterized surface roughness, measured (not nominal) laser power, calibrated spot size (D4σ and FWHM from beam profiling), and cross-sectional melt-pool width and depth from etched metallographic sections (lane2020measurementsofmelt pages 1-3, lane2020measurementsofmelt pages 6-8). Bare-plate tracks isolate the laser–material interaction from powder-layer variability and are essential for calibrating absorptivity in both conduction and keyhole regimes (sarker2026llmagentguidedstochastic pages 5-8, zhang2024understandingmeltpool pages 7-9).

**Single tracks on powder.** Powder-bed tracks are essential complements because powder changes the absorptivity (initially higher, then dropping as the layer melts) and introduces effective thermal properties that differ from bulk (zhang2024understandingmeltpool pages 7-9, trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 4-6). Zhang et al. (2024) showed that powder tracks are consistently taller than bare-plate tracks at the same conditions (zhang2024understandingmeltpool pages 7-9). Tracks at multiple layer thicknesses (e.g., 80 µm and 160 µm as in the 2025 NIST AM Bench) further constrain the model's powder-layer treatment (kumar2026laserpowderbed pages 17-21, kumar2026laserpowderbed pages 14-17).

**Constant P/v at different speeds.** Smith et al. (2024) demonstrated that nine parameter sets with identical VED (85 J/mm³) but varying power (46–468 W), speed (0.3–2.0 m/s), and hatch spacing (60–180 µm) produced systematically different defect types and melt-pool geometries in 316L (smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 3-5, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 7-9). This directly tests whether the model correctly separates the independent effects of power and speed, which a VED-based approach cannot.

**King et al. paired-enthalpy design.** King et al. (2014) designed 14 pairs of tracks with approximately equal normalized enthalpy at two different beam sizes, spanning conditions well below and well above the keyhole threshold (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 6-11, king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 1-6). This paired design efficiently probes the keyhole transition and spot-size sensitivity with a moderate number of experiments.

**Multi-track pads and thin walls.** For multi-track calibration, pads of different sizes (1×5 mm and 5×5 mm) reveal track-to-track heat accumulation and remelting effects (kumar2026laserpowderbed pages 17-21, kumar2026laserpowderbed pages 14-17). The user's thin-wall (fin/comb) geometry is valuable because it provides large cross-sections through the full wall height, revealing the last-layer melt-pool shape and steady-state hatch overlap at each condition, while keeping the number of conditions high within a single build.

**Number of conditions.** The Sobol-sequence design with 60 conditions plus controls is well-suited for model calibration and testing. Benchmark studies typically use 10–36 single-track conditions (rangaswamy2026physicsguideddecisionsupportframework pages 7-8, lane2020measurementsofmelt pages 1-3); 60+ conditions with scrambled space-filling coverage over the P–v plane provides strong statistical power for Bayesian calibration and can expose systematic model biases.

**Orientation pairs.** The 21 pairs printed in both recoater orientations test whether wall orientation affects density or melt-pool shape, which is a machine-specific effect not captured by the AM Module.

**Essential metadata.** Based on the benchmark literature, the following metadata should be recorded and reported:

1. **Measured laser power** at the work plane, not just set-point (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 6-11, king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 1-6)
2. **Beam profile and spot size definition** (D4σ and FWHM, ideally at focus and at the working plane; for defocused conditions, the full beam caustic) (lane2020measurementsofmelt pages 1-3)
3. **Powder size distribution** (D10, D50, D90), morphology, and apparent/tap density
4. **Powder chemistry** (ICP or equivalent)
5. **Layer thickness** (set-point and, ideally, measured effective layer)
6. **Atmosphere** (gas type, O₂ level, flow rate)
7. **Build-plate temperature** (measured, not just set-point)
8. **Scan strategy details** (hatch angle, overlap, contour parameters)

The final row explicitly separates documented Thermo-Calc property workflows from evidence validating the commercial AM Module itself.*

**Key points of comparison:**

- **Eagar–Tsai** is the most widely used fast model and predicts conduction-regime melt-pool width with mean absolute error below 10%, but it systematically underpredicts depth once keyholing begins because it has no cavity physics (rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsand pages 84-89, sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 3-5, hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 1-2). The E-T model ignores Marangoni convection, evaporation, recoil pressure, latent heat, and temperature-dependent properties in its standard form (whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 1-4, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 14-15). A neural-network surrogate can accelerate it by approximately 1000× (550,000 cases in 120 s versus 45 days for the analytical E-T) (sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 10-13).

- **Rosenthal** is less reliable than Eagar–Tsai for both width and depth because of the point-source singularity and fixed-property assumption (rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsand pages 84-89, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 3-5). Its semicircular pool assumption gives a width-to-depth ratio of 2, which is not observed experimentally under many conditions (smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 7-9).

- **Gladush–Smurov** provides a reduced-order keyhole-depth correction that improves depth prediction to below 20% error, and is used to augment E-T in the CALPHAD-coupled printability framework (rangaswamy2026multiscalemultiphysicsand pages 84-89, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 14-15).

- **FEM** can achieve width and depth errors of approximately 2–5% when well-calibrated (javanbakht2021meltpoolsize pages 40-46, kumar2026laserpowderbed pages 10-14), but requires careful mesh refinement and heat-source calibration. Its main limitations are computational cost (minutes to hours per track) and the need to calibrate volumetric heat-source dimensions.

- **CFD/multiphysics** models are the most capable, explicitly resolving Marangoni flow, recoil pressure, evaporation, and keyhole dynamics, but at a cost of approximately 20 hours on 64 CPU cores per track (hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 2-4). NIST AM Bench validations with CFD codes have achieved depth and width predictions within approximately 2–5% (kumar2026laserpowderbed pages 14-17).

- **Thermo-Calc AM Module** occupies an intermediate position: its CALPHAD property coupling is a clear advantage over models that assume constant properties, and its batch printability-map capability is designed for rapid process-window exploration. However, no peer-reviewed paper was found that independently validates the module's own thermal solver predictions against experiments. The published peer-reviewed record documents Thermo-Calc for CALPHAD properties plus external E-T/Gladush–Smurov models for melt-pool predictions (sheikh2023anautomatedfullycomputational pages 3-5, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 14-15, sheikh2025highthroughputalloyand pages 7-10). The user's planned experimental programme is therefore well-positioned to provide the first independent, controlled validation of the AM Module against a large, well-characterized 316L dataset.
