## Porosity, Melt-Pool Depth, and Process Maps for LPBF 316L Stainless Steel: A Literature Benchmark for the Concept Laser M2 Parameter Window

### 1. Published 316L Process Maps and Density Data in the Proposed Window

The most directly comparable published study is the LLNL work by Kamath et al. (2014), who used a **Concept Laser M2** with a D4σ laser spot of **54 µm** (equivalent to Φ(99%) ≈ 81 µm) and 316L powder (CL20ES) at powers of **150–400 W**, 30 µm layers, and scan speeds spanning approximately 1200–2400 mm/s for density pillars (kamath2014densityofadditivelymanufactured pages 1-5, kamath2014densityofadditivelymanufactured pages 5-7). At 250 W and above, suitable scan speeds produced **>99% relative density**; at 400 W over 1900–2200 mm/s, density reached approximately **99.5%** (kamath2014densityofadditivelymanufactured pages 13-16). Density fell sharply at excessive speed (insufficient melting/LOF) and more gradually at low speed as keyhole voids formed (kamath2014densityofadditivelymanufactured pages 1-5). Single-track screening on the same M2 targeted melt-pool depths of **60–120 µm** (approximately 2–3× the 30 µm layer thickness) for high-density builds; the largest measured single-track depth was **195 µm** at 200 W and 500 mm/s (kamath2014densityofadditivelymanufactured pages 9-13). Note that the Kamath beam (54 µm D4σ) is considerably smaller than the user's production spot (130 µm), so the energy density at the same P and v is distributed over a larger area in the user's case, reducing penetration depth and shifting the keyhole and LOF boundaries.

Bateman et al. (2025) provide the **closest match to the user's exact machine and production parameters**: a GE Concept M2 with gas-atomized 316L (19–47 µm). Their standard/production condition used **370 W, 1350 mm/s, 90 µm hatch, 50 µm layer, and a 130 µm spot** (VED = 60.9 J/mm³), yielding porosity of **0.003–0.98%** across baseline and enhanced-spatter specimens (bateman2025effectsoflpbf pages 6-13, bateman2025effectsoflpbf pages 1-6). Their low-energy condition (290 W, 1200 mm/s, 150 µm hatch, 50 µm layer, 50 µm spot; VED = 32.2 J/mm³) produced **5.85–8.60% porosity**, dominated by lack-of-fusion defects (bateman2025effectsoflpbf pages 6-13). Their high-energy condition (290 W, 800 mm/s, 70 µm hatch, 50 µm layer, 125 µm spot; VED = 103.6 J/mm³) yielded **0.55–1.82% porosity**, with both keyhole and LOF features reported (bateman2025effectsoflpbf pages 13-18). This demonstrates that even at higher VED, density did not necessarily improve, consistent with the onset of keyhole porosity.

Vallejo et al. (2021) used an **SLM 125HL** (≈70 µm spot, 100 °C preheat, argon, 30 µm layers, 120 µm hatch). Their optimized condition was **200 W, 800 mm/s** (VED = 69 J/mm³), achieving **99.89 ± 0.06% relative density**. The dense window spanned **46–127 J/mm³**, with ≥99.8% density throughout (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 2-4, vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 1-2). At 200 W, densities across scan speeds of 600–1400 mm/s were 99.92, 99.89, 99.88, 99.83, and 99.44%, respectively, illustrating the LOF edge near 40 J/mm³ (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 2-4). Melt-pool widths ranged from ~50–300 µm and depths from ~30–600 µm across all conditions (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 13-14).

Barrionuevo et al. (2024) used a **Concept Laser MLAB 200R** (≈80 µm spot, nitrogen, 30 µm layers) with 120–160 W and 700–1100 mm/s. The best densification was **98.3%** (Archimedes) or **99.70%** (image analysis) at 160 W, 700 mm/s, 40 µm hatch (barrionuevo2024influenceofthe pages 4-6). At a supplementary 180 W point, density reached **99.41 ± 0.23%**; above 180 W, density fell below 98% due to overheating (barrionuevo2024influenceofthe pages 11-14).

Yusuf et al. (2017) reported **Concept Laser M2** results at 200 W, 1600 mm/s, 50 µm layers: Archimedes density ≥99%, mean porosity ≈0.82%, XCT maximum porosity ≈0.61%, with spatially non-uniform pore distribution (yusuf2017investigationonporosity pages 1-3).

### 2. Lack-of-Fusion Boundary

**Tang et al.'s geometric overlap criterion** is the most widely used LOF predictor. It models the melt pool as a half-ellipse and requires that adjacent tracks and layers overlap sufficiently. The criterion is expressed as **(H/W)² + (t/D)² ≤ 1**, where H is hatch spacing, W is melt-pool width, t is layer thickness, and D is melt-pool depth (harkin2023evaluationofthe pages 2-3, gordon2020defectstructureprocess pages 3-4). When this sum exceeds unity, lack-of-fusion porosity is predicted. An alternative linear form, **t/D + H/W ≤ 1**, has also been used (subraveti2024threedimensionalpredictionof pages 1-4). Gordon et al. (2020) applied this criterion in Ti-6Al-4V defect structure process maps and noted that VED alone is not a robust LOF predictor because different H and t combinations at the same VED produce different overlap (gordon2020defectstructureprocess pages 3-4, gordon2020defectstructureprocess pages 1-2).

**In VED terms**, the LOF boundary for 316L at 30 µm layers and typical hatch spacings falls at approximately **40–50 J/mm³** (strongly supported). Vallejo et al. found ≥99.8% density for 46–127 J/mm³ (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 16-18), and density dropped to 99.44% at 39.7 J/mm³ and further at lower VED. Bateman et al.'s 32.2 J/mm³ condition (with a larger 150 µm hatch and 50 µm spot) produced 5.85–8.60% porosity (bateman2025effectsoflpbf pages 6-13), confirming that the LOF boundary is **sharp**: porosity can rise from <0.5% to >5% over a relatively narrow VED range once the melt pool no longer spans the hatch spacing or layer thickness.

**In P/v terms**, for the user's conditions (h = 0.09 mm, t = 0.03 mm), the LOF boundary corresponds to approximately P/v ≈ 0.12–0.14 J/mm (VED ≈ 44–52 J/mm³), though this depends on the actual melt-pool geometry at the specific spot size.

Smith et al. (2024) demonstrated that at a constant VED of 85 J/mm³ with a 100 µm spot on a DMP Flex 350, different P/v combinations produced different defect types and amounts, with low-P/low-v builds showing interpass LOF and high-P/high-v builds showing spatter-induced intrapass LOF (smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 5-7, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 10-11, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 1-2). This strongly supports the view that **VED is necessary but not sufficient** as a LOF predictor; the geometric criterion using actual melt-pool dimensions is more reliable.

Barrionuevo et al. recommended a VED above **125 J/mm³** for their Concept Laser MLAB (80 µm spot, 30 µm layers), which is significantly higher than Vallejo's 46 J/mm³ lower bound (barrionuevo2024influenceofthe pages 11-14). This discrepancy likely reflects the smaller spot size (deeper, narrower pools) and different hatch spacing. This highlights that the LOF boundary is **machine- and spot-dependent** (weakly to moderately supported as a general principle; the specific numbers are machine-specific).

### 3. Keyhole Porosity Threshold for 316L at 50–130 µm Spots

King et al. (2014, LLNL) established the keyhole transition for 316L at a **normalized enthalpy ΔH/h_s ≈ 30 ± 4** using the definition ΔH/h_s = AP/(h_s √(π D_th v σ³)), where A is absorptivity (~0.4), h_s is the enthalpy at melting, D_th is thermal diffusivity, v is scan speed, and σ is the beam-radius parameter (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 19-28, king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 15-19). This threshold was validated with **both 52 µm and 130 µm D4σ beams**, confirming that it scales across beam sizes (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 19-28). The geometric keyhole criterion was **d > w/2** (depth exceeds half-width) (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 11-15, king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 28-36).

Bertoli (2018, LLNL) derived a theoretical threshold of **ΔH/h_s ≈ 6** based on the energy to reach the boiling point: ΔH/h_s ≥ πT_b/T_m (bertoli2018processstructurepropertiesrelationshipsina pages 50-56, bertoli2018processstructurepropertiesrelationshipsinb pages 50-56). The difference between the theoretical ≈6 and experimental ≈30 reflects that the definitions differ (King's includes additional scaling factors) and that heat losses reduce effective enthalpy delivery.

Sabzi and Rivera-Díaz-del-Castillo (2019) reported that for 316L, normalized enthalpy values **above ~7** (using their definition) corresponded to keyhole formation, while below 7 was pore-free. At 200 W with scan speeds 850–1600 mm/s, pore fractions were 0.4–2.5% under keyhole conditions (sabzi2019defectpreventionin pages 8-10).

Forien et al. (2020, LLNL) identified the conduction-to-keyhole transition at ΔH/h ≈ **7.3** via in-situ pyrometry (forien2020detectingkeyholeporosity pages 14-19).

**Can 370 W at 1350 mm/s with a 130 µm spot keyhole?** Using King's formulation with the user's 130 µm D4σ spot (σ ≈ 32.5 µm), the normalized enthalpy at 370 W and 1350 mm/s evaluates to a value that falls **near or below** the keyhole threshold of 30 ± 4 (king2014observationofkeyholemode pages 19-28). The larger beam distributes energy over a wider area, reducing peak intensity. For comparison, Gordon et al. noted a keyhole threshold surface power density of ~10⁶ W/cm² for Ti-6Al-4V; at 370 W over a 130 µm diameter circle, the irradiance is approximately **2.8 × 10⁶ W/cm²** (gordon2020defectstructureprocess pages 4-5), which could potentially induce keyhole behavior. However, the relatively high scan speed (1350 mm/s) limits the interaction time, reducing the likelihood of deep keyhole formation. Metelkova et al. (2018) showed that positive defocus (larger effective spot) generally retained conduction-mode processing over a wider parameter range (metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 10-13), and that melt-pool depth was far more sensitive to beam size than width (metelkova2018ontheinfluence pages 6-8).

**Assessment (moderately supported):** The production condition (370 W, 1350 mm/s, 130 µm) likely operates in the **conduction-to-transition regime**, not deep keyhole. Keyhole porosity is more likely at slower scan speeds within the user's range (e.g., P/v > 0.4 J/mm at 130 µm spot). At the 50–70 µm contour spot and 100–120 W, the lower power keeps the system well within conduction mode.

### 4. Porosity and Melt-Pool Scatter / Repeatability

**Measurement repeatability:** Obeidi et al. (2021) reported a pycnometer measurement standard deviation of only **0.0002** (after 10 repeat measurements), with error bars of approximately **0.002** in relative density (obeidi2021comparisonofthe pages 4-6). Thus, the measurement method itself introduces negligible uncertainty.

**Within-condition scatter:** Vallejo et al. reported standard deviations on relative density ranging from **±0.05% to ±1.35%** across different parameter sets. At the optimum (200 W, 800 mm/s), the SD was only ±0.06%, while at suboptimal conditions (e.g., 125 W, 200 mm/s), it reached ±1.35% (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 2-4). This suggests that **scatter increases dramatically at marginal parameters** where stochastic defects (spatter, powder distribution irregularities) dominate.

**Spatter-induced variability:** Bateman et al. showed that at the standard 370 W/1350 mm/s condition, baseline porosity was **0.003%** while enhanced-spatter porosity reached **0.98%**—a variation of two orders of magnitude from spatter alone (bateman2025effectsoflpbf pages 18-22). The relative increase from spatter was 115% for standard energy, 22% for low energy, and 48% for high energy.

**Machine-to-machine variability:** Obeidi et al. found that four machines with the same powder and nominal parameters produced densities spanning approximately **93.88–97.67%** (a ~4 percentage-point range), with Aconity and EOS M280 performing best and Concept Laser (nitrogen atmosphere) performing worst (obeidi2021comparisonofthe pages 4-6, obeidi2021comparisonofthe pages 6-8). Contributing factors included build-chamber size, gas flow, recoater design, and gas type (obeidi2021comparisonofthe pages 10-12).

**Implications for the user's comb specimens (weakly supported, extrapolated):** No study was found reporting direct wall-to-wall porosity scatter for thin-wall geometries. However, based on the above data, for well-optimized parameters (VED ~50–100 J/mm³), wall-to-wall porosity differences below **~0.1–0.3 percentage points** may be within normal stochastic scatter, while differences exceeding **~0.5 percentage points** likely reflect a real process-parameter effect. At marginal parameters near the LOF boundary, scatter increases substantially.

### 5. Open Datasets for Comparison

**LLNL Keyhole-Mode Microscopy Dataset** (Ahlquist et al. 2022, DOE OSTI ID 1878448, DOI: 10.2172/1878448): Contains single-track cross-sectional images, melt-pool width, depth, height, contact angles, continuity ratings, and cavity locations/contours from LPBF experiments. CSV/Excel files are indexed by plate and track. Directly relevant for keyhole-mode benchmarking (ahlquist2022keyholemodemicroscopydataset pages 15-21).

**MeltpoolNet** (Akbari et al.): A tabular dataset of metallic AM melt-pool characteristics referenced in multiple ML studies (jabbour2025amultisourcemelt pages 7-10). It aggregates process parameters and melt-pool dimensions across alloys including 316L, though the depth of 316L coverage varies.

**Melt-Pool-Kinetics** (Jabbour et al. 2025, Figshare, DOI: 10.1038/s41597-025-05597-2): A multi-source compilation in HDF5 format with metadata covering AM process, material, laser power, scan speed, beam size, layer thickness, and defect results. A metadata spreadsheet enables filtering for LPBF 316L entries (jabbour2025amultisourcemelt pages 7-10, jabbour2025amultisourcemelt pages 11-12).

**NIST AM-Bench** (2018, 2022, 2025 challenges): Primarily focused on **IN718** and **IN625** single-track and multi-layer builds with carefully controlled melt-pool measurements. The 316L coverage is limited; the benchmark's main value for the user is methodological rather than material-specific (jabbour2025amultisourcemelt pages 11-12).

**Journal supplementary data:** Vallejo et al. (2021) Table 1 contains a full parameter-density matrix for 316L on the SLM 125HL (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 2-4). Barrionuevo et al. (2024) Table 1 compiles a literature survey of 316L LPBF parameters and densities across >10 machines (barrionuevo2024influenceofthe pages 2-4). Gordon et al. (2020) provide Ti-6Al-4V defect process maps with XCT-derived porosity data that can serve as a cross-material reference for the Tang criterion (gordon2020defectstructureprocess pages 4-5, gordon2020defectstructureprocess pages 1-2).

### 6. Summary of Key Thresholds and Recommendations

**Lack-of-fusion boundary (316L, 30 µm layers, 0.09 mm hatch, 100–130 µm spot):**
- VED: approximately **40–50 J/mm³** (strongly supported by multiple studies)
- P/v: approximately **0.11–0.14 J/mm** (dependent on spot and hatch)
- Tang criterion: **(H/W)² + (t/D)² < 1**; this requires knowing W and D at each condition

**Keyhole boundary (316L, 130 µm spot):**
- King's normalized enthalpy: **ΔH/h_s ≈ 30 ± 4** (strongly supported, validated at both 52 and 130 µm)
- Geometric criterion: **d/w > 0.5** or depth exceeding half-width
- At 370 W / 1350 mm/s / 130 µm: likely **conduction-to-transition mode**, not deep keyhole (moderately supported)
- Keyhole risk increases at slower speeds (P/v > ~0.4–0.5 J/mm at 130 µm)

**Expected porosity across the user's window (P = 150–370 W, v ≈ 335–2930 mm/s, h = 0.09 mm, t = 30 µm, 130 µm spot):**
- VED range: ~33–204 J/mm³
- At 33–45 J/mm³ (high-v / low-P corner): **>1–8% porosity** from LOF (bateman2025effectsoflpbf pages 6-13)
- At 50–130 J/mm³ (central window): **<0.5% porosity**, potentially <0.1% at optimum (vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 2-4, bateman2025effectsoflpbf pages 18-22)
- At >130–200 J/mm³ (low-v / high-P corner): porosity may rise to **0.5–2%** from keyhole and overheating effects (bateman2025effectsoflpbf pages 13-18, vallejo2021processoptimizationand pages 16-18)

**Melt-pool depth estimates:** At the production condition (370 W, 1350 mm/s, 130 µm spot), Kamath et al.'s single-track screening targeted **60–120 µm** depth for high-density builds with a 54 µm beam (kamath2014densityofadditivelymanufactured pages 9-13). With the larger 130 µm spot, shallower pools are expected at the same P/v. At slow speeds (e.g., 370 W, 335 mm/s), depths could reach **200+ µm**, potentially entering keyhole territory.
