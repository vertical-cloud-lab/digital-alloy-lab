## 1. Mechanism: Drying Cracks in Sprayed Particulate Coatings

### 1.1 Capillary Pressure and Substrate Constraint

When a suspension of hard particles dries on a rigid substrate, evaporation drives the air–liquid interface toward the substrate until the particles reach close packing. At this point, liquid menisci form between surface particles. The resulting capillary pressure is described by the Young–Laplace equation:

**p_c = 2γ cos θ / r**

where γ is the liquid–vapor surface tension, θ is the contact angle, and r is the characteristic pore-neck radius (fischer2020influenceofdrying pages 1-3). This pressure compresses the particle network and causes film shrinkage. If the coating is free to contract in all directions, no stress develops. However, adhesion to a rigid substrate restricts lateral shrinkage, generating an in-plane tensile stress that can drive crack propagation when it exceeds the fracture resistance of the film (fischer2020usinganadded pages 1-2, fischer2020usinganadded pages 2-5).

### 1.2 Critical Cracking Thickness

Tirumkudulu and Russel (2005) derived the critical stress for crack initiation in a thin particulate film (rollag2019dryingtemperatureand pages 1-6):

**σ_c R/(2γ) = 0.1804 × (2γ/(GMφ_rcp R))^(1/3) × (h/R)^(-2/3)**

where R is particle radius, G is shear modulus, M is coordination number, φ_rcp is the random-close-packing volume fraction, and h is film thickness. Thinner films require higher stresses to crack.

Singh and Tirumkudulu (2007) extended this by equating the critical stress to the maximum capillary pressure, yielding the maximum crack-free thickness (rollag2019dryingtemperatureand pages 1-6):

**h_max = 0.64 × [(G M φ_rcp R³)/(2γ)]^(1/2) × [2γ/(P_max R)]^(3/2)**

where P_max R/(2γ) = (3 cos θ / 2) × [φ_rcp/(1 − φ_rcp)]. The critical scaling relationships are:

- **h_max ∝ R^(3/2)**: larger particles allow much thicker crack-free films
- **h_max ∝ γ^(−1/2)**: lower surface-tension solvents increase the crack-free limit
- **h_max ∝ G^(1/2)**: stiffer particles increase fracture resistance

## 2. Substrate and History: Why Coat 2 Cracked but Coat 1 Did Not

### 2.1 Changed Substrate Character

The most significant difference between Coat 1 (no cracking) and Coat 2 (cracking) is the substrate condition. On 27 October, the spray went onto clean, bare, porous graphite. By 30 October, the crucible had been through a 1300°C firing cycle and carried a heterogeneous surface: patches of fired BN coating, bare graphite where the skull pulled away the coating, and possible metal/oxide residues.

**Solvent absorption.** Fresh isostatic graphite is porous (typically 10–20% open porosity) and can wick low-viscosity solvents such as acetone and ethanol into its pore network. This wicking effectively thins the wet film, reduces the peak liquid content at the surface, and promotes more gradual consolidation from below—all of which reduce drying stress (fischer2020influenceofdrying pages 1-3, fischer2020usinganadded pages 2-5). A fired BN layer, by contrast, is a compacted, binder-free platelet bed with different porosity and surface energy. If it absorbs less solvent than bare graphite, the new wet coat remains thicker at the surface and consolidates as a more highly constrained film. Conversely, if the fired layer is more porous and wicks aggressively, it can dehydrate the new coat from below, creating a skin-formation scenario. Either mechanism increases the likelihood of cracking.

**Weak interface.** The fired BN coat (after binder burnout) is held together only by platelet surface energy and mechanical interlocking (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7). It constitutes a weak boundary layer under the new spray. If the new coat bonds better to the old fired BN than the fired BN bonds to the graphite, cracks may propagate along the old-coat/graphite interface or through the old coat itself.

**Locally doubled thickness.** Where new spray overlaps retained fired coating, the total BN thickness is the sum of both layers. The crescent-shaped bare patches create abrupt thickness transitions that can act as stress concentrators, analogous to the surface-step effects observed in nanocrystalline coatings where critical thickness was lower near substrate steps (mahe2008crackingoftitania pages 7-8).

### 2.2 B₂O₃ and Borate Residue

Hexagonal BN begins to oxidize at approximately 800°C in air, forming B₂O₃ (fleming2019oxidation‐leddecompositionof pages 1-5, rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7). In the present case, the argon atmosphere would limit oxidation, but even small oxygen leaks or pre-melt air exposure during heat-up can produce some B₂O₃. B₂O₃ is hygroscopic: it absorbs atmospheric moisture and can react with water vapor to form metaboric acid (HBO₂) and orthoboric acid (H₃BO₃) (fleming2019oxidation‐leddecompositionof pages 1-5, fleming2019oxidation‐leddecompositionof pages 5-9). If boric acid crystals are present on the fired coating surface, they create a swelling, soluble boundary layer that can weaken adhesion and alter the drying dynamics of the new coat. This mechanism is **plausible but unconfirmed** for the described situation, since the crucible was fired under argon.

### 2.3 Metal and Oxide Residues

The Cu-10Al-5Ni-3Fe skull that adhered to the crucible bottom could leave metal smears, alumina (Al₂O₃ from oxidation of the alloy during cooling), or other reaction products. These residues change the local surface energy, roughness, and chemistry, potentially causing non-uniform wetting and local film thickness variations.

### 2.4 Literature vs. Speculation Summary

- **Literature-supported:** substrate constraint drives cracking (fischer2020influenceofdrying pages 1-3, fischer2020usinganadded pages 1-2); thickness above h_max causes cracking (rollag2019dryingtemperatureand pages 1-6); substrate steps concentrate stress (mahe2008crackingoftitania pages 7-8); B₂O₃ is hygroscopic (fleming2019oxidation‐leddecompositionof pages 1-5); fired BN coatings lose binder and become friable (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7).
- **Speculation requiring verification:** the relative solvent absorption rates of bare graphite vs. fired BN; the presence and extent of B₂O₃ on this argon-fired crucible; the role of metal residues in local wetting changes.

## 3. Drying Conditions

### 3.1 Fume Hood Airflow

A laboratory fume hood typically operates at 0.3–0.5 m/s face velocity. This creates a unidirectional, forced airflow across the crucible mouth that accelerates evaporation of the volatile acetone (boiling point 56°C, vapor pressure ~24 kPa at 20°C) and ethanol (bp 78°C, ~5.8 kPa at 20°C). The effects include:

- **Faster evaporation:** reduces the time available for stress relaxation through particle rearrangement. While capillary pressure is not directly a function of evaporation rate, rapid drying increases the Péclet number, promoting skin formation at the top surface before the bulk consolidates (rollag2019dryingtemperatureand pages 1-6, fischer2020influenceofdrying pages 9-11).
- **Lateral drying fronts:** the airflow creates an asymmetric evaporation field, with the side of the crucible nearest the hood face drying faster. This produces edge-first consolidation and inward-propagating drying fronts that generate shrinkage gradients and stress (fischer2020influenceofdrying pages 9-11).
- **Non-uniform drying in a deep cup:** the interior of a crucible is partially sheltered from the airflow. The rim and upper wall dry rapidly while the bottom and lower wall remain wet, creating differential consolidation and stress concentrations.

### 3.2 Blushing

"Blushing" is a coating defect in which rapid evaporation of a low-boiling solvent cools the film surface below the dew point, causing atmospheric water vapor to condense into the film. For acetone evaporating at room temperature (~20°C), the surface temperature can drop by 10–15°C (estimate based on psychrometric cooling). If the ambient relative humidity is above ~40–50%, this could produce condensation. Water in an acetone/ethanol BN suspension would raise the effective surface tension of the residual liquid from ~22 to as high as 40–50 mN/m (partial mixing), increasing capillary pressure and promoting cracking. Additionally, condensed water could hydrate any B₂O₃ residue in the old coat. This mechanism is **speculative** for the described situation since humidity was not recorded.

### 3.3 Mitigation

Slower drying in still air, gentle warming (50–60°C), or placement in a dry environment with low airflow would reduce drying-front velocity, promote more uniform consolidation, and reduce the risk of blushing. Fischer and Koos (2020) showed that increasing relative humidity reduces peak drying stress in some particulate coatings, likely by slowing the constant-rate period and permitting more uniform consolidation (fischer2020influenceofdrying pages 15-17).

## 4. Spray Technique

### 4.1 Insufficient Shaking

hBN has a density of 2.27 g/cm³ and settles rapidly in low-viscosity solvents. The organoclay rheology additive (1–5% bentonite derivative) is specifically included to retard sedimentation, but its effectiveness depends on adequate dispersion. If the can is not shaken vigorously for at least 1–2 minutes, the first spray may be solvent-rich (thin and prone to running) while later spray is BN-rich (thick and prone to cracking).

### 4.2 Spray Distance and Atomization

Too close a distance produces a wet, coarse spray that deposits thick, liquid-rich films. Too far produces a dry, dusty overspray with poor adhesion. A nearly empty can has lower propellant pressure, producing larger droplets and wetter deposition. Standard aerosol spray distances for industrial coatings are typically 15–30 cm; the specific recommendation for this product should be obtained from Momentive.

### 4.3 Deep Crucible Geometry

Spraying into a cylindrical crucible creates overspray accumulation at the bottom and in the bottom-wall corner radius. These areas can receive 2–5× the nominal coating thickness, readily exceeding h_max locally even if the wall coating is within limits.

### 4.4 Multiple Passes Without Flash Time

Without adequate flash time (time for solvent evaporation between passes), successive spray passes merge into a single thick wet layer rather than building up as discrete thin layers. This is equivalent to applying one very thick coat, which has a much higher cracking risk.

### 4.5 Vendor Guidance on Thickness and Cracking

Rudolph (2013) described BN coating application for aluminum casthouse work, noting that "extremely thin layers for this coating are needed only for a successful application" when recoating distribution troughs (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 7-8). For recoating, the industry practice is to apply after each casting break, always in thin layers (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7).

Independent experimental work using ZYP Coatings BN products found that hand-applied BN coatings cracked at two coats (edge cracking) and three coats (full-surface cracking), while spray application with appropriate dilution produced no cracking (manousis2026castingselfreflectionthreedimensional pages 159-166). This directly supports the hypothesis that excessive thickness—whether from heavy hand application or multi-pass spraying—is the primary driver of cracking in BN release coatings.

General BN coating vendor guidance (compiled from Rudolph 2013 and general industry practice):
- Apply thin, uniform coats; multiple light passes are preferred over one heavy coat.
- Recoat after each use for consumable applications (troughs, ladles).
- For hard-coat (inorganic binder) products, higher use temperatures and better adhesion are available but may require curing at 800–900°C (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7).
- Clay-mineral binders keep the coating soft throughout the temperature range (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7).

## 5. Momentive Boron Nitride Spray II

### 5.1 Composition

Based on the SDS description provided by the user: hexagonal BN in ethanol and acetone, LPG (propane/butane) propellant, and 1–5% organoclay (bentonite-based rheology additive). The dry film therefore consists primarily of hBN platelets with a small amount of organoclay, forming a low-binder particulate coating. This composition is consistent with the general BN coating formulation strategy described by Rudolph (2013): carrier liquids may be water or organic solvents such as ethanol and 2-propanol; coatings can be produced without refractory binder if the BN has sufficient specific surface area (≥25 m²/g), but most applications require some binder for reasonable adherence (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7).

### 5.2 Binder and Adhesion

The organoclay serves primarily as a rheological additive (anti-settling, thixotropy for spray) rather than as a structural binder. After drying, the coating is essentially a weakly bonded platelet bed held together by surface energy and mechanical interlocking. After firing at 1300°C under argon, the organoclay decomposes (burnout), leaving a binder-free BN layer with no structural cohesion beyond platelet-platelet van der Waals forces.

### 5.3 Temperature Limits

Under inert gas, hBN is stable to approximately 1650°C (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 1-4, rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7). Under argon at 1300°C, the BN itself is thermally stable. However, at 1300°C with any oxygen present, oxidation to B₂O₃ begins to become significant. For the described argon atomization process, the primary risk is trace oxygen causing localized B₂O₃ formation.

### 5.4 Alternative Products

For repeated recoating of a graphite crucible at 1300°C under argon with an aluminum-containing copper melt, alternative approaches include:

- **Momentive water-based BN paints** (e.g., Momentive AC-6111 or equivalent): these provide more controlled thickness through brush or spray-gun application, and water-based formulations allow for thicker crack-free films due to slower drying kinetics. However, graphite must be dried thoroughly before use.
- **BN coatings with inorganic binders** (phosphate or alumina-based): these provide stronger adhesion and harder coats but require a curing cycle at 800–900°C. Phosphate binders are suitable for ceramic substrates with low thermal expansion; for graphite (higher CTE), alumina or alumina-silica binders may be more appropriate (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7).
- **ZYP Coatings BN Lubricoat aerosol** and **Saint-Gobain Combat BN sprays**: similar products with different formulation details. ZYP products have been documented in kiln-casting applications with specific dilution and application guidance (manousis2026castingselfreflectionthreedimensional pages 159-166).

## 6. Consequences and Diagnosis

### 6.1 Distinguishing Coating Cracks from Graphite Cracks

- **Mud cracks (drying cracks):** polygonal network pattern, typically equiaxed cells 1–10 mm across, confined to the coating thickness, edges may curl upward. Under magnification, bare graphite is visible at the crack floor.
- **Graphite thermal-shock cracks:** linear or branching, penetrate into the graphite substrate, may propagate across the entire crucible wall. These would be visible as cracks in bare graphite areas as well.
- **Telegraphed cracks:** pre-existing cracks in the old fired coat or graphite that propagate through the new coat as linear features, not polygonal networks. These can be identified by their correspondence with old-coat features.

A simple diagnostic: gently scrape the cracked region with a wooden tool. If only BN flakes come away and the graphite beneath is smooth and intact, the cracking is confined to the coating. If the crack continues into the graphite, the crucible itself may be damaged.

### 6.2 Acceptability of a Cracked BN Coat at 1300°C

A finely mud-cracked but adherent BN coat is **not acceptable** for an aluminum bronze melt at 1300°C, for the following reasons:

- **Al₄C₃ formation:** aluminum carbide formation is thermodynamically favorable whenever molten aluminum contacts graphite, with ΔG = −196 kJ/mol at 298 K. The reaction is favored below ~2100°C (zare2026onadvancedcasting pages 24-27, zare2026onadvancedcasting pages 20-24). At 1300°C with 10 wt% Al in the melt, the driving force is large. Cracks in the BN coat provide direct pathways for the melt to contact graphite, potentially forming Al₄C₃ at the interface. This brittle intermetallic can cause carbon pickup in the alloy and strengthen skull adhesion.

- **BN non-wetting:** intact hBN shows contact angles of ~160° against molten aluminum up to 900°C, declining above 900°C but remaining above 90° (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7). However, this non-wetting applies only to a continuous BN surface. Cracks expose the underlying graphite, which can be wetted by the Al-containing copper melt.

- **Flake contamination:** loose BN flakes from a cracked, curling coat can detach into the melt, travel through the nozzle, and contaminate the atomized powder. BN inclusions in the powder would be carried into final parts.

- **Skull adhesion:** if the melt penetrates cracks and reacts with graphite, the resulting Al₄C₃/metal interface bonds the skull more strongly to the crucible, making extraction difficult and damaging the coat further.

**Recommendation:** the cracked coat must be stripped and replaced before the next melt.

### 7.1 Coupon Test Protocol

To identify which factor caused the cracking, a structured coupon test on graphite offcuts is recommended:

**Materials:** 4–6 graphite offcuts of the same grade as the crucible, each approximately 25 × 25 × 5 mm, weighed to 0.1 mg on an analytical balance.

**Variables to test (one at a time):**

| Test | Substrate | Drying Environment | Number of Passes |
|------|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| A | Clean bare graphite | Still air (bench, no hood) | 1 light pass |
| B | Clean bare graphite | Fume hood (0.3–0.5 m/s) | 1 light pass |
| C | Clean bare graphite | Still air | 3 passes with 3-min flash |
| D | Clean bare graphite | Still air | 6 passes with 3-min flash |
| E | Fired BN coat (from a previous cycle) | Still air | 1 light pass |
| F | Fired BN coat | Still air | 3 passes with 3-min flash |

**Procedure:** Shake the can for 2 minutes. Spray each coupon at 20–25 cm distance with consistent sweeping speed. Reweigh after drying to determine dry deposit mass and calculate thickness (using hBN theoretical density 2.27 g/cm³ and estimated packing fraction 0.45). Photograph each coupon at 1 hour and 24 hours. Score for cracking (none / hairline / mud-crack network / lifting flakes).

**Expected outcomes:** If cracking appears only on multi-pass coupons (D, F), thickness is the primary issue. If cracking appears only on fired-coat coupons (E, F), substrate condition is dominant. If cracking appears on hood-dried coupons (B) but not still-air (A), airflow is significant. These results will directly identify the corrective action.

## Conclusions

The cracking of Coat 2 most likely resulted from a combination of excessive local coating thickness (particularly at the crucible bottom and old-coat edges), a changed substrate that modified solvent absorption and film consolidation, and rapid non-uniform drying in the fume hood. The cracked coat should not be used for the aluminum bronze melt because exposed graphite can react with aluminum in the melt to form Al₄C₃, and loose BN flakes can contaminate the atomized powder. The recommended corrective action is to strip the crucible, recoat with minimal passes targeting <50 µm dry thickness, dry under controlled conditions, and validate the procedure through the proposed coupon test matrix.
