Question: What is standard practice for cleaning woven-wire metal test sieves between different metal-alloy powders so that one alloy does not cross-contaminate the next? Our specific case: we just sieved a bronze (Cu-Sn) powder through a 63 um (about 230 mesh) and a 250 um (about 60 mesh) test sieve, and next we want to sieve an aluminum-alloy powder (e.g., AlSi10Mg or a new Al alloy) through the same two sieves as feedstock for laser powder bed fusion (LPBF). Please cover, with citations, DOIs or standard numbers:

(1) Cleaning methods for test sieves (ASTM E11 / ISO 3310-1) per standards and sieve-maker guidance (e.g., ASTM B214, ASTM E2427, ISO 2591-1, MPIF Standard 05, and sieve-maker handbooks), and how they differ between a fine 63 um mesh and a coarser 250 um mesh: dry methods (tapping, soft brushing from the underside, vacuum, low-pressure air) versus wet methods (ultrasonic bath: frequency, detergent or solvent, time, temperature), rinsing and drying (temperature limits for epoxy- or solder-bonded mesh), and what damages fine meshes (stiff or wire brushes, pushing trapped particles through with needles, high-pressure air, overloading).

(2) Whether cleaning alone is considered enough for additive-manufacturing-grade powder, or whether dedicated sieves for each alloy family are standard practice (e.g., ISO/ASTM 52907, ASTM F3049, ISO/ASTM 52904, NASA MSFC-SPEC-3717, or industry powder-handling guidance). How do labs verify that a sieve is clean before switching alloys (backlit or stereo-microscope inspection of the mesh, weighing, wipe or tape tests, running a sacrificial flush portion of the new powder, SEM-EDS or ICP-OES of the first aluminum powder for Cu and Sn)?

(3) How much Cu-Sn particle contamination matters in aluminum LPBF powder: documented cross-contamination cases in powder bed fusion (foreign-alloy particles in Ti-6Al-4V, aluminum, nickel or steel powders), the defects they cause (inclusions, unmelted particles, Cu-rich regions, cracking, corrosion), and any quantified tolerance limits or detection thresholds.

(4) Safety: any hazard from residual bronze (possibly surface-oxidized, CuO/Cu2O) mixing with fine aluminum powder (thermite, combustible dust, NFPA 484), and how to safely clean aluminum-powder residue off the sieves afterwards (wet or ultrasonic cleaning producing hydrogen, wet aluminum fines, solvent choice, waste disposal).

(5) A step-by-step cleaning and verification procedure suitable for a small university lab, distinguishing recommendations that are well supported by the literature or standards from those that rest on vendor guidance or common practice.

Context: university materials lab making small batches of alloy powder (ultrasonic atomization) for LPBF, sieving by hand or on a sieve shaker with standard round test sieves.

# Cleaning test sieves when changing from bronze to aluminum LPBF powder

**Recommendation.** For LPBF feedstock, obtain a **separate, labeled 63 µm and 250 µm sieve set for aluminum alloys**, together with dedicated collection pans and powder-contact tools. Cleaning the bronze sieves is appropriate for maintaining the sieves, but **visual cleanliness alone does not qualify their next aluminum batch as free of Cu–Sn particles**. If immediate reuse is unavoidable, treat the first aluminum batch as *quarantined* until a documented cleaning, inspection, and powder-comparison check has passed. This is a conservative laboratory recommendation, **not a claim that ASTM E11 or ISO/ASTM 52907 mandates dedicated sieves**. Particles smaller than either sieve opening can pass with the product, and published AlSi10Mg parts have contained foreign-alloy inclusions after powder changes. (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18, gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42, atkins2026mirrorfabricationusing pages 13-15)

## 1. What the standards and sieve manufacturers establish

**Separate sieve construction, analytical method, and feedstock-quality requirements.** ASTM **E11** and ISO **3310-1** specify woven-wire test sieves and their apertures; ASTM **B214**, ISO **4497**, and **MPIF Standard 05** concern sieve analysis of metal powders. ISO/ASTM **52907** addresses characterization of AM metal powders, including particle-size and chemical-composition methods. None of the available passages from those sources establishes a universal bronze-to-aluminum sieve-decontamination recipe, an ultrasonic frequency, or a permissible number of retained foreign particles. ASTM **E2427**, ISO **2591-1**, ASTM **F3049**, ISO/ASTM **52904**, and NASA **MSFC-SPEC-3717** should not be cited as prescribing a specific cleaning time or proving a shared sieve clean without checking the applicable full editions; the retrieved evidence does not establish such a provision. (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4, gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42)

Manufacturer instructions provide the more useful operational distinction:

| Sieve | Dry removal after bronze | If particles remain | Damage to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **250 µm, No. 60** | Invert over a bronze-waste collector; gently tap the **frame** and, if needed, gently brush the **underside only** in a circular motion using a clean, soft nylon brush. | Use a compatible, manufacturer-approved ultrasonic cleaning cycle; rinse and completely dry before reuse. | Hard or wire brushes, pressing powder through openings, needles, forceful air jets, and overloading. |
| **63 µm, No. 230** | Invert and gently tap the frame; collect loose particles without rubbing the cloth. A suitable powder-rated vacuum may remove accessible loose residue **without touching or drawing on the mesh**. | Prefer ultrasonic cleaning **if the sieve construction and laboratory safety assessment permit it**; otherwise retire or reserve it for bronze rather than forcing out lodged particles. | **Avoid brushing this fine mesh**, including vigorous underside brushing; never use a pick, needle, or concentrated air jet. |

Advantech recommends gentle underside-only nylon brushing between tests for **No. 100 and coarser** sieves, but avoiding brushing finer than No. 100; it warns that compressed-air jets locally distort fine openings and that lodged particles should not be picked out. Another section describes particular care beyond No. 80. Thus the 250 µm sieve is brushable with care, whereas the 63 µm sieve is not a good candidate for brushing. Neikov and Yefimov also note that overloading wedges particles into openings and recommend microscopic examination for distorted or torn cloth. A metal-powder vacuum is a **proposed contained-removal option**, not permission from these manufacturers to use an ordinary shop vacuum. (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 13-14, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18, neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4)

**Wet cleaning parameters are maker-dependent, not ASTM E11 settings.** Retsch guidance says to ultrasonically clean meshes finer than **500 µm** in water with *mild detergent* for approximately **3–5 minutes**, rinse thoroughly, and dry upright; both sieves in this question meet that size criterion. An Advantech *L3P sonic-sifter accessory* manual instead describes a cleaner rated **150 W or less**, mild dishwashing-detergent solution at **24–27 °C**, tap-water rinse, and air drying, and cautions against prolonged sonication. Its component-specific restrictions must not be assumed to apply to every round sieve. Neither cited instruction supplies a bath frequency: **40 kHz is not a verified requirement here**. If the sieve manufacturer approves your specific round sieves for aqueous ultrasonics, a short, monitored **3–5-minute room-temperature** cycle is a defensible *Retsch-derived starting point*, not a cross-alloy cleanliness validation. Thoroughly remove detergent, rinse with suitable clean water where permitted, and dry fully; avoid leaving water or cleaning residues that could contaminate aluminum feedstock. (bath2003finetuningsieve pages 3-4, separatorUnknownyearmodell3p pages 10-15, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18)

**Check the actual mesh-to-frame bond before choosing heat or liquid.** An Advantech account of *soldered* sieves says to keep the sieve below **149 °C** in its described wet-sieving procedure; that number is **not an approved drying temperature for an epoxy-bonded sieve**. The L3P manual prohibits heating its own stack components. For an unidentified round sieve, use ambient, upright drying or a maker-approved low-temperature method; get the sieve maker’s limits for its epoxy, solder, sealant, and frame before oven drying or solvent immersion. Replace torn, slack, or distorted cloth rather than repairing a precision 63 µm sieve with solder or epoxy: localized solder heat distorts openings, and epoxy blocks openings or can fracture. Avoid solvent ultrasonics unless the bath and solvent procedure are specifically approved for flammable-liquid service. (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 13-14, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18, separatorUnknownyearmodell3p pages 10-15, bath2003finetuningsieve pages 3-4)

## 2. Why LPBF calls for stricter changeover control

ISO/ASTM **52907** provides methods for characterizing powder, **not proof that an apparently clean sieve yields chemically uncontaminated powder**. Powder-life-cycle guidance summarized in the retrieved literature calls for tracking operations including sieving, assigning reusable hoppers to an alloy base, and cleaning between batches. A secondary source reports a stricter NASA **single-alloy-per-LPBF-machine** policy; that is a machine requirement as reported there, **not a verified NASA requirement to dedicate every handheld sieve**. In an observed AM facility, NIOSH documented feedstock-specific external vacuums and identified migrated powder particles on facility surfaces using SEM–EDX. These observations support dedicated powder-contact equipment as prudent practice, while showing that nearby tools, gloves, and surfaces also matter. (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42, gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 42-44, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 16-23, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 23-29)

The evidence for consequences is material, but **not a measured Cu–Sn-in-AlSi10Mg dose–response**:

* In a controlled Ti-6Al-4V/maraging-steel powder study, SEM–BSE plus particle-resolved EDS identified deliberately added **0.5, 1, and 2.5 wt%** foreign powder. At **0.5 wt% maraging steel in Ti64**, bulk-area EDS reported Fe as **not detectable**, although individual foreign particles were identified; ordinary bulk-property or bulk-composition screening can therefore miss discrete particles. The authors also documented foreign-alloy inclusions at fracture regions in related PBF examples, including an inclusion associated with crack initiation. **0.5 wt% was a tested detection case, not an acceptable contamination limit.** DOI: **10.3390/ma12152342**. (santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 1-3, santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 3-6, santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 6-9)
* An AlSi10Mg LPBF mirror study identified Ti-6Al-4V and probable 316L inclusions by SEM–EDS and associated observed scratches with inclusions. It implicated both printer powder interchange and contamination from the broader production environment; even a printer assigned only AlSi10Mg showed evidence consistent with environmental transfer. It also noted that a nominal **60 µm** sieve cannot remove smaller inclusions—many observed oxides were **<50 µm**. This demonstrates the limitation of trying to *sieve away* chemically different particles in the LPBF size range; it does **not** establish a Cu–Sn-specific cracking or corrosion threshold. DOI: **10.1088/2631-8695/ae545f**. (atkins2026mirrorfabricationusing pages 6-9, atkins2026mirrorfabricationusing pages 13-15, atkins2026mirrorfabricationusing pages 15-17)

An illustrative published AlSi10Mg composition table gives **Cu ≤0.10 wt%** and **Sn ≤0.05 wt%** (approximately **1,000 and 500 ppm by mass**). Its reference list attributes the composition to **ASTM F3318-18**, but the cited full standard and its applicability to your *new* Al alloy were not independently checked. **Composition maxima are not permission to introduce bronze particles:** a batch can meet a bulk Cu number yet contain an isolated Cu–Sn inclusion. Set acceptance against the actual feedstock specification and uncontaminated baseline. For example, if baseline Cu is 700 ppm and a 1,000-ppm maximum applies, the *arithmetic* bulk allowance is 300 ppm—**not** a demonstrated permissible foreign-particle level. (karolewska2025improvingthemechanical pages 5-7, karolewska2024improvingthemechanical media 2e0cbe01, karolewska2025improvingthemechanical pages 23-24, santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 1-3)

## 3. Cleaning verification and a small-lab changeover SOP

**Evidence grades:** *Manufacturer/handbook supported* below means a documented technique for maintaining sieve integrity; *proposed laboratory control* means an appropriately cautious qualification step **without a universal standard acceptance limit**. Do not turn a negative spot check into a claim of zero contamination.

1. **Before touching the bronze powder, control dust and keep alloys separate.** Identify the sieve construction and condition; label the bronze and aluminum streams, separate their pans, lids, funnels, scoops, wipes, brushes, gloves, and containers. Record sieve IDs, prior alloy, batch, and operator. Work using the lab’s combustible-metal-powder controls, containment, and appropriate PPE; do not shake or bang dusty sieves in the open. *Manufacturer-supported handling plus proposed changeover control.* (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 9-13, gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 42-44)
2. **Recover bronze without embedding it further.** Empty the **250 µm** sieve and then the **63 µm** sieve over separate designated bronze collectors; gently tap the frames. Gently brush only the underside of the 250 µm cloth with a clean soft nylon brush, if needed. Do **not** brush the 63 µm cloth or force lodged grains through either opening. Clean the frames, rim seams, lid, pan, and contact tools, not merely the visible center of the mesh. Use a vacuum **only if rated and approved for this combustible metal powder**, with an alloy-specific attachment; do not use a shop vacuum or jet powder into the room. *Manufacturer-supported mesh protection; proposed equipment qualification.* (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 13-14, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 16-23, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 2-3)
3. **Inspect before considering wet cleaning.** Examine both faces against a backlight and, preferably, under a stereo microscope; inspect particularly the 63 µm weave, mesh/frame junction, holes, sagging, clogged apertures, and retained bronze-colored grains. Regular microscopic examination and replacement of visibly damaged sieves are supported. A photograph and a **noncontact** tare-mass comparison can document gross residue but **cannot exclude a few particles**. If visible bronze remains in inaccessible joints, reserve or replace the sieve. *Handbook/maker-supported inspection; proposed cleanliness-release decision.* (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4, bath2003finetuningsieve pages 3-4, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18)
4. **Clean further only by an approved route.** For the current **bronze-only residue**, an aqueous mild-detergent ultrasonic cycle, thorough rinsing, and complete upright drying is a manufacturer-derived option **after** checking mesh bonding, bath compatibility, and EHS approval. Follow the *actual sieve maker* where its instructions differ. Do **not** use 149 °C as a default dryer setting or invent a mandatory frequency. Repeat microscopic inspection when dry. If ultrasonics cannot be conducted safely or damage-free, the low-risk answer is **new aluminum sieves**, not increasingly aggressive brushing. *Manufacturer supported, conditional on construction and EHS.* (bath2003finetuningsieve pages 3-4, separatorUnknownyearmodell3p pages 10-15, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18)
5. **Verify carryover before releasing aluminum powder.** With fresh gloves and a clean workspace, sample accessible frame/rim surfaces using a **blank-controlled wipe or carbon-tape lift**, then inspect for Cu–Sn particles by SEM–EDS if available. NIOSH used tape-mounted SEM–EDX to identify feedstock-like particles and surface wipes to quantify metals; a wiped **mesh face** itself could be damaged, so sample frames and other robust surfaces instead. A clean backlit mesh, no recovered Cu–Sn particles in the *inspected* areas, and no unexplained mass gain are useful screens, **not validated detection limits**. *Analytical methods supported; application and acceptance criteria proposed.* (stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 23-29, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 16-23, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18)
6. **If shared sieves must be used, quarantine a first aluminum fraction.** Process a small, separately labeled **sacrificial flush** of the new aluminum alloy through the same full stack, including pan and tools; **do not put that fraction back into feedstock**. Then take representative samples of the *first retained production fraction* and an unused, same-lot reference. Examine dispersed particles individually by SEM–BSE/EDS for coincident Cu **and Sn**; obtain validated digest/**ICP-OES** results for Cu and Sn versus the baseline and the alloy’s product specification when available. Record sampling mass, analytical reporting limits, and the area or number of particles examined. **No detected Cu–Sn particle in finite sampled areas is not a proof of none in the batch**; an ICP average alone cannot rule one out. A sacrificial flush is a **proposed process-development measure, not a standard-prescribed amount or substitute for release testing**. Fail or quarantine the batch if foreign particles are found, composition exceeds specification or baseline allowance, or the sieve is damaged. (santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 3-6, santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 6-9, gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 23-29)
7. **After aluminum sieving**, remove powder with the same controlled, contained dry-first approach, then store the aluminum-dedicated set clean, dry, covered, and labeled. Do not automatically transfer the bronze aqueous/ultrasonic recipe to an aluminum-loaded sieve; assess wet-cleaning and waste controls separately. Maintain an inspection and sieve-condition record; a sieve can be chemically clean yet have invalid enlarged openings. *Manufacturer-supported care; alloy-specific safety assessment required.* (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18, neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4, saluenaberna2024controlledandsafe pages 2-4)

**Loading matters for the fine sieve.** An Advantech guide tabulates, for **8-inch** sieves, illustrative recommended sample bulk volumes of **70 cm³ at 250 µm** versus **35 cm³ at 63 µm**, and maximum volumes left on those sieves after sieving of **35 and 17 cm³**, respectively. These are guidance for that sieve diameter and method, **not a universal batch-size limit**: divide larger atomized batches into suitable runs to reduce blinding and particle wedging. (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 21-23, mfg2001testsievingprinciples media f196beec, mfg2001testsievingprinciples media 1552c97a)

## 4. Aluminum-specific fire, hydrogen, and waste precautions

Finely divided aluminum presents a **combustible-metal-dust** hazard. Consult the applicable edition of **NFPA 484**, the powder SDS, local fire code, and university EHS for the actual setup; use suitable grounded/controlled handling equipment, avoid dispersed dust and ignition sources, and provide the prescribed metal-fire response rather than assuming water or an ordinary extinguisher is suitable for a burning metal. The retrieved literature emphasizes that metal dust requires more specialized controls than ordinary dust housekeeping. Compressed-air cleaning can both damage fine mesh and make an airborne dust cloud. (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 2-3, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 3-4, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18)

Aluminum and copper oxides *can* form an energetic thermite, but **a trace of surface-oxidized micron-scale bronze is not demonstrated to behave like an intentionally proportioned, intimately mixed Al/CuO nanothermite**. The distinction is not permission to ignore the hazard: a DOE incident report describes approximately **50 g of 44 µm aluminum and copper-oxide powders igniting while shaken in a partly filled polyethylene bottle**, with investigators suspecting electrostatic ignition of an aluminum–air dust cloud followed by thermite ignition. Avoid deliberately accumulating or vigorously mixing aluminum fines with copper-oxide-bearing waste. That incident provides **no quantitative safe bronze carryover level** for this sieve changeover. (cadwallader2003dustcombustionsafety pages 32-34, yao2020thermaldecompositionbehavior pages 3-5)

**Wet aluminum residues require a different assessment from clean metal mesh or residual bronze.** The native aluminum oxide film often delays reaction with water, but heating, agitation/ultrasound, exposed surfaces, and especially **alkaline** cleaners can promote hydrogen release. Consequently, do not immerse an aluminum-powder-loaded sieve in a heated alkaline ultrasonic bath, assume a water-filled ultrasonic tank is automatically safe, or put wet aluminum fines into an unassessed sealed waste bottle: hydrogen accumulation and pressure are plausible hazards. First recover bulk aluminum powder by a **metal-powder-rated, EHS-approved** method; use any subsequent aqueous or solvent wash only under a written, small-quantity procedure addressing ventilation, ignition control, bath/solvent compatibility, drying, and separately labeled metal-bearing waste. Never pour aluminum-fines slurry or bronze-containing rinse into the drain without institutional authorization. IPA or another solvent is **not an automatically safer substitute**: it introduces flammable-vapor and electrostatic hazards, particularly in ultrasonics. These are conservative risk controls, **not a claim that the cited standards prescribe one universal aluminum-sieve-washing recipe**. (saluenaberna2024controlledandsafe pages 2-4, zhang2024hydrogenproductionfrom pages 2-4, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 9-10, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 3-4)

**Bottom line:** Preserve the bronze sieves for bronze or noncritical work, purchase an aluminum-designated pair for LPBF, and qualify *any* unavoidable bronze-to-aluminum reuse through mesh inspection **and** representative, particle-resolved Cu–Sn checks of quarantined aluminum. There is no demonstrated universal concentration below which a foreign bronze particle is guaranteed harmless to an aluminum LPBF build. (santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 6-9, atkins2026mirrorfabricationusing pages 13-15, karolewska2025improvingthemechanical pages 5-7)
