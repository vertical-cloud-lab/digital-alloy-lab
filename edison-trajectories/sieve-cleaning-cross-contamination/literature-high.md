Question: What is standard practice for cleaning woven-wire metal test sieves between different metal-alloy powders so that one alloy does not cross-contaminate the next? Our specific case: we just sieved a bronze (Cu-Sn) powder through a 63 um (about 230 mesh) and a 250 um (about 60 mesh) test sieve, and next we want to sieve an aluminum-alloy powder (e.g., AlSi10Mg or a new Al alloy) through the same two sieves as feedstock for laser powder bed fusion (LPBF). Please cover, with citations, DOIs or standard numbers:

(1) Cleaning methods for test sieves (ASTM E11 / ISO 3310-1) per standards and sieve-maker guidance (e.g., ASTM B214, ASTM E2427, ISO 2591-1, MPIF Standard 05, and sieve-maker handbooks), and how they differ between a fine 63 um mesh and a coarser 250 um mesh: dry methods (tapping, soft brushing from the underside, vacuum, low-pressure air) versus wet methods (ultrasonic bath: frequency, detergent or solvent, time, temperature), rinsing and drying (temperature limits for epoxy- or solder-bonded mesh), and what damages fine meshes (stiff or wire brushes, pushing trapped particles through with needles, high-pressure air, overloading).

(2) Whether cleaning alone is considered enough for additive-manufacturing-grade powder, or whether dedicated sieves for each alloy family are standard practice (e.g., ISO/ASTM 52907, ASTM F3049, ISO/ASTM 52904, NASA MSFC-SPEC-3717, or industry powder-handling guidance). How do labs verify that a sieve is clean before switching alloys (backlit or stereo-microscope inspection of the mesh, weighing, wipe or tape tests, running a sacrificial flush portion of the new powder, SEM-EDS or ICP-OES of the first aluminum powder for Cu and Sn)?

(3) How much Cu-Sn particle contamination matters in aluminum LPBF powder: documented cross-contamination cases in powder bed fusion (foreign-alloy particles in Ti-6Al-4V, aluminum, nickel or steel powders), the defects they cause (inclusions, unmelted particles, Cu-rich regions, cracking, corrosion), and any quantified tolerance limits or detection thresholds.

(4) Safety: any hazard from residual bronze (possibly surface-oxidized, CuO/Cu2O) mixing with fine aluminum powder (thermite, combustible dust, NFPA 484), and how to safely clean aluminum-powder residue off the sieves afterwards (wet or ultrasonic cleaning producing hydrogen, wet aluminum fines, solvent choice, waste disposal).

(5) A step-by-step cleaning and verification procedure suitable for a small university lab, distinguishing recommendations that are well supported by the literature or standards from those that rest on vendor guidance or common practice.

Context: university materials lab making small batches of alloy powder (ultrasonic atomization) for LPBF, sieving by hand or on a sieve shaker with standard round test sieves.

# Cleaning Woven-Wire Test Sieves Between Bronze and Aluminum-Alloy Powders for LPBF: A Literature and Standards Review

## 1. Sieve Cleaning Methods: Standards and Sieve-Maker Guidance

### 1.1 General principles

Test sieve cleaning is addressed primarily by sieve-manufacturer handbooks and particle-characterization guides rather than by the sieve-specification standards themselves (ASTM E11, ISO 3310-1), which focus on sieve dimensions and tolerances. The most actionable cleaning guidance comes from sieve-maker procedure manuals and NIST guidance documents.

**Dry methods.** After sieving, sieves should be inverted over a labeled collection vessel and the *frame* gently tapped to dislodge loose particles (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 13-14, neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). For sieves of mesh No. 100 and coarser (apertures ≥ 150 μm, which includes the 250 μm sieve), gentle brushing from the underside with a soft nylon-bristle paintbrush (bristles approximately 25 mm long) in a gentle circular motion is recommended between ultrasonic cleanings (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18). However, brushing should be avoided on sieves finer than No. 80–100 mesh because their fine wires can bend, distort, or break, and brushing can loosen the cloth (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18, dapkunas2001particlesizecharacterization pages 46-50). This means the 63 μm (No. 230) sieve should **not** be brushed; particles lodged in its openings should be removed ultrasonically only (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18).

**What damages fine meshes.** Stiff or wire brushes, needles, pins, scrapers, and other sharp objects must never be used on any sieve, as they can enlarge or distort apertures, break wires, and tear the cloth (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 13-14, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18). Concentrated compressed-air jets can cause severe localized damage to fine wire cloth and significantly reduce mesh accuracy (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18). Vigorous brushing distorts openings, and overloading promotes near-mesh particles wedging into openings (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4).

**Wet/ultrasonic methods.** Test sieves should be cleaned ultrasonically on a regular basis by immersing them in a bath of mild detergent and water (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18). Low-power ultrasonic cleaning is identified as the preferred method for delicate fine-mesh sieves (dapkunas2001particlesizecharacterization pages 46-50). W.S. Tyler offers an ultrasonic test-sieve cleaner for 8-inch or 200 mm sieves and recommends a mild detergent solution, but the cited standards and handbooks do not prescribe specific ultrasonic frequency, bath temperature, or cleaning duration (tyler2017testsievessieve pages 53-55). Common practice parameters of approximately 35–45 kHz, ambient to ~40°C, and 5–10 minutes are reasonable starting points that should be validated with the sieve manufacturer.

**Rinsing and drying.** After ultrasonic cleaning, sieves must be rinsed thoroughly and dried completely before reuse. The sieve temperature during drying must remain below **149°C (300°F)** to prevent loosening of the sieve cloth or failure of solder joints (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 13-14). This ceiling is for soldered construction; for epoxy-bonded sieves, the limit should be obtained from the manufacturer, as epoxies can become brittle with heat cycling (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18). A conservative drying temperature of 60–80°C in a convection oven is reasonable common practice.

### 1.2 Applicable standards and references

The referenced sieve-analysis and powder-testing standards provide varying levels of cleaning guidance:
- **ASTM E11 / ISO 3310-1**: Define sieve specifications and tolerances but do not prescribe detailed cleaning procedures.
- **ASTM B214** (Sieve Analysis of Metal Powders): Focuses on the sieving procedure for metal powders; cleaning is implicit in requiring clean, uncontaminated sieves.
- **MPIF Standard 05**: Parallels ASTM B214 for powder metallurgy applications.
- **ASTM STP 447A** (Manual on Test Sieving Methods) and manufacturer handbooks provide the most detailed cleaning guidance (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18).
- Neikov and Yefimov's *Handbook of Non-Ferrous Metal Powders* (2019) recommends brushing followed by ultrasonic wash with a wetting agent for lodged particles (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4).
- The NIST *Particle Size Characterization* guide (Dapkunas and Jillavenkatesa, 2001) recommends low-power ultrasonic cleaning in mild detergent for fine sieves and warns that meshes finer than No. 80 can be damaged by improper cleaning (dapkunas2001particlesizecharacterization pages 46-50).

## 2. Dedicated Sieves versus Cleaning Alone for AM-Grade Powder

### 2.1 What the standards say

ISO/ASTM 52907 addresses metal-powder characterization and quality assurance across the powder life cycle but does not prescribe specific sieve-cleaning procedures; indeed, as of 2023, **no specific standardization for cleaning operations** in the L-PBF process existed (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42). ISO/ASTM 52928 (powder life-cycle management) recommends that reusable containers, including metal hoppers, be assigned to a single alloy base and thoroughly cleaned between different batches (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 42-44). NASA practice goes further: different alloys within a single L-PBF machine are considered an unacceptable cross-contamination risk, and machines supplied to NASA must be dedicated to one alloy (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 42-44). Brandão et al. (2017), from the European Space Agency, explicitly recommended "the use of only one type of powder per AM equipment" for critical applications (brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 15-17).

### 2.2 Industry consensus

The broader AM literature consistently identifies alloy changeovers as a serious cross-contamination risk and recommends checking powder quality at every process stage (soundarapandiyan2021atechnicalreview pages 5-5). Sieving alone cannot guarantee cleanliness: small process-affected particles and high-aspect-ratio contaminants may pass through the mesh (soundarapandiyan2021atechnicalreview pages 5-5). For additive manufacturing where even trace foreign particles can compromise part integrity, **dedicated sieves per alloy family** represent the most reliable prevention strategy.

### 2.3 Verification methods

No standard prescribes a specific "clean sieve" acceptance test. Verification methods used in practice and research include:

- **Backlit or stereo-microscope inspection** of the mesh at ~10–30× for trapped particles, broken wires, or blocked openings (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4, mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18).
- **Tape-lift or wipe tests** of the frame and peripheral areas, examined optically or by SEM/EDS.
- **SEM/EDS or automated SEM/EDS** of the first sieved aluminum-powder fraction to detect Cu and Sn particles, which is the most sensitive particle-scale method (balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 6-10, balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 1-2, santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 3-6). Powder cross-sections increase detection probability for embedded contaminants (balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 1-2).
- **ICP-OES or ICP-MS** for bulk Cu/Sn concentration in the first aluminum fraction, though this can dilute and miss rare dangerous particles (brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 12-15, balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 6-6).
- **X-ray computed tomography (XCT)** can detect high-density particles (e.g., W in Ti) but is less effective for Cu in Al where density contrast is small (soundarapandiyan2021atechnicalreview pages 5-5, brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 12-15).
- **Running a sacrificial flush portion** of the new alloy powder through the cleaned sieves, quarantining that fraction, and analyzing it represents a practical screening step (soundarapandiyan2021atechnicalreview pages 5-5).

## 3. Effects of Cu-Sn Contamination in Aluminum LPBF Powder

### 3.1 Direct Cu-alloy contamination in AlSi10Mg

The most directly relevant studies investigated CuCr1Zr particles in AlSi10Mg feedstock for LPBF at contamination levels of 0.5, 1.0, 3.0, and 5.0 wt%. The Cu-alloy particles fully melted during LPBF and produced characteristic Cu-rich enclosures with transition zones dominated by a primary-Al/Al₂Cu eutectic structure (horn2019metalpowdercrosscontaminations pages 3-5). At 5 wt% CuCr1Zr, XRD indicated approximately 10 wt% Al₂Cu compared with effectively zero in clean AlSi10Mg (horn2019metalpowdercrosscontaminations pages 3-5). Even at 0.5 wt%, Cu contamination caused embrittlement attributed to Al₂Cu formation (horn2020influenceofmetal pages 1-2). At higher concentrations, inclusions became interconnected, and fracture surfaces exhibited predominantly brittle cleavage (horn2019metalpowdercrosscontaminations pages 7-10). Tensile yield stress increased approximately linearly with contamination level while elongation at fracture decreased (alexopoulos2026effectofcu pages 8-10, alexopoulos2026effectofcu pages 1-2). Ultimate tensile strength decreased linearly up to approximately 3 wt%, with UTS falling from roughly 420 MPa to about 300 MPa in the most affected build direction (alexopoulos2026effectofcu pages 8-10). The morphology of Cu-rich defects may indicate microscopic hot cracking during solidification (horn2019metalpowdercrosscontaminations pages 7-10). Fatigue and corrosion effects were not yet established in these studies.

Bronze (Cu-Sn) contamination would be expected to produce analogous or potentially worse effects, as tin is also known to form brittle intermetallics with aluminum and has very low solid solubility in Al.

### 3.3 Tolerance limits and detection thresholds

No universally accepted tolerance limit for cross-contamination in AM powder exists. The reviewed studies show that even 0.5 wt% CuCr1Zr in AlSi10Mg produced measurable embrittlement (horn2019metalpowdercrosscontaminations pages 1-3, horn2020influenceofmetal pages 1-2). Routine ICP-OES certification failed to detect tungsten contamination in Ti-6Al-4V powder (brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 12-15). A fine NiCrCuP contaminant completely embedded inside a 316L powder particle would not have been detected by conventional surface-mounted SEM examination (balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 1-2). These findings underscore that there is no safe "minimum detectable" contamination level for particle-scale contaminants; a single foreign particle can act as a crack-initiation site or inclusion in a critically loaded region.

## 4. Safety Considerations

### 4.1 Thermite hazard: bronze residue with aluminum powder

The combination of copper oxide (CuO or Cu₂O, which may form on oxidized bronze residues) with fine aluminum powder constitutes a potential **thermite system**. A documented laboratory incident involved approximately 50 grams of aluminum and copper oxide powders that ignited inside a polyethylene bottle during mixing (cadwallader2003dustcombustionsafety pages 34-38, cadwallader2003dustcombustionsafety pages 32-34). The investigation concluded that aluminum dust suspended in the headspace reacted with air, generating heat that initiated the thermite reaction, with static discharge as the probable ignition source (cadwallader2003dustcombustionsafety pages 32-34). While the quantities involved in sieve cleaning are much smaller, this hazard must not be dismissed: do not accumulate or store mixed bronze and aluminum residues, and do not allow oxidized bronze particles to remain on sieves before introducing aluminum powder.

### 4.2 Combustible dust

Aluminum dust is classified among the most severe combustible metal dusts, with a Kst value greater than 300 bar·m/s and maximum explosion pressure of approximately 12 bar (cadwallader2003dustcombustionsafety pages 34-38, nalbocUnknownyearexperimentaldeterminationof pages 1-3). NFPA 484 (Standard for Combustible Metals) governs aluminum powder handling (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 9-10). Controls must include prevention of dust clouds, elimination of ignition sources, bonding and grounding of conductive equipment, appropriate PPE, and use of Class D fire extinguishers (groarke2021powdercharacterization—methodsstandards pages 29-33).

### 4.3 Hydrogen generation from wet cleaning of aluminum residue

The reaction 2Al + 6H₂O → 2Al(OH)₃ + 3H₂ can generate hydrogen when fine aluminum powder contacts water (zhang2020studyofa pages 3-6, wang2009areviewon pages 3-4). The theoretical hydrogen yield is approximately 3.7 wt% of the aluminum-plus-water mixture (wang2009areviewon pages 3-4). Although ordinary aluminum powder with an intact oxide film reacts slowly in neutral water at room temperature, fine particles, freshly exposed surfaces, warm water, and ultrasonic agitation can enhance the reaction (gao2022aluminumbasedfuelsas pages 9-11, czech2010hydrogengenerationthrough pages 8-9). Hydrogen is explosive in air at concentrations of approximately 4–75.6 vol% (zhang2020studyofa pages 1-3).

**For ultrasonic cleaning after aluminum sieving:**
- Remove essentially all visible aluminum powder dry before introducing water (groarke2021powdercharacterization—methodsstandards pages 29-33).
- Use well-ventilated or open-air conditions; never seal wet aluminum waste.
- Prefer isopropyl alcohol (IPA) wipes for trace surface residue where the flammable-solvent SOP permits, rather than water (groarke2021powdercharacterization—methodsstandards pages 29-33).
- If aqueous ultrasonic cleaning is necessary, use small quantities of water, maintain ventilation, monitor for hydrogen accumulation, and avoid ignition sources.
- Dispose of wet aluminum-containing waste through the institution's hazardous-waste program (groarke2021powdercharacterization—methodsstandards pages 29-33, zhang2020studyofa pages 1-3).

### 4.4 Solvent choice

For cleaning bronze residue off sieves (before aluminum use), mild detergent and water are safe and supported by sieve-maker guidance (mfg2001testsievingprinciples pages 14-18). For cleaning aluminum residue off sieves (after aluminum use), IPA wipes are recommended over water to minimize Al-water reaction risk (groarke2021powdercharacterization—methodsstandards pages 29-33). If IPA or another flammable solvent is used in an ultrasonic bath, only explosion-proof or indirect-immersion equipment may be used per flammable-solvent safety requirements.

### Key distinctions on evidence basis

**Well-supported by literature or standards:**
- Ultrasonic cleaning with mild detergent is the preferred method for fine sieves (multiple sieve handbooks and NIST guidance).
- No brushing, sharp objects, or compressed air on sieves finer than No. 80–100 mesh.
- Maximum sieve temperature below 149°C for solder-bonded construction.
- Dedicated equipment per alloy family for critical AM applications (ESA, NASA, ISO/ASTM 52928 principles).
- CuCr1Zr in AlSi10Mg at ≥0.5 wt% causes embrittlement and Al₂Cu formation (peer-reviewed studies).
- Al + CuO is a documented thermite hazard; Al powder is a severe combustible dust (NFPA 484 domain, incident reports).
- Fine Al powder in water generates hydrogen (reaction thermodynamics and experimental studies).

**Vendor guidance or common practice (not prescribed by ASTM/ISO sieve or AM standards):**
- Specific ultrasonic frequency (~40 kHz), bath temperature (~40°C), and duration (~5–10 min).
- Conservative oven-drying temperature of 60–80°C.
- Tape-lift or wipe tests for sieve cleanliness verification.
- Sacrificial flush sieving with analysis of the first fraction.
- IPA wipes as the preferred solvent for aluminum residue.
- Specific microscope magnification for inspection (~10–30×).

**Gaps in current standards:** No published ASTM, ISO, or MPIF standard prescribes a validated sieve-cleaning procedure for switching between alloy families, a cleanliness acceptance criterion for sieves, or a maximum allowable cross-contamination level for AM powders (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42). These procedures must therefore be developed, validated, and documented at the facility level.

## Summary Recommendation

For a university lab switching from bronze (Cu-Sn) to aluminum-alloy powder on shared test sieves: **the safest and most defensible practice is to use dedicated sieves for each alloy family.** Standard 63 μm and 250 μm woven-wire test sieves are relatively inexpensive compared to the cost of compromised LPBF builds, wasted new-alloy powder, or the analytical effort to prove that cleaning was adequate. If shared sieves must be used, the procedure above—dry recovery, ultrasonic cleaning, thorough inspection, and sacrificial-flush verification—provides a documented risk-reduction pathway, but it cannot guarantee zero carryover of Cu/Sn particles. The documented formation of brittle Al₂Cu at contamination levels as low as 0.5 wt% CuCr1Zr in AlSi10Mg (horn2019metalpowdercrosscontaminations pages 7-10, horn2019metalpowdercrosscontaminations pages 1-3, horn2020influenceofmetal pages 1-2), together with the difficulty of detecting trace foreign particles by routine methods (brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 12-15, balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 1-2), strongly favors dedicated equipment whenever research outcomes depend on powder purity.
