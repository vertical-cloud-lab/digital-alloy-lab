Question: CONTEXT: FLAIME is a DOE Genesis Mission Phase I proposal ($500-750K, 9 months) for Topic 3B: "AI-Enabled Materials Discovery and Engineering — Reducing Demand for Critical Minerals" (AMMTO). It is an autonomous closed-loop alloy discovery platform: CALPHAD → ultrasonic atomization → LPBF 3D printing → ASTM E8 tensile testing → BOFN (Bayesian Optimization with Function Networks) optimization. Material strategy: Al-Zr and Al-Mn-Cr-Zr families PRIMARY (~80%), Cu-Cr-Zr COMPLEMENT (~20% stretch for Be-Cu replacement). AlSi10Mg is benchmark only.

KPI UNDER REVIEW: "Closed-Loop Throughput" — alloy compositions tested/month through the full pipeline (CALPHAD → atomization → LPBF → tensile → BOFN). Phase I target: ≥8 compositions/month (Base: ≥5/month). Phase II target: ≥20 compositions/month. Total 9-month target: ≥30 (Base) to ≥50 (Expected) unique mineral-lean compositions.

Provide a critical literature-grounded review of this KPI:
1. Is ≥8 compositions/month realistic for a full CALPHAD→atomization→LPBF→tensile→BOFN pipeline? What throughputs have been demonstrated by comparable SDL platforms? Reference A-Lab (2.4 materials/day but XRD-only), BIRDSHOT/ULTIMATE (5 HEA iterations but arc-melting), Abolhasani flow chemistry SDLs (higher throughput but different domain).
2. What are the bottlenecks? Ultrasonic atomization batch time, LPBF build time for multi-composition prints, ASTM E8 tensile testing (specimen prep + testing), BOFN computation time. Is the pipeline serial or are there parallelizable steps?
3. Is "compositions tested" the right unit? Should it be "compositions with complete property records" (including failed builds)? How should partial data (density but no tensile) count?
4. The ramp-up profile (months 1-3 infrastructure, months 4-9 steady state) — is 3 months to steady state realistic for a novel alloy SDL? What does the SDL setup literature say?
5. What improvements would make this KPI more precisely defined and defensible?

# Critical Literature-Grounded Review of FLAIME's "Closed-Loop Throughput" KPI

## 1. Is ≥8 Compositions/Month Realistic? Benchmarking Against Comparable SDL Platforms

The proposed FLAIME target of ≥8 compositions/month through a full CALPHAD→atomization→LPBF→tensile→BOFN pipeline is ambitious but represents a qualitatively different challenge compared to existing SDL benchmarks, because FLAIME's pipeline includes both powder production and destructive mechanical testing — steps absent from the most cited SDL throughput demonstrations.

**A-Lab.** The A-Lab autonomous laboratory at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory demonstrated the synthesis and XRD characterization of 41 novel inorganic compounds from 58 targets over 17 days of continuous operation, yielding ~2.4 realized materials per day and performing 355 total experiments (~20.9 experiments/day) with an average cycle time of ~69 minutes per experiment (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 4-5, szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 2-3). However, A-Lab's cycle involves only robotic precursor dispensing, furnace synthesis (4-hour dwell), and automated XRD — no powder production, additive manufacturing, specimen machining, or tensile testing (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 7-7). The A-Lab throughput is therefore not directly comparable to FLAIME's multi-step structural alloy pipeline.

**BIRDSHOT/ULTIMATE.** The BIRDSHOT program for refractory high-entropy alloys achieved a synthesis throughput of ~13 alloys/week (~56 alloys/month) using vacuum arc melting (arroyave2024ultimatebirdshot.final pages 1-5, arroyave2024ultimatebirdshot.final pages 11-14). Over approximately two years, 140 unique compositions were synthesized, 108 characterized (SEM/EDX, XRD), and 116 homogenized (arroyave2024ultimatebirdshot.final pages 9-11). The planned iteration cadence was ~2 months per design iteration with 5 iterations targeted per year (mulukutla2024illustratinganeffective pages 5-7). Critically, BIRDSHOT's characterization significantly lagged synthesis, and the pipeline used arc melting — orders of magnitude faster per composition than ultrasonic atomization plus LPBF. BIRDSHOT did not include ASTM E8 tensile testing in its primary high-throughput loop.

**HT-READ.** The HT-READ platform demonstrated fabrication of 16 distinct alloy compositions per directed energy deposition (DED) build cycle using an automated 16-vial feeder (vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 7-11, vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 4-7). Automated characterization (XRD at ~10 min/sample, SEM-EDS/EBSD at ~1 min/sample) enabled rapid multi-modal screening (vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 33-50). However, HT-READ used nanoindentation as a mechanical proxy rather than ASTM E8 tensile testing, and the combinatorial DED library approach is not directly transferable to LPBF-based qualification.

**Flow Chemistry SDLs.** Abolhasani and colleagues have demonstrated flow-chemistry SDLs with throughputs of up to 100 samples/hour, with physical automation providing ~2× acceleration and AI-enabled closed loops providing an additional 5–20× acceleration factor (volk2024performancemetricsto pages 2-3, delgadolicona2023researchaccelerationin pages 2-2). These platforms benefit enormously from reaction miniaturization, continuous flow, and in-line spectroscopic characterization — advantages unavailable in structural alloy discovery where powder production, AM builds, and destructive testing impose irreducible time floors.

**Assessment.** The target of ≥8 compositions/month through a complete CALPHAD-to-tensile pipeline is achievable under favorable conditions (pre-existing equipment, experienced team, effective batching), but the ≥5/month base target is more defensible. No existing SDL has demonstrated comparable throughput for a pipeline that includes both powder production and destructive mechanical testing closure.

## 2. Pipeline Bottleneck Analysis

A step-by-step time analysis of the FLAIME pipeline reveals that the physical processing steps — not computation — dominate cycle time.

**Ultrasonic atomization** is the most likely rate-limiting step. Laboratory-scale ultrasonic atomizers (e.g., ATO Lab) operate on ~60-minute run cycles (bałasz2024aninvestigationof pages 2-4). Starting from 40–50 g rods, only 45–50 wt.% converts to powder particles <100 µm (hinrichs2021flexiblepowderproduction pages 4-7), and a high fraction (>85%) falls in the 40–80 µm range suitable for LPBF (hinrichs2021flexiblepowderproduction pages 4-7). Including setup, cleaning, and composition changeover, each alloy composition may require 1.5–2 hours. Five of sixteen planned runs in one study were discontinued due to process instability (bałasz2024aninvestigationof pages 2-4), indicating that composition-dependent failure is a significant risk for novel alloys.

**LPBF build time** for ASTM E8 or sub-size tensile specimens typically ranges from 4–12 hours per build plate depending on the number of specimens and part geometry. Multi-composition builds on a single plate can partially amortize this time but introduce cross-contamination concerns and require careful powder management.

**Specimen preparation and tensile testing** include EDM/wire-cutting of specimens from the build plate, surface preparation, and fixturing. ASTM E8 testing itself is rapid (~15–30 min per specimen), but specimen preparation and quality checks add 1–2 hours per composition.

**BOFN computation** is not a bottleneck. The original p-KGFN algorithm requires ~5–11 minutes per Bayesian optimization iteration, while the accelerated Fast p-KGFN variant reduces this to ~0.3–2.8 minutes per iteration with up to 16× speedup (buathong2506fastbayesianoptimization pages 1-2, buathong2506fastbayesianoptimization pages 6-8). CALPHAD screening is similarly fast, capable of processing thousands of compositions in hours on a desktop (feng2021highthroughputdesignof pages 8-9).

**Parallelizability.** The pipeline is predominantly serial at the composition level: powder must be produced before it can be printed, and specimens must be printed before they can be tested. However, pipeline-level parallelism is achievable — e.g., atomizing composition N+1 while printing composition N while testing composition N−1. This pipelining could approximately triple effective throughput relative to a purely serial workflow.

## 3. Is "Compositions Tested" the Right Unit?

The SDL performance metrics literature strongly suggests that "compositions tested" is insufficient as a standalone KPI and should be disaggregated.

Volk and Abolhasani (2024) recommend reporting both demonstrated and theoretical throughput, with explicit inclusion of sample preparation and measurement rates, and distinguish between throughput units (samples/hour, measurements/hour) that may differ substantially (volk2024performancemetricsto pages 2-3, volk2024performancemetricsto pages 1-2). Sadeghi et al. (2024) warn that "oversizing" throughput can be misleading and recommend including maintenance/reliability metrics such as downtime, discarded experiments, and time-consuming rectification (sadeghi2024engineeringasustainable pages 7-8). Adesiji et al. (2026) define enhancement factor (EF) and acceleration factor (AF) as more informative benchmarks that compare algorithm-driven performance against a reference strategy after a fixed number of experiments (adesiji2026benchmarkingselfdrivinglabs pages 1-4, adesiji2026benchmarkingselfdrivinglabs pages 4-7).

For FLAIME specifically, the following disaggregation is recommended:

- **Tier 1 (Primary KPI): Compositions with complete property records** — compositions for which CALPHAD predictions, powder characteristics, LPBF build density, and ASTM E8 tensile data (UTS, YS, elongation) are all recorded. This is the metric that matters for the BOFN loop.
- **Tier 2: Compositions attempted** — compositions that entered the pipeline but failed at any stage (atomization instability, LPBF build failure, specimen defects). These still represent valuable data for the BOFN model, particularly for learning constraint boundaries.
- **Tier 3: Compositions with partial data** — e.g., density measured but tensile testing not completed. BOFN with partial evaluations is specifically designed to exploit such data, making partial records algorithmically valuable (buathong2023bayesianoptimizationof pages 20-22, buathong2023bayesianoptimizationof pages 9-10).

Failed builds should be counted explicitly as they provide constraint information to the optimizer. Reporting only successful completions would understate the platform's actual experimental activity and miss the opportunity to leverage BOFN's partial-evaluation capabilities.

## 4. Is a 3-Month Ramp-Up to Steady State Realistic?

The SDL literature consistently indicates that commissioning autonomous platforms is time-intensive. MacLeod (2022) reports that building an SDL "can take years" and requires "a year or more of full-time work for a team of roughly 5–10 people," with the requirement that each workflow step fail fewer than 1 in 1,000 times for reliable end-to-end operation (macleod2022aselfdrivinglaboratory pages 122-126). The BIRDSHOT program allocated 1.5 months solely for data and sample management infrastructure setup, with a 2-month buffer in its 1-year program timeline (mulukutla2024illustratinganeffective pages 5-7). Abolhasani and Kumacheva (2023) note that setup duration depends heavily on the targeted class of materials and the required hardware/software integration (abolhasani2023theriseof pages 2-3).

For FLAIME, a 3-month ramp-up is aggressive but not impossible under the following conditions: (a) the team has prior hands-on experience with each individual pipeline component (CALPHAD, ultrasonic atomization, LPBF, tensile testing); (b) the equipment is pre-existing and operational, not being procured or commissioned from scratch; (c) the initial integration focuses on a benchmark alloy (AlSi10Mg) where parameters are well-established; and (d) the BOFN software framework is already developed and tested in silico. If any of these conditions are not met — particularly if equipment procurement or novel software development is required — 3 months will likely prove insufficient, and a 4–5 month ramp-up should be planned.

A key risk is that novel alloy compositions (Al-Zr, Al-Mn-Cr-Zr, Cu-Cr-Zr) will require substantial process parameter development for both atomization and LPBF, which can consume weeks per alloy family. The first 1–2 months of the "steady state" period may actually deliver reduced throughput as the team encounters composition-dependent failures.

**Recommendation:** Define months 1–3 as "infrastructure and qualification," months 4–5 as "ramp-up with benchmark alloy + first novel compositions at reduced rate (~3–5/month)," and months 6–9 as "steady-state target rate (≥8/month)." This phased profile is more defensible than a step-function transition at month 4.

## 5. Recommendations for Making the KPI More Precisely Defined and Defensible

Based on the SDL performance metrics literature and the pipeline bottleneck analysis, the following improvements are recommended:

**(i) Disaggregate the throughput KPI into a tiered metric:**
- Primary: "Compositions with complete tensile property records per month" (the metric that directly feeds BOFN)
- Secondary: "Compositions entered into pipeline per month" (including failures, which provide BOFN constraint data)
- Tertiary: "Cumulative unique compositions with at least density data" (exploits BOFN partial evaluation capability)

**(ii) Report demonstrated vs. theoretical throughput**, following Volk and Abolhasani's (2024) recommendation (volk2024performancemetricsto pages 1-2). The current KPI conflates these. The theoretical throughput should be computed from the bottleneck analysis; the demonstrated throughput should be reported monthly.

**(iii) Define a minimum viable data record** for a composition to count as "tested." This should include at minimum: alloy composition (ICP verified), powder PSD and morphology, LPBF build density (Archimedes or CT), and at least one mechanical property (hardness or tensile). Compositions achieving this minimum record should count toward the KPI.

**(iv) Include a pipeline efficiency metric**, such as "completion rate" = (compositions with full records) / (compositions entered into pipeline). This captures reliability, a key concern for novel alloy SDLs (sadeghi2024engineeringasustainable pages 7-8, macleod2022aselfdrivinglaboratory pages 122-126). A target of ≥70% completion rate would be defensible based on A-Lab's 71% success rate for a simpler pipeline (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 4-5).

**(v) Adopt a phased ramp-up profile** with explicit monthly targets: months 1–3 at 0 compositions (infrastructure), month 4 at 2–3 compositions (commissioning with AlSi10Mg benchmark), months 5–6 at 5–6 compositions (ramp-up with novel alloys), months 7–9 at ≥8 compositions/month (steady state). This acknowledges the reality of SDL commissioning timelines while preserving the ambitious Phase I total.

**(vi) Report an acceleration factor** relative to a defined baseline (e.g., conventional alloy development cycle of 6–12 months per composition), following the benchmarking framework of Adesiji et al. (2026) (adesiji2026benchmarkingselfdrivinglabs pages 1-4). This contextualizes the absolute throughput number for DOE reviewers.

**(vii) Explicitly account for BOFN's partial evaluation capability** as a distinguishing feature of the proposal. BOFN can intelligently select which pipeline nodes to evaluate for which compositions, potentially skipping expensive downstream steps (e.g., tensile testing) for compositions predicted to fail early (buathong2023bayesianoptimizationof pages 20-22, buathong2023bayesianoptimizationof pages 9-10). This should be reflected in the KPI by allowing partial evaluations to count toward a secondary metric.

In summary, the ≥8 compositions/month target through the full CALPHAD→atomization→LPBF→tensile→BOFN pipeline is at the frontier of what has been demonstrated for structural alloy SDL platforms and should be presented as an ambitious but achievable target, with ≥5/month as a defensible base case. The KPI would benefit substantially from tiered metrics, a phased ramp-up schedule, explicit failure accounting, and contextualization against established SDL benchmarking frameworks.
