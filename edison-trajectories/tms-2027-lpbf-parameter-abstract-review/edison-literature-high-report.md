## 1. Standard Parameter-Optimization Workflows

### 1.1 Single-Track Melt-Pool Studies and Density Cubes

The canonical LPBF parameter development workflow proceeds from single-track experiments to density-cube fabrication. Pfaff et al. (2020; DOI: 10.3390/ma13235400) presented a comprehensive eight-step empirical methodology: single laser track experiments characterize melt pool width and depth across laser power and scan speed combinations, followed by iterative design-of-experiments (DoE) using central composite design (CCC) to optimize volumetric parameters including laser power, scanning speed, and hatch spacing (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 10-13, pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 3-5, pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 5-8, pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3). This workflow was validated across six alloys (tungsten, tantalum, MS1, AlSi10Mg, Scalmalloy, 1.6947), achieving relative densities of 99.18–99.99% (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 5-8).

Bergmueller et al. (2023; DOI: 10.1007/s00170-023-11377-2) demonstrated a systematic approach using normalized enthalpy scaling laws to classify single tracks into conduction, transition, and keyhole modes across wide parameter ranges (laser power 100–1000 W, scan speed 300–2500 mm/s, spot size 0.08–0.25 mm). Parameters identified in the transition mode were used to optimize density cubes with varying hatch distance (0.06–0.12 mm), achieving relative densities exceeding 99.8% (bergmueller2023systematicapproachto pages 1-2).

### 1.2 Roles of Key Process Parameters and Energy Density

The five principal process parameters—laser power, scan speed, hatch spacing, layer thickness, and beam diameter—are universally recognized as the most critical variables (ozturk2026processparametereffects pages 1-2). Volumetric energy density (VED = P/v·t·d) remains widely used as a first-order screening metric (pfaff2020anempiricalapproach pages 1-3). However, Smith et al. (2025; DOI: 10.1007/s11837-024-06946-z) provided compelling evidence that VED is an insufficient standalone predictor: nine parameter sets for 316L stainless steel with identical VED (85 J/mm³) produced systematically different pore types and amounts depending on the specific laser power, scan velocity, and hatch spacing combinations (smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 2-3, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 1-2, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 5-7, smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 10-11). The authors proposed mechanism-specific defect maps using analytical models for keyholing, lack of fusion, and spatter-induced porosity as superior alternatives (smith2025assessingvolumetricenergy pages 3-5). Öztürk et al. (2026; DOI: 10.1007/s40964-025-01512-8) similarly showed through full-factorial DoE on M300 maraging steel that different parameter sets with identical VED values produce significantly different mechanical properties (ozturk2026processparametereffects pages 1-2).

### 1.3 ISO/ASTM 52902 Geometric Artifacts and Tensile-Bar Qualification

Berez et al. (2021; DOI: 10.1016/j.promfg.2021.06.042) implemented the ISO/ASTM 52902 standard on an LPBF system, evaluating linear, cylindrical, and resolution artifacts via coordinate measuring machines. Their study identified beam positioning and offset errors that scaled with distance from the build area origin, and emphasized that measurement methodology choices can significantly bias results—the standard provides flexibility but minimal prescription of measurement strategy (berez2021assessinglaserpowder pages 11-12, berez2021assessinglaserpowder pages 1-2, berez2021assessinglaserpowder pages 2-3). Brenner & Nedeljkovic-Groha (2025; DOI: 10.3390/app15179756) extended this work using the ISO/ASTM 52902:2023 Z-artefact for vertical dimensional accuracy correction in AlSi10Mg, reducing RMSE from over 100 µm to below 25 µm through shrinkage compensation.

### 1.4 Design-of-Experiments vs. Sequential Methods

Mulhi et al. (2023; DOI: 10.3390/met13040725) employed response surface methodology across a wide energy density range (22.22–428.87 J/mm³) for 2507 super duplex stainless steel, identifying lack-of-fusion, metallurgical, and keyhole pore regimes and an optimal window from 68.24 to 126.67 J/mm³ yielding ≥99.6% relative density (mulhi2023processparameteroptimization pages 8-11). Gobert (2023; DOI: 10.1184/r1/23699754) demonstrated both full factorial search and Bayesian optimization approaches within a unified framework for process development at the melt pool scale, using single-track characterization followed by density cube validation with porosity thresholds of 0.1–1% (gobert2023processdevelopmentin pages 46-49).

## 2. Transferability and Reproducibility Across Machines

### 2.1 Quantified Transfer Gaps

Reijonen et al. (2021; DOI: 10.1016/j.matdes.2021.109684) provided the most rigorous quantitative evidence for cross-machine variability. Cross-testing 316L stainless steel between two different LPBF machines from different vendors (SLM 125 HL and EOS M 290) with different powder batches revealed approximately 7% maximum variation in tensile properties and 20% variation in impact toughness between machine-powder combinations (reijonen2021crosstestinglaserpowder pages 1-2, reijonen2021crosstestinglaserpowder pages 2-3). They identified key machine-dependent variables including build nesting density, part location on platform, shielding gas flow, laser spot characteristics, and scanning strategies. Standardized post-process heat treatments (stress-relieving, solution annealing, and HIP) reduced but did not fully eliminate these differences (reijonen2021crosstestinglaserpowder pages 13-13).

Öztürk et al. (2026) explicitly noted that parameter sets optimized on one LPBF machine often cannot be directly applied to another due to variations in laser sources, beam diameters, chamber atmospheres, and preheating strategies, typically requiring adaptation or redevelopment for each machine model (ozturk2026processparametereffects pages 1-2). Zongo et al. (2018; DOI: 10.3390/jmmp2030056) quantified intra- and inter-repeatability for AlSi10Mg tooling components on an EOSINT M280, finding that all geometrical variations of 147 identical parts fell within a range of 230 µm at 99.73% confidence.

### 2.2 Transfer-Learning and Domain-Adaptation Approaches

Safdar et al. (2024; DOI: 10.1115/1.4065090) proposed a three-step knowledge transferability analysis framework for data-driven AM, demonstrating successful transfer between LPBF and DED at multiple levels: data representation, model architecture, and model parameters (safdar2024transferabilityanalysisof pages 5-7, safdar2024transferabilityanalysisof pages 1-3, safdar2024transferabilityanalysisof pages 8-9, safdar2024transferabilityanalysisof pages 4-5). Their framework formalizes when, what, and how to transfer knowledge using instance-based, feature-based, parameter-based, and relation-based approaches. While the case study crosses process technologies (LPBF→DED) rather than machine instances, the methodology is directly applicable to reducing re-qualification cost on a target machine. Notably, Liu et al. (2021; DOI: 10.1016/j.addma.2021.101877, unobtainable but referenced) demonstrated ML-based knowledge transfer across multiple metal AM printers, and Funcke et al. (2026; DOI: 10.3390/met16040438, unobtainable) addressed knowledge transfer between machines in LPBF with small training datasets.

**Evidence strength assessment:** Cross-machine variability is **strongly** supported by Reijonen et al. (2021). Transfer-learning approaches specifically for LPBF machine-to-machine transfer are **weakly** supported by currently retrieved literature—most published work addresses cross-process (e.g., LPBF→DED) or cross-material transfer rather than same-process/different-machine scenarios. This represents a genuine gap in the literature that the FLAIME project could address.

## 3. Machine-Learning and Bayesian Optimization Methods

### 3.1 GP-Based Bayesian Optimization for LPBF

Liu et al. (2020; DOI: 10.1016/j.actamat.2020.10.010) demonstrated Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) for AlSi10Mg LPBF optimization, identifying a larger processing window for achieving ≥99% relative density than previously known, using 82 parameter combinations. The GPR model achieved prediction errors less than 0.3% in relative density and enabled previously unattainable combinations of high strength and ductility (UTS 297–389 MPa, elongation 6.3–10.3%) (liu2020machinelearningassistedlaser pages 9-14, liu2020machinelearningassistedlaser pages 1-5, liu2020machinelearningassistedlaser pages 29-34, liu2020machinelearningassistedlaser pages 5-9).

Chepiga et al. (2023; DOI: 10.3390/ma16031050) applied multi-objective Bayesian optimization using the DGEMO algorithm for 316L, employing a GP surrogate model trained on 57 empirical data points. The method achieved three Pareto front points in only six iterations, producing parts with hardness 224–235 HV and porosity 0.2–0.37% (chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 7-9, chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 1-2, chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 2-4, chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 9-10). This represents a practical batch BO approach where samples are printed according to DGEMO-proposed parameters and experimentally validated between iterations (chepiga2023processparameterselection pages 2-4).

### 3.2 Surrogate Models and Uncertainty-Aware Design

Kizhakkinan et al. (2023; DOI: 10.1007/s10845-022-02038-4) developed a tensor-train and GPR surrogate model for LPBF that achieves ~10⁴ computational speedup over high-fidelity powder-scale simulations, predicting thermal history and melt pool geometry in under one second (kizhakkinan2023developmentofa pages 1-2). Karkaria et al. (2024; DOI: 10.1016/j.jmsy.2024.04.023) developed BOTSPO—Bayesian Optimization for Time Series Process Optimization—using a Bayesian LSTM surrogate model with Monte Carlo dropout for uncertainty quantification, demonstrating uncertainty-aware sequential design for laser power profile optimization (karkaria2024towardsadigital pages 9-10).

Liu et al. (2023; DOI: 10.1007/s10845-022-02012-0) provided a comprehensive review of ML techniques for LB-PBF covering supervised, ensemble, and deep learning methods applied to process-property relationships across multiple materials and objectives (liu2023areviewof pages 16-18).

### 3.3 Printability-Map-Seeded Optimization

The combination of physics-based printability maps with data-driven optimization represents the state of the art. Gobert (2023) demonstrated using process maps derived from single-track boundary criteria as priors for Bayesian optimization of density cubes, effectively constraining the experimental search space to avoid keyholing, lack-of-fusion, and balling regimes (gobert2023processdevelopmentin pages 46-49). This is directly analogous to the FLAIME approach of using a Thermo-Calc AM Module-derived printability map as a Bayesian prior.

## 4. CALPHAD-Based Printability Maps and Analytical Models

### 4.1 Automated Printability Map Frameworks

Sheikh et al. (2024; DOI: 10.1038/s41524-024-01436-x) presented the most comprehensive automated computational framework for constructing printability maps. Their approach uses CALPHAD-based databases to estimate thermophysical material properties from nominal chemistry, then feeds these into Eagar-Tsai (E-T) thermal models to predict melt pool geometry. When keyhole mode is predicted (where E-T underestimates depth), a Gladush-Smurov correction is applied. Balling and lack-of-fusion criteria are evaluated to demarcate defect regions in power-velocity space (sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 14-15, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 1-2, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 2-3, sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 3-3). Critically, this framework is composition-agnostic and benchmarked against data for 316 SS, Inconel 718, Ti-6Al-4V, AF96, and NiTi alloys.

### 4.2 Bayesian Eagar-Tsai Models

Whalen et al. (2021; DOI: 10.1007/s40192-021-00238-z) introduced Bayesian inference for calibrating the Eagar-Tsai model, determining probability distributions for apparent absorptivity and powder bed porosity as functions of laser power using MCMC sampling. Their Bayesian E-T model correctly predicts keyhole regions—which the standard E-T model with constant parameters fails to capture—and generates printability maps demarcating keyhole, conduction, lack-of-fusion, and balling regions for 316L (whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 1-4, whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 16-19, whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 12-16, whalen2021abayesianapproach pages 19-22). The uncertainty quantification inherent in this Bayesian approach makes it particularly suitable as a prior for downstream BO campaigns. Sheikh et al. (2024) noted that while Bayesian inference improves predictions, applying it across large alloy spaces becomes computationally expensive, creating bottlenecks for high-throughput design (sheikh2024anautomatedcomputational pages 2-3).

### 4.3 Thermo-Calc Scheil and Solidification Modeling

Chechik et al. (2023; DOI: 10.1007/s11661-023-07029-5) demonstrated the use of Thermo-Calc Scheil solidification simulations with options for back diffusion and solute trapping to calculate solidification cracking susceptibility in nickel superalloys for AM. They emphasized that multiple susceptibility measures should be combined rather than relying on a single criterion, and that no standardized printability metric yet exists (chechik2023toolsforthe pages 19-21, chechik2023toolsforthe pages 4-6, chechik2023toolsforthe pages 16-19, chechik2023toolsforthe pages 6-9). Sjöström et al. (2022; DOI: 10.3389/fmats.2022.797226) demonstrated linkage of Simufact Additive macroscale thermal simulations with Thermo-Calc microscale models (Scheil, DICTRA) for predicting elemental segregation, martensite start temperatures, and phase fractions as a function of process parameters.

### 4.4 Defect Maps as Priors

Psihoyos & Lampeas (2023; DOI: 10.3390/alloys2010004) developed a modeling framework combining finite-element single-scan thermal models with literature-based geometric criteria for LOF, keyhole, and balling defects to construct printability maps for 316L and Ti-6Al-4V, validating against experimental data (psihoyos2023densitybasedoptimizationof pages 2-3, psihoyos2023densitybasedoptimizationof pages 20-21). The use of such maps as informative priors for Bayesian optimization is the approach most consistent with the FLAIME workflow.

## 5. LLM and Agentic Systems for AM

### 5.1 Current State

This is an emerging and rapidly evolving area with limited published peer-reviewed literature specifically addressing LPBF process-parameter optimization. Chandrasekhar et al. (2024; DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2406.00031) introduced AMGPT, a specialized LLM for metal AM built on Llama2-7B using Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) over ~50 AM papers and textbooks. AMGPT assists researchers in navigating AM literature and generating domain-specific responses, but does not autonomously select process parameters or interface with experimental hardware (chandrasekhar2024amgptalarge pages 4-8, chandrasekhar2024amgptalarge pages 1-4).

Fan et al. (2025; DOI: 10.1088/2631-7990/ada8e4) proposed a hierarchical framework for autonomous AM with four layers: knowledge, generative solution, operational, and cognitive. The cognitive layer employs AI agents with planning, acting, and reflecting capabilities, representing the most advanced conceptual framework for agentic AM (fan2025neweratowards pages 40-41). However, this remains a framework rather than a validated implementation.

### 5.2 Agentic and Tool-Calling Systems

No published peer-reviewed work was found that combines agentic LLM tool-calling (e.g., MCP-style interfaces) with LPBF parameter optimization, defect prediction, or SEM image analysis in a unified system. The broader materials science community has begun exploring agentic AI—Yao et al. (2025; DOI: 10.20517/aiagent.2025.03) traced the evolution from LLMs as knowledge assistants to autonomous agents with reasoning, planning, and tool-use capabilities for materials discovery. Dobrovsky et al. (2025; DOI: 10.31399/asm.cp.istfa2025p0041) demonstrated LLM-based planning agents for semiconductor failure analysis that integrate external tool utilization and autonomous processing. These provide architectural precedents but have not been applied to AM process parameter selection.

**Evidence strength assessment:** The claim that agentic LLM systems can meaningfully assist LPBF parameter selection is **weakly supported** by current literature—it is plausible based on analogous demonstrations in adjacent fields but essentially unvalidated for AM. The FLAIME project's integration of an LLM-agent layer with Thermo-Calc priors and Bayesian optimization would therefore represent a genuinely novel contribution.

## Implications for the FLAIME Project

The published literature strongly supports the individual components of the proposed FLAIME workflow:

1. **Thermo-Calc → printability map prior:** The Sheikh et al. (2024) framework demonstrates that CALPHAD-derived printability maps can serve as high-throughput, composition-agnostic priors. For novel aluminum compositions, this allows pre-experimental identification of keyholing/LOF/balling boundaries without any experimental data.

2. **Bayesian optimization with ~20 runs per powder:** The Chepiga et al. (2023) demonstration of Pareto-optimal 316L conditions in six iterations, and the Liu et al. (2020) identification of a larger AlSi10Mg window via GPR, establish that GP-based BO can achieve useful convergence within the planned experimental budget.

3. **Cross-machine transferability study:** Reijonen et al. (2021) quantify the transfer gap (~7% tensile, ~20% impact toughness) but no published study has used a systematic BO + prior framework to characterize and reduce this gap across same-model instances or across vendors. This is a clear gap the project can fill.

4. **LLM-agent layer:** The integration of an LLM agent with tool-calling capabilities for AM parameter selection is essentially unpublished and would represent a first-of-its-kind contribution.

5. **Open-architecture Aconity MIDI:** Several recent papers use the Aconity MIDI+ for research (e.g., Cacace et al. 2025; Tosoratti et al. 2025), confirming its suitability as a flexible research platform, though no published systematic parameter optimization study using the specific Thermo-Calc + BO + LLM stack was found.
