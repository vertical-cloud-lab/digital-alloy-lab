## Query 1: State of the Art in Slicing and Build-Preparation Software for Metal AM

*Edison Scientific LITERATURE query (task a11a3070-111c-4cbc-9972-7ed129843ccb)*

Question: What is the current state of the art (2022-2026) in slicing and build-preparation software for metal additive manufacturing (AM), particularly laser powder bed fusion (LPBF)?

Cover the following:
(1) Major commercial slicing platforms (e.g., Materialise Magics, Autodesk Netfabb, Siemens NX AM, 3DXpert/Oqton, Amphyon/Additive Works, nTopology, Ansys Additive Suite, MSC Simufact) - their capabilities, strengths, and limitations
(2) Open-source slicing alternatives (e.g., Slic3r, PrusaSlicer, CuraEngine derivatives adapted for metal AM, OpenSCAD-based toolpaths)
(3) Advanced slicing strategies: non-planar slicing, variable layer thickness, multi-laser coordination, island/checkerboard scanning, contour-hatch strategies
(4) Process simulation integration in slicers: thermal distortion prediction, residual stress compensation, support structure optimization
(5) AI/ML-enhanced slicing: machine learning for scan strategy optimization, generative design integration, topology-optimized support generation
(6) Alloy-specific slicing considerations: how do different alloy systems (Al, Ti, Ni, steel, Cu) require different scanning strategies?

## Scope and framing (2022–2026)
LPBF “slicing/build preparation” has expanded from geometry-to-layers conversion into an integrated digital process-planning stack: (i) geometry conditioning (repair, part segmentation, feature recognition), (ii) build orientation + nesting, (iii) support generation and optimization, (iv) scan-path generation (contours/hatch and variants), (v) part-scale simulation for distortion/residual stress and (increasingly) pre-compensation, plus (vi) emerging AI/ML layers for scan-path planning, parameter optimization, and digital-twin feedback. Contemporary reviews emphasize that *no single software platform covers the full multi-scale physics stack* (powder spreading, laser–powder interaction, melt-pool CFD, microstructure, part-scale thermomechanics), so industrial workflows often chain multiple tools via scripts/APIs, creating interoperability and validation burdens (lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50).

## (1) Major commercial slicing/build-prep platforms: capabilities, strengths, limitations
The literature accessible here provides the clearest evidence for *build-prep suites that explicitly combine supports + simulation* rather than for all UI-level slicing functions of each brand.

### Materialise Magics (and e-Stage)
**Capabilities/strengths.** Magics is repeatedly cited as a central build-prep environment for metal AM, with explicit evidence of (a) part orientation setting and (b) extensive support libraries and workflows: block, line, point, web, contour, gusset, combi, volume, cones, with both automatic and manual generation and adjustable support parameters (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3). Materialise **e-Stage** is described as a *fully automatic* support-generation tool, positioned to reduce preparation time, powder consumption, and post-processing (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3).
**Limitations.** A recurring limitation in the support-design ecosystem is that supports are frequently still positioned by geometric heuristics and predefined patterns rather than being optimized directly from thermal-stress fields; support design remains iterative and expert-driven (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3).

### Autodesk Netfabb / Fusion 360 + Netfabb
**Capabilities/strengths.** Netfabb (often paired with Fusion 360) is described as providing support generation, model repair, printability analysis, and process simulation that calculates residual stress and part distortion (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3). In an LPBF workflow description, Netfabb is also characterized as covering orienting, slicing, contour generation, and prediction steps (perino2024design3dprinting pages 1-6). Netfabb appears in recent ML/RL scan-planning work as a validation engine (“Netfabb™ Local Simulation”) coupled to experiments (dou2026reinforcedscana pages 1-2).
**Limitations.** Reduced-order simulation assumptions are common across commercial FE-based solutions; for example, small-deformation-type assumptions are often used and known failure modes (support collapse, part separation) can violate them (kobir2023predictionofrecoater pages 19-23). In addition, the broader ecosystem limitation remains: no single environment is sufficient for full multiscale physics without tool chaining (lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50).

### ANSYS Additive Print / Additive Suite
**Capabilities/strengths.** ANSYS Additive is described as providing build-prep and simulation functions including distortion/residual stress/thermal deformation prediction, automatic support generation, and scanning-pattern optimization; it has been coupled with ML to predict melt-pool geometry and microstructure and reduce simulation time (lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50). A part-scale NiTi study explicitly used **Ansys Additive Print** with an inherent-strain approach because full 3D thermomechanical FE was computationally prohibitive (monu2023bidirectionalscanpattern pages 1-2).
**Limitations.** The same NiTi study explicitly motivates inherent-strain/reduced-order modeling due to computational constraints and material-property requirements for full thermomechanical FE (monu2023bidirectionalscanpattern pages 1-2). More generally, toolchain fragmentation persists (lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50).

### MSC Simufact Additive
**Capabilities/strengths.** Simufact Additive is directly evidenced as being used for LPBF distortion prediction of Ti-6Al-4V components (ninpetch2022numericalmodelingof pages 1-2). In that study, support design was a strong driver of distortion: switching from lattice supports to block supports reduced maximum distortion by up to **44%** (ninpetch2022numericalmodelingof pages 1-2). Reviews also attribute to Simufact Additive: part-orientation optimization, minimizing support volume/build risk, and residual stress/distortion simulation with weighted-criteria optimization (lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50).
**Limitations.** As with other part-scale commercial tools, Simufact Additive’s common application is via inherent-strain or reduced-order approaches rather than fully resolved scan-vector melt-pool physics (ninpetch2022numericalmodelingof pages 1-2, lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50).

### Amphyon (Additive Works / Oqton)
**Capabilities/strengths.** Amphyon is identified as a simulation-based process preparation tool offering thermal/structural simulations to calculate residual stress and distortion and improve thermal stability (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3). A 2025 validation in Ti6Al4V provides unusually concrete quantitative evidence: Amphyon predicted distortions with a maximum deviation of about **14%** vs experiment, and its pre-compensation workflow reduced deformation by **>70%** (giubilini2025assessmentofsimulation pages 1-3, giubilini2025assessmentofsimulation pages 10-13).
**Limitations.** The same validation notes that mismatch arises from missing microscale physics such as melt-pool dynamics and powder behavior (giubilini2025assessmentofsimulation pages 10-13).

### 3DXpert / Oqton
**Capabilities/strengths.** 3DXpert is explicitly grouped with Amphyon as a simulation-based process-preparation tool, offering topology optimization plus thermal/structural simulation to compute residual stress and distortion (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3).
**Limitations.** In the accessible corpus, detailed evidence about its scan-path feature set (e.g., multi-laser scheduling, contour/hatch variants) is not explicitly described beyond this simulation/prep positioning (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3).

### AM-Studio (Hexagon / CADS Additive), 4D_Additive, VoxelDance Additive
These are evidenced as widely used build-prep environments with overlapping core features: orientation definition, file repair, support generation, and (for some) process simulation for residual stress/distortion (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3). AM-Studio is specifically noted to include nontraditional supports such as **tree** and **AdaptiveCell** (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3). The main limitation in the accessible texts is lack of detailed, product-specific documentation of scan-path algorithms and simulation/compensation workflows beyond high-level capability lists (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3).

### Siemens NX Additive Manufacturing
Direct evidence in the retrieved corpus is case-study based rather than feature-list based: Siemens NX is explicitly used for **orientation optimization** in an LPBF aerospace bracket study (perino2024design3dprinting pages 1-6). The accessible texts do not provide detailed descriptions of NX’s dedicated slicing or scan-strategy authoring functions.

### nTopology
In the accessible literature, nTopology is evidenced primarily as an implicit-design/lattice design environment supporting metal AM design workflows (e.g., optimizing lattice designs for LPBF porous blanks) (perino2024design3dprinting pages 1-6). In this corpus it functions as a DfAM front-end rather than a slicer.

### Bottom line for commercial state-of-the-art
Across the evidence, the distinguishing frontier (2022–2026) is *simulation-informed build preparation* (distortion/residual stress prediction and pre-compensation) plus richer support-generation automation; scan-path optimization is increasingly discussed, but its vendor-specific implementations are often not transparent in publications (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3, lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50).

## (2) Open-source and research slicing alternatives (metal LPBF)
Open-source “slicers” for LPBF are less mature than polymer FFF slicers, but 2022–2026 literature shows active, practical research toolchains that reuse or extend open-source components.

### Research-driven G-code/build-file tailoring for LPBF
A key driver is that *commercial LPBF slicers are proprietary and machine-tied*, limiting access to path generation and parameter control even though slicing directly affects porosity and residual stress (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 1-2). To bypass these constraints, researchers: 
* generate scan vectors via custom conversion pipelines (e.g., geometry → image → scan vectors), or 
* programmatically modify build files to change parameters near overhangs/thin features (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 1-2).
Englert et al. explicitly report developing an **open-source tool to tailor LPBF G-code** to geometric features and advanced paths (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 1-2).

### Repurposing Cura (open-source FFF slicer) for LPBF scan-path experiments
Englert et al. describe a concrete two-step method: **Cura 4.7.1** slices STL into RepRap G-code, then the commands are reinterpreted into LPBF machine code. For example, nozzle temperature fields are repurposed to encode laser power, and feed rates are mapped to scan speed; extrusion-related commands are replaced by laser on/off toggles (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 2-4). This enables rapid experimentation with scan patterns and descriptive-statistics analysis of toolpaths, but requires machine-specific translation and careful disabling of FFF-specific behaviors (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 2-4).

### PySLM and mesh-processing toolchains
PySLM is explicitly cited as an open-source LPBF-focused library used in LPBF support-generation/optimization tooling ecosystems; Trimesh is cited as a mesh-processing dependency (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 14-14). PySLM is also used as a build-file interface (e.g., extracting timestamps from machine build files) in monitoring/modeling workflows (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3).

### Open-source multiphysics components (OpenFOAM)
OpenFOAM is cited as an open-source CFD tool adapted for melt-pool physics (including Marangoni effects) and validated against experiments, supporting the broader open toolchain approach for PBF modeling (lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50). However, it is not a slicer; it is typically a component in chained workflows.

### ORNL Slicer 2 (open-source, DOE national lab)
[ORNL Slicer 2](https://github.com/ORNLSlicer/Slicer-2) is an open-source (GPL-3.0) advanced slicing and toolpath-planning tool developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s Manufacturing Demonstration Facility (MDF). It was not captured by the Edison queries (which focused on LPBF literature), but is directly relevant to the FLAIME workflow for several reasons:
* **Site-specific / region-specific control:** Slicer 2 supports “settings meshes” that allow parameters (bead width, infill density, speed, temperature) to vary within specific zones of a part — the same paradigm needed for combinatorial multi-parameter builds.
* **500+ configurable parameters** with per-part, per-layer, and per-region granularity.
* **Real-time sensor feedback** integration for closed-loop process control.
* **Multi-process support:** 30+ printer types including blown-powder DED, laser wire DED, MIG welding, and large-format polymer — though its primary focus is large-format AM (LFAM) rather than LPBF specifically.
* **ORNL MDF partnership:** Since ORNL MDF is already identified as a key FLAIME collaborator (see [genesis-mission §MDF Software Tools](https://github.com/vertical-cloud-lab/digital-alloy-lab-private/blob/genesis-mission/genesis-mission-topic12-focus-area-f.md#mdf-software-tools-proprietary--licensable)), Slicer 2’s architecture for region-wise parameter assignment and adaptive path planning could inform or be extended for LPBF combinatorial workflows.

**Limitation for LPBF:** Slicer 2 is primarily designed for LFAM/DED processes, not powder-bed fusion. LPBF-specific scan strategies (island/checkerboard, interlayer rotation, contour/hatch separation) are not its current focus. For LPBF, the OEM build processor remains the primary slicer, with PySLM and custom build-file scripts layered on top (see §5 of Query 2).

### Gaps vs the user’s named open-source slicers
Within the retrieved evidence, there is *no explicit, citable documentation* of Slic3r/PrusaSlicer/OpenSCAD being used as primary LPBF slicers in 2022–2026 metal AM; the LPBF-oriented open ecosystem in these sources is instead dominated by Cura repurposing, PySLM, and bespoke build-file/G-code manipulation (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 2-4, dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 14-14).

## (3) Advanced slicing and scan strategies (LPBF)
### Island/checkerboard vs stripe and other segmentation strategies
Recent work continues to position **stripe** and **chessboard/island** strategies as the dominant baselines, with motivations centered on thermal management (residual stress, distortion, defects) (dou2026reinforcedscana pages 1-2). Englert et al. note that scan-vector endpoints are susceptible to keyhole pore formation and that alternative patterns can reduce laser on/off events and jump distances, but may introduce geometry-dependent defect architectures and increased porosity (example overall porosity ~0.42% in one examined geometry) (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 1-2).

### Concentric/continuous, spiral, perimeter variants and contour–hatch interactions
Englert et al. compare scan strategies (hatching, spiral, perimeter, perimeter fill) and link toolpath geometry (acute kinks) to pore formation patterns using µCT (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 12-14). They propose a defect-mitigation modification: *staggering perimeter lines across layers by reducing the first line distance to 75 µm in the second layer*, placing scan lines between those of the previous layer to avoid lack-of-fusion pores (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 12-14). Visual evidence for these toolpaths and µCT pore distributions is shown in the extracted figures (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies media 9bfd1dcb, englert2022concentricscanningstrategies media 405862b9, englert2022concentricscanningstrategies media cb79294d).

### Variable layer thickness / changing layer heights
A systematic review of productivity improvement in metal PBF highlights “changing layer heights” as one of the most quantitatively impactful levers (10%–252% productivity improvements reported across studies), although cross-study comparability is limited (mcconnell2024productivityimprovementopportunities pages 1-2). (Detailed, LPBF-specific adaptive-slicing implementations were not fully accessible in this corpus.)

### Non-planar slicing
Non-planar slicing is active in multi-axis AM research, but the retrieved evidence is dominated by extrusion/robotic contexts rather than LPBF; LPBF-specific non-planar slicing is therefore not established as a mainstream 2022–2026 industrial practice in the available corpus.

### Multi-laser coordination
Multi-laser coordination is a known industrial direction, but explicit toolpath-scheduling algorithms at the “slicer” level were not captured in the accessible evidence here; the closest retrieved evidence focuses on scan-path optimization and on the need to improve productivity without hardware changes (mcconnell2024productivityimprovementopportunities pages 1-2).

## (4) Process simulation integration in build-prep
The dominant 2022–2026 trend is *part-scale simulation integrated into build prep* for RFT (“right first time”) manufacturing.

### Distortion/residual-stress prediction and why reduced-order models dominate
The NiTi study using Ansys Additive Print explicitly adopts an inherent-strain method because full 3D thermomechanical FE is constrained by prohibitive material-property needs and computational cost (monu2023bidirectionalscanpattern pages 1-2). This rationale aligns with broader reviews emphasizing that no single tool covers all scales/physics, forcing tool-chaining and reduced-order approximations (lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50).

### Pre-compensation workflows
Amphyon’s compensated-geometry workflow provides a clear example of simulation-to-geometry feedback: it generates a pre-distorted geometry intended to print into the desired nominal shape, achieving >70% deformation reduction in a Ti6Al4V validation case (giubilini2025assessmentofsimulation pages 10-13).

### Support optimization in simulation-aware workflows
Commercial tool ecosystems provide many support archetypes and automation (e.g., Magics support libraries and e-Stage automation), while research literature notes that many tools are still not placing supports directly based on thermal stress fields, leaving support optimization iterative (dimopoulos2024aninteractivewebbased pages 2-3). Simulation studies using Simufact Additive show that support design selection strongly affects distortion (e.g., block supports reducing distortion vs lattice supports) (ninpetch2022numericalmodelingof pages 1-2).

## (5) AI/ML-enhanced slicing and build-prep
### ML/RL scan-strategy optimization
A 2026 paper proposes reinforcement-learning-enabled scan sequencing (“Reinforced Scan”) to improve thermal uniformity; importantly, it is **validated using Netfabb™ Local Simulation and real Ti-6Al-4V laser scanning experiments**, showing a concrete pathway by which AI planners can sit on top of commercial simulation toolchains (dou2026reinforcedscana pages 1-2).

### AI/ML embedded in commercial ecosystems and digital twins
A 2026 modeling review states that **ANSYS Additive** has been coupled with ML to predict melt-pool geometry and microstructure, reducing simulation time, and that commercial offerings include automatic support generation and scanning-pattern optimization (lach2026advancednumericalmodeling pages 49-50). More broadly, AI reviews connect ML to (i) parameter optimization, (ii) in-situ monitoring/defect classification, and (iii) medium-term digital-twin architectures that combine physics + ML continuously updated by sensor streams; they also highlight practical deployment barriers (closed machine interfaces, sensor heterogeneity, certification-grade uncertainty quantification) (sustacha2026artificialintelligencein pages 18-19, sustacha2026artificialintelligencein pages 17-18).

### Generative design and topology-optimized supports
Across reviews, generative design/topology optimization is treated as a key AI-enabled DfAM trend, but is frequently characterized as still research-heavy when manufacturability constraints must be enforced (sustacha2026artificialintelligencein pages 18-19, saimon2026advancingadditivemanufacturing pages 1-4). In practical workflows, lattice/generative geometry design via nTopology appears upstream of build prep rather than inside the slicer itself in the accessible evidence (perino2024design3dprinting pages 1-6).

## (6) Alloy-specific slicing/scan considerations
The evidence supports the general principle that scan-path decisions are alloy- and application-dependent because scan strategy affects thermal history, residual stress, texture, and functional response (monu2023bidirectionalscanpattern pages 1-2, andani2025exploringtheimpact pages 1-2).

### Titanium alloys (Ti-6Al-4V)
Ti-6Al-4V appears prominently in simulation calibration and distortion-compensation workflows (Simufact Additive distortion modeling; Amphyon validation and pre-compensation) (ninpetch2022numericalmodelingof pages 1-2, giubilini2025assessmentofsimulation pages 10-13). In practice this translates to build-prep emphasis on distortion prediction/compensation, careful support strategy, and scan-sequence choices that moderate thermal gradients.

### NiTi (shape memory alloys)
NiTi shows strong scan-strategy sensitivity at fixed energy input: island and hexagonal strategies promoted equiaxed grains and lower residual stresses, while a 90° rotation strategy produced strong <001> texture and superior recoverable strain (~6%) with minimal plasticity (andani2025exploringtheimpact pages 1-2). This is a direct example where build-prep scan planning is used to tailor *functional properties* (superelasticity) rather than only geometric accuracy.

### Nickel alloys (e.g., IN718/IN625) and steels
These appear in the scan-strategy and modeling context (e.g., discussions of scan patterns and simulation/digital-twin studies including Inconel 718), supporting the view that high thermal gradients and cracking/defect sensitivity motivate scan-path engineering and simulation-informed planning (englert2022concentricscanningstrategies pages 1-2, sustacha2026artificialintelligencein pages 17-18).

### Aluminum and copper alloys (AlSi10Mg, Cu/CuCrZr)
Within the retrieved corpus, direct alloy-specific scan-strategy prescriptions for Al and Cu (beyond general energy-density/process-window logic) were not captured at the level needed for strong citations. Consequently, any detailed, alloy-by-alloy scanning “rules” for Al/Cu would require additional targeted retrieval beyond this evidence set.

## 1) Slicing in high-throughput/combinatorial LPBF for multiple compositions on one plate

### 1.1 Multi-parameter “combinatorial parts” (same composition, many parameter sets)
A common high-throughput approach is to design a single “combinatorial” specimen whose cross-section is partitioned into many discrete sub-regions (“columns”/“tiles”), each assigned a different parameter set. Weaver et al. printed a single contiguous sample containing 24 small rectangular columns (each 4 mm × 3 mm), where 20 distinct laser power–scan speed combinations were mapped to columns, while a surrounding shell/base were printed with default settings and a fixed print order (shell first, then columns). They placed repeats of the default condition diagonally and randomized the remaining assignments to detect spatial (thermal-field) dependence, and treated the experiment as a split-plot design because the unit is contiguous and processed together (including common stress-relief when used). This geometry–parameter “tiling” is fundamentally a slicing/build-file task: the build processor must map parameter IDs to geometric regions rather than to whole parts. (weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 5-10, weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 1-5)

### 1.2 Multi-composition builds (composition libraries and gradients)
For composition screening, high-throughput AM reviews describe building multiple compositions in one build by controlling powder feedstock spatially (zoned powder spreading/gradient spreading) or temporally (multi-hopper systems). A representative workflow is: “each powder type is printed for a set number of layers, with changes in material composition achieved by switching hoppers,” enabling many compositions in one specimen; alternatively, gradient powder spreading can create composition variation across the build area (horizontal gradients), requiring consistent spreading/mixing per layer to maintain intended ratios. These approaches shift the slicing challenge from “parameter IDs per tile” to “composition state + parameter set per tile/layer segment,” where the build plan must coordinate: (i) geometric zoning, (ii) powder-change schedules, and (iii) local parameter adjustments for each composition. (liu2024high‐throughputpreparationfor pages 12-12, rabbitt2025rethinkingbiomedicaltitanium pages 16-17)

## 2) Scan strategy parameters that most affect printability of new alloys, and how slicers parameterize them

### 2.1 Dominant knobs for printability (density/defects) in new alloys
Across sources, the most influential variables for printability are the canonical four: laser power P, scan speed v, hatch spacing h (or scan spacing/overlap), and layer thickness t. These define the volumetric energy input and melt-pool geometry, which governs defect regimes: insufficient energy yields lack-of-fusion/balling, while excessive energy yields evaporation/keyholing and spherical porosity. (cobbinah2020laserpowderbed pages 5-7, khaimovich2022optimizationofprocess pages 1-2)

Practical ranges and actionable “slicer-level” quantities include:
- Hatch distance ~50–150 µm, with larger hatch increasing productivity but raising lack-of-fusion risk due to reduced remelt fraction. (barber2025laserpowderbed pages 52-56)
- Layer thickness ~20–100 µm (noting nominal vs actual can differ), trading productivity against surface quality and fusion robustness. (barber2025laserpowderbed pages 52-56)
- Track spacing/overlap: recommended overlap of ~25–30% has been reported to improve density; dense/smooth conditions are associated with scan spacing thresholds (<~0.12–0.2 mm, depending on context) to maintain adequate overlap and bonding. (cobbinah2020laserpowderbed pages 7-10)

### 2.2 Strategy parameters beyond energy density (critical for “unknown” alloys)
Novel alloys often fail by cracking or anisotropy even when density is high, making scan-path topology and rotation unusually important:
- Scan pattern choice: meander/raster, stripes (striped partition), and island/chessboard (tiled squares) are common. (barber2025laserpowderbed pages 52-56)
- Interlayer rotation angle: widely used to avoid repetitive thermal histories and strong texture; industry-favored ~67° is explicitly noted as suppressing strongly textured columnar grains, while 45° or 90° can produce more repetitive patterns. (barber2025laserpowderbed pages 56-59)
- Island/stripe size and scan-vector length: smaller vectors can reduce long-range residual stress accumulation but introduce borders/junctions where lack-of-fusion or cracking can localize. (yadav2022microstructuralandmechanical pages 1-5, zhang2023additivemanufacturingof pages 11-13, cobbinah2020laserpowderbed pages 7-10)

### 2.3 How modern slicers/build processors represent these parameters
In practice, commercial LPBF build preparation exposes these as “parameter sets” assigned to regions (core/hatch, contour, downskin/upskin). Sources explicitly describe parameterization in terms of: laser power, scan speed, hatch distance/scan spacing, layer thickness, scan strategy (meander/stripe/island), island/stripe segmentation, and interlayer rotation angle. For pulsed systems, scan speed can be implemented via point distance/exposure time/delay, which also becomes a slicer/build-file parameterization issue when doing region-wise parameter sweeps. (barber2025laserpowderbed pages 52-56, clement2024investigationintothe pages 43-51)

## 3) DOE approaches integrated with slicing/build preparation for LPBF parameter optimization

### 3.1 Split-plot and tiled-region DOE in a single build
Combinatorial parts naturally implement DOE “in the build file” by assigning factor levels to tiles/columns. Weaver et al. use a split-plot design because the contiguous combinatorial specimen is processed together, include repeated control conditions for spatial effects, and use multi-level factors to capture non-linear responses. (weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 5-10)

### 3.2 Model-driven automated vetting + scripted DOE seeding
Two closely related process-development frameworks use automated, physics-based filtering of candidate P–v windows before committing to physical DOE. They discretize the P–v domain, compute melt-pool dimensions via fast Rosenthal solutions or higher-fidelity ANSYS models, and automatically vet candidate points against defect criteria for lack-of-fusion, balling, and keyholing using Python scripting. After down-selecting a stable regime, they seed a physical DOE with coded factor levels (e.g., laser power, scan speed, hatch spacing) to fit regression models for outputs (surface roughness, dimensional accuracy, porosity). This is a “DOE + automation” pattern where slicing can be driven programmatically from the vetted parameter table. (flam2022laserpowderbed pages 34-42, graybill2022frameworktoaccelerate pages 30-37)

### 3.3 Physics-informed build-file rewriting (feature-aware feed-forward control)
Druzgalski et al. describe a software framework that reads build files, performs feature extraction on the scan path to identify regions at high risk of defect formation, and then uses a library of validated physics-model results to select and apply optimized parameters along the scan trajectory, generating optimized machine-agnostic build files. This is essentially DOE/optimization coupled directly to build-file generation rather than to “static” parameter sets. (druzgalski2020processoptimizationof pages 1-2)

### 3.4 Bayesian optimization for automated tuning during LPBF
A more “adaptive” paradigm is Bayesian Optimization (BO) applied to in-layer controller autotuning, exploiting layer-to-layer repetition. This provides a route for automated parameter tuning that can be integrated with scanning instruction generation (e.g., modulating laser power to avoid overheating), with the caution that BO-tuned controllers can also induce lack-of-fusion if they undershoot energy. (kavas2025insitucontrollerautotuning pages 1-3)

## 4) Scan strategy effects on microstructure, texture, and cracking in novel alloys

### 4.1 Texture and grain morphology control through scan rotation and path topology
Across alloy systems (Ti-6Al-4V, IN718/IN738LC, tungsten systems, HEAs), rotation strategy and scan pattern shift texture class and grain morphology. Reported examples include:
- Unidirectional vs bidirectional scanning changes residual-stress anisotropy, and bidirectional patterns can reduce residual stress and make it more uniform. (li2024advancesinlaser pages 12-13, li2024advancesinlaser pages 10-12)
- Interlayer rotation schemes (90°, ±45°, 67°) produce different textures (e.g., cubic vs fibrous) and can reduce porosity and anisotropy. (li2024advancesinlaser pages 12-13)
- In an interstitial-strengthened HEA, stripe scanning produced a strong <001> texture aligned with scan direction and increased mechanical anisotropy, while chessboard reduced anisotropy but introduced more border-related lack-of-fusion pores and microcracks. (zhang2023additivemanufacturingof pages 11-13)

### 4.2 Cracking susceptibility: trade-offs of islands and borders
Island/chessboard strategies can lower residual stress by shortening scan vectors, but multiple sources warn that borders/junctions create thermal or fusion discontinuities:
- In the HEA study, lack-of-fusion pores often formed at square-domain borders and acted as crack-initiation sites. (zhang2023additivemanufacturingof pages 11-13)
- Superalloy-focused reviews report island strategies can reduce residual stress but also cause “band-heating” that leads to cracks. (cobbinah2020laserpowderbed pages 7-10)
- For AlSi7Mg0.6, stripes achieved higher densification than chessboard (less lack-of-fusion at island junctions) and chessboard failure was associated with elongated porosity at junctions; rotation between layers lowered texture intensity. (yadav2022microstructuralandmechanical pages 26-30)

### 4.3 Solidification conditions (G/R) and microstructure refinement
Scan vector length and strategy alter heat flow and cooling rates, changing the effective thermal gradient (G) and solidification rate (R), thereby influencing columnar-to-equiaxed transition and texture strength. Reviews and experiments emphasize that smaller scan vectors and layer rotation can reduce residual stress and texture strength, which is often critical for crack-prone novel alloys. (yadav2022microstructuralandmechanical pages 1-5, zhang2023additivemanufacturingof pages 1-3)

## 5) Open-source or scriptable slicing/toolpath tools for research workflows

Direct open-source LPBF slicers/build-processors are less commonly documented than for polymer extrusion in the retrieved sources, but several *scriptable* pathways exist that researchers use to gain programmatic toolpath control:

### 5.1 Standards-based, programmable process-description formats (STEP / STEP-NC)
A standards route to programmatic build preparation is to represent geometry and process parameters using STEP infrastructure: Part 21 (human-readable clear-text files), SDAI APIs for programmatic access, and EXPRESS schema/code generation. AP242 is highlighted as having potential to represent AM data including process parameters. STEP-NC (ISO 10303-238) is positioned as an alternative to STL/G-code for richer, per-layer representation of operations and parameters, enabling more programmatic control and interoperability. (rodriguez2025stepncinadditive pages 7-9, rodriguez2025stepncinadditive pages 1-3)

### 5.2 Scripted in-house slicers and custom toolpath generation
An open-source preprocessing tool implemented in Scilab demonstrates end-to-end scripted slicing: reading STL, applying rotations, computing plane–triangle intersections for slicing, generating contours/trajectories, and exporting machine code (G-code-like) with selectable raster/zigzag/contour/hybrid strategies and per-layer angle shifts. Although demonstrated for WAAM, the core idea (programmatic slicing + explicit trajectory matrices + per-layer strategy parameters) transfers directly to LPBF research where the “machine code” is a laser scan-vector build file rather than extrusion G-code. (ferreira2022developmentandimplementation pages 1-2, ferreira2022developmentandimplementation pages 4-7)

### 5.3 Modifying existing open-source engines and using intermediate exports
The same WAAM preprocessing paper notes that CuraEngine (open-source) has been modified for metal deposition (e.g., blocking path crossing, adding on/off commands, variable idle time) and that Netfabb can export a common layer interface (CLI) format that can be consumed by in-house code for trajectory generation and analysis. These patterns are directly relevant to LPBF workflows where researchers often parse/export intermediate slice/scan-vector formats to enable automated experimentation. (ferreira2022developmentandimplementation pages 2-4)

### 5.4 ORNL Slicer 2 (open-source, ORNL MDF)
[ORNL Slicer 2](https://github.com/ORNLSlicer/Slicer-2) is an open-source (GPL-3.0) advanced slicer from ORNL’s Manufacturing Demonstration Facility, supporting 30+ printer types with 500+ configurable parameters. Its “settings meshes” paradigm — where parameters vary per-region within a single part — is architecturally analogous to the combinatorial multi-zone builds described in §1 above. While Slicer 2 currently targets large-format AM (DED, polymer, concrete) rather than LPBF, its region-specific parameter control and real-time sensor feedback integration represent a mature open-source model for the kind of “experiment compiler” slicing needed for novel-alloy LPBF workflows. Given that ORNL MDF is already a key FLAIME partner, Slicer 2’s architecture could serve as a reference or extension point for LPBF combinatorial build-file generation.


## 6) Scan strategy × composition interactions and advances in composition-aware / physics-informed slicing

### 6.1 Why scan strategy must be composition-aware in alloy development
Multiple sources provide direct or mechanistic evidence that identical scan strategies will not transfer between compositions because composition changes absorptivity, melting/solidification behavior, nucleation kinetics, segregation, and cracking risk:
- High-throughput composition-gradient studies show composition-dependent microstructure transitions (e.g., Mn additions in Al promoting equiaxed grains by reducing incubation time for nucleation), demonstrating that the same thermal history produces different grain structures depending on composition. (liu2024high‐throughputpreparationfor pages 5-5)
- Composition affects cracking susceptibility through low-melting phases and element enrichment during remelting, which can increase liquation/solidification cracking risk—meaning that scan strategies that increase remelting/heat accumulation can be beneficial for mixing but harmful for crack-prone chemistries. (liu2024high‐throughputpreparationfor pages 5-5)
- In blended powders (DSS/SDSS with 316L), achieving near-full density and altering phase balance required a much higher volumetric energy density for some blends, imposing slower solidification and greater melt-pool homogeneity to enable diffusion of austenite stabilizers. This is a direct composition–process interaction relevant to slicing/scan planning (energy input and scan strategy determine whether diffusion-limited phase formation can occur). (gargalis2023novelpowderfeedstock pages 1-2)
- Reviews emphasize that different alloys have different melting points and laser absorption rates, that process parameters are often empirical per alloy, and that a laser-parameter database across alloys is needed; however, adjustments in laser parameters for composition changes are “seldom discussed,” highlighting a gap that composition-aware slicing aims to close. (liu2024high‐throughputpreparationfor pages 13-13)
- Mechanistically, compositions with differing thermophysical properties (melting point, heat capacity, reflectivity) require enough energy to fully melt the least favorable constituent, otherwise unmelted particles and poor bonding occur; increasing energy can also reduce segregation by enhancing melt-pool mixing. (rabbitt2025rethinkingbiomedicaltitanium pages 16-17)
- Absorptivity differences are explicitly composition-dependent (e.g., Cu-based alloys low absorptivity vs Ni-based alloys higher), motivating composition-specific beam/parameter choices. (barber2025laserpowderbed pages 52-56)

### 6.2 Recent advances toward physics-informed and (proto-)composition-aware slicing
Within the retrieved evidence, the clearest “physics-informed slicing” advances are frameworks that (i) use physics-based models to propose/modify parameters, and (ii) operate at the level of scan vectors/build files:
- Feature-aware, feed-forward build-file generation that reads scan trajectories, identifies risky regions, and applies model-library-derived parameter updates to generate optimized machine-agnostic build files. This architecture is compatible with composition-aware slicing if the physics models and constraints are made alloy-dependent. (druzgalski2020processoptimizationof pages 1-2)
- Automated process-window vetting pipelines that sweep P–v space with melt-pool models, flag defect regimes, and script selection of DOE factor levels. These can be extended to composition-aware workflows by adding composition-dependent absorptivity, melting range, and crack criteria into the vetting models. (flam2022laserpowderbed pages 34-42, graybill2022frameworktoaccelerate pages 30-37)
- BO-based in-layer control autotuning is an example of adaptive parameter adjustment (here, controller parameters affecting laser power assignment) during printing; although not explicitly composition-aware, it provides a mechanism to correct for composition-dependent deviations from predicted behavior, provided constraints are included to avoid under-melting. (kavas2025insitucontrollerautotuning pages 1-3)

## Practical synthesis for novel-alloy LPBF workflows
For novel alloys, slicing/build prep is not merely geometric. It functions as an experiment compiler that must: (i) map parameter sets and/or compositions to sub-regions on a plate, (ii) encode scan strategy (islands/stripes + rotation + overlap) to manage residual stress and crack risk, (iii) support automated DOE loops and physics-based pre-vetting of candidate settings, and (iv) allow programmatic generation/rewriting of scan-vector files to implement feature-aware or adaptive (potentially composition-aware) control. The literature shows that both scan strategy and energy-input parameters interact strongly with composition via absorptivity, diffusion/phase formation, nucleation, and crack-prone solidification pathways, motivating composition-linked parameter databases and model-informed, region-wise parameter assignment in next-generation slicers. (weaver2021demonstrationofa pages 5-10, barber2025laserpowderbed pages 52-56, druzgalski2020processoptimizationof pages 1-2, kavas2025insitucontrollerautotuning pages 1-3, liu2024high‐throughputpreparationfor pages 5-5, gargalis2023novelpowderfeedstock pages 1-2, rabbitt2025rethinkingbiomedicaltitanium pages 16-17)
