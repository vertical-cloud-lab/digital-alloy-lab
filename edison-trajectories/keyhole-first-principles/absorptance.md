## Direct Measurements of Laser Absorptance During Keyhole Formation and Their Relation to Depression Geometry

### 1. Overview of the Measurement Landscape

Three complementary experimental techniques have directly measured absorptance during laser melting at 1070 nm under conditions relevant to laser powder bed fusion (LPBF):

**(a) Calorimetry** — Trapp et al. (2017) measured effective absorptivity of bare and powder-coated 316L, Al 1100, and W discs by direct calorimetric measurement of absorbed energy during single-track scanning at 100, 500, and 1500 mm/s, with a 1070 nm fibre laser focused to a 60 ± 5 µm (1/e²) diameter Gaussian spot over 30–540 W (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 1-2, trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 3-4, trapp2017insituabsorptivity media 513c2402). Post-process cross-sectioning provided track depth and width, but not time-resolved cavity geometry.

**(b) Integrating-sphere radiometry (ISR) combined with inline coherent imaging (ICI)** — Allen et al. (2020) simultaneously measured keyhole depth (ICI at 200 kHz, 15 µm axial resolution) and absolute absorptance (ISR, 4.4 µs resolution) during 10 ms stationary irradiation of AISI 316 stainless steel at 1070 nm through a 238 µm (1/e²) diameter spot, 100–410 W (allen2020simultaneousinoperando pages 2-4, allen2020simultaneousinoperando pages 1-2, allen2019monitoringlasermateriala pages 39-44).

**(c) Integrating-sphere radiometry combined with synchrotron X-ray imaging** — Simonds et al. (2020, 2021, 2024) coupled ISR with high-speed X-ray transmission imaging at the Advanced Photon Source. This enabled simultaneous measurement of absolute absorptance and two-dimensional projected cavity/melt-pool geometry with microsecond time resolution (simonds2020simultaneoushighspeedxray pages 2-3, simonds2021thecausalrelationship pages 6-7, simonds2024abilitytosimulate pages 3-6).

### 2. Absorptance–Keyhole Depth Relationships

**Simonds et al. (2021)** provide the most explicit quantitative relationship between absorptance and keyhole depth. For Ti-6Al-4V at 201 W, 0.7 m/s, and a 49.5 µm spot, the normalized keyhole depth was found to be approximately linearly proportional to the absorptance-dependent normalized enthalpy β = AP/(πH_m D u a³)^(1/2), with Pearson correlation coefficient r = 0.98 on a bare plate (slope 0.42) and r = 0.70 on a powder bed (slope 0.31) (simonds2021thecausalrelationship pages 7-8). The key physical mechanism is that a deeper, higher-aspect-ratio vapor depression produces more internal laser reflections, which increases absorptance, which in turn deepens the cavity further. When the keyhole aspect ratio decreases (e.g., during partial collapse at ~400 µs), absorptance falls rapidly (simonds2021thecausalrelationship pages 6-7, simonds2021thecausalrelationship pages 1-2).

**Simonds et al. (2020)** found that for Ti-6Al-4V during stationary irradiation at 113.6 W, initial absorptance was approximately 0.30, rising rapidly at ~0.45 ms when the process transitioned from conduction to keyhole mode. Among keyhole depth, width, and projected area, depth had the strongest correlation with absorptance (simonds2020simultaneoushighspeedxray pages 2-3).

**Allen et al. (2020)** reported that for 316 SS at a 238 µm spot, absorptance plateaued near 0.21 in argon conduction mode and reached 0.51 in air. At the keyhole threshold (0.58 MW/cm²), coupling efficiency reached 0.58–0.59, and absorptance exceeded 0.9 in deep keyhole mode (allen2020simultaneousinoperando pages 2-4, allen2023sheddinglightona pages 56-61). Absorptance and vapour-depression depth fluctuations occurred at similar kilohertz-scale frequencies and correlated with one another (allen2023sheddinglightona pages 56-61).

### 3. Calorimetric Absorptance Data (Trapp et al. 2017)

For **316L** (bare disc, 500 mm/s): absorptivity was 0.29–0.31 at low power, rose sharply at a keyhole-formation threshold of approximately 70 W, and saturated at approximately 0.78, corresponding to a keyhole depth of roughly 300 µm. At 1500 mm/s the keyhole transition shifted to higher power, and 540 W produced a keyhole only about 207 µm deep (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 3-4). For powder layers (~100 µm thick), low-power absorptivity was about twice that of the bare disc, dropped sharply upon full powder consolidation/melting, then rose again upon keyholing (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 1-2).

For **Al 1100** (bare disc, 1500 mm/s): absorptivity was approximately 0.10 at low power, began its keyhole-driven rise near 200 W, and reached approximately 0.50–0.55 at 540 W (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 4-6, trapp2017insituabsorptivity media 513c2402). Both Al and W exhibited lower absorptivity than 316L and required higher power to initiate keyholing (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 6-7).

### 4. Aluminium-Specific Absorptance Data

Directly measured absorptance data for aluminium alloys at 1 µm with simultaneous keyhole geometry remain scarce relative to Ti-6Al-4V and 316L:

- **Trapp et al. (2017)**: Al 1100 bare, 1070 nm, 60 µm spot, 1500 mm/s — conduction-mode absorptivity ~0.10, rising to ~0.55 in keyhole mode at 540 W (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 4-6, trapp2017insituabsorptivity media 513c2402).
- **Simonds et al. (2024), AM-Bench 2022**: Bare polished Al 5182 (NIST SRM 1241c), 1070 nm, 122.5 µm (1/e²) spot — stationary 501 W with 1.98 ms exposure. Keyhole onset (defined as absorptance ≥ 35%) occurred at approximately 0.189 ms, with a nearly threefold absorption increase within microseconds of cavity formation. In scanning mode (473 W, 0.7 m/s), maximum melt-pool depth was 88.8 ± 0.4 µm and width 326 ± 16 µm (simonds2024abilitytosimulate pages 3-6, simonds2024abilitytosimulate pages 8-10). Aluminum keyhole formation was much less repeatable than Ti-6Al-4V, with ±190 µs spread for stationary and ±650 µs for scanning (simonds2024abilitytosimulate pages 8-10). The full time-resolved datasets are archived at NIST (DOI: 10.18434/mds2-2525) (simonds2024abilitytosimulate pages 1-3).
- **Reed (2024, thesis)**: Al alloys remain near an absorptance of approximately 0.15 at 1070 nm during heating before melting (reed2024timeresolvedabsorptancein pages 27-31, reed2024timeresolvedabsorptanceina pages 27-31).

No published study was found that simultaneously measured keyhole depth and absorptance for AlSi10Mg or Al6061 specifically. The Gan et al. (2021) X-ray dataset includes Al6061 keyhole depths but without concurrent absorptance measurement.

### 5. Keyhole Geometry Benchmarks Without Concurrent Absorptance (Gan et al. 2021)

Gan et al. (2021) measured operando keyhole depths by high-speed synchrotron X-ray imaging for Ti-6Al-4V, Al6061, and SS316 at 100–520 W, 0.3–1.2 m/s, and spot sizes of 44–70 µm (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 2-3). They defined the dimensionless Keyhole number Ke = ηP / [(T_l − T_0) √(π ρ C_p α V_s r_0³)] and reported a universal linear scaling law for normalized keyhole aspect ratio: e* = e/r₀ = 0.4(Ke − 1.4), with conduction mode at Ke < 1.4, transition at 1.4 ≤ Ke ≤ 6.0 (e* ≤ 2), and keyhole mode at Ke > 6.0 (e* > 2) (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 1-2). Aspect-ratio fluctuation scaled as Δe* = 0.36 Ke^0.86; small fluctuations below Ke ≈ 16 and chaotic keyholes above Ke ≈ 30 (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 2-3). The absorptivity η used in the Keyhole number was taken from literature rather than measured simultaneously.

### 6. Ye et al. (2019) Absorptance Scaling Law

Ye et al.'s absorptance model, as reported in secondary sources, provides a process-dependent effective absorptivity: A_e = 0.70 [1 − exp(−0.66 β A_m L_th*)], where β is the normalized enthalpy, A_m is the conduction-mode baseline absorptance, and L_th* = D/(2σ u) is the normalized thermal diffusion length. The saturation value is 0.70. The baseline absorptances used are A_m = 0.34 for 316L and 0.27 for IN625 (hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 4-5, weaver2022laserspotsize pages 6-8). The keyhole threshold corresponds to normalized depth d/(2σ) > 1 (weaver2022laserspotsize pages 6-8). This scaling was fitted to calorimetric data across multiple alloys and process conditions.

The key findings for liquid aluminium specifically are:

- **Krishnan & Nordine (1993)** measured optical properties and emissivities of electromagnetically levitated liquid metals including aluminium by ellipsometry/polarimetry over 360–990 nm and temperatures of 1200–1600 K. Their reported liquid Al normal spectral emissivity (equivalent to absorptance for an opaque equilibrium surface) was approximately 0.04, but this datum does not extend to 1064 nm and must be extrapolated (krishnan1993opticalpropertiesand pages 6-11, krishnan1993opticalpropertiesand pages 1-6). For comparison, liquid Cu showed emissivity 0.085 and liquid Ni 0.31 at 1064 nm (krishnan1990spectralemissivitiesand pages 21-24).

- **Schmid et al. (2012)** used a custom high-temperature rotating-compensator ellipsometer with a 1070 nm SLED source to measure n and k through the solid-liquid transition. They published results for Au and Ag but not for Al at 1070 nm. They noted that temperature-dependent Drude-model predictions can diverge substantially from experiment, particularly for liquid metals (schmid2012measuringopticalproperties pages 1-3).

- **Drude-model applicability**: For aluminium, the free-electron Drude model is physically plausible at wavelengths above approximately 1 µm, where aluminium follows nearly-free-electron behavior and interband transitions (prominent below ~0.8 µm) are negligible (schmid2012measuringopticalproperties pages 1-3). However, the available experimental evidence does not establish that an unadjusted room-temperature Drude model, extrapolated to liquid-phase temperatures, quantitatively reproduces the flat-surface absorptance of liquid Al at 1064–1070 nm. The Drude model qualitatively predicts the correct trend (small absorptance increasing with temperature) but may require temperature-dependent collision-frequency adjustments. For gold and silver at 1070 nm, Schmid et al. showed that a Drude+Lorentz combined model was needed for quantitative accuracy, particularly near and above the melting point (schmid2012measuringopticalproperties media 6b6d82cf).

- **Practical validation band**: Given the sparsity of direct measurements, a reasonable validation band for the Drude-predicted normal-incidence absorptance of liquid Al at 1064–1070 nm is approximately 0.05–0.15. The lower end corresponds to the extrapolated Krishnan value and clean-interface Drude prediction; the upper end includes the effect of oxide, finite surface roughness, and the higher calorimetric values measured by Trapp et al. for polished Al 1100 at low power (~0.10) (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 4-6, krishnan1993opticalpropertiesand pages 6-11).

### 8. Summary and Recommendations for Model Validation

For a first-principles keyhole model of LPBF aluminium alloys at 1070 nm:

1. **Flat-surface Fresnel absorptance**: Use a Drude model for the dielectric function of liquid Al at 1070 nm, but validate against the Krishnan/Nordine emissivity data (~0.04 at shorter wavelengths), the Trapp et al. calorimetric low-power value (~0.10 for polished Al 1100), and the Simonds AM-Bench pre-keyhole absorptance. Expect the normal-incidence absorptance to fall in the range 0.05–0.15 for a clean flat liquid Al surface.

2. **Absorptance vs. keyhole depth**: The strongest quantitative benchmark is Simonds et al. (2021) for Ti-6Al-4V (r = 0.98 linear correlation between normalized depth and absorptance-based enthalpy) (simonds2021thecausalrelationship pages 7-8). For Al, the Simonds et al. (2024) AM-Bench dataset provides time-resolved absorptance paired with X-ray melt-pool images for Al 5182 (simonds2024abilitytosimulate pages 3-6, simonds2024abilitytosimulate pages 1-3). The NIST archived data (DOI: 10.18434/mds2-2525) should be downloaded for detailed ray-tracing comparison.

3. **Keyhole depth validation**: Use the Gan et al. (2021) X-ray depths for Ti-6Al-4V, Al6061, and SS316 at 100–520 W, 0.3–1.2 m/s, 44–70 µm spots (gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 2-3, gan2021universalscalinglaws pages 1-2). The Keyhole number Ke provides a universal framework relating absorptivity, process parameters, and keyhole aspect ratio.

4. **316L comparison**: Trapp et al. (2017) absorptivity vs. power curves (60 µm spot, 100–540 W, three speeds) provide the most comprehensive calorimetric dataset (trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 1-2, trapp2017insituabsorptivity pages 3-4, trapp2017insituabsorptivity media 513c2402). Allen et al. (2020) offer simultaneous depth–absorptance data for 316 SS at a larger 238 µm spot (allen2020simultaneousinoperando pages 2-4, allen2020simultaneousinoperando pages 1-2).

5. **Ye et al. scaling law**: The empirical absorptance model A_e = 0.70[1 − exp(−0.66 β A_m L_th*)] with A_m = 0.34 (316L) or 0.27 (IN625) provides a useful check on whether your ray-traced absorptance reproduces the correct functional dependence on normalized enthalpy (hong2026fittingfreediagnosisof pages 4-5, weaver2022laserspotsize pages 6-8).

No published simultaneous absorptance + keyhole-depth dataset was found specifically for AlSi10Mg or for a hypereutectic Al–24Si blend. The closest aluminium dataset is the Simonds et al. (2024) Al 5182 integrating-sphere + X-ray data at 501 W / 122.5 µm stationary and 473 W / 0.7 m/s scanned (simonds2024abilitytosimulate pages 3-6).
