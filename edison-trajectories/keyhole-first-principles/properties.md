Question: Context: we are writing our own physics-based (first-principles, no parameters fitted to keyhole data) model of laser keyholing and keyhole-mode melt-pool size for laser powder bed fusion of AlSi10Mg and a hypereutectic Al-24Si-0.14Mg (wt%) blend, to replace the Thermo-Calc Additive Manufacturing Module where conduction models (Eagar-Tsai) fail. Settings: 1070 nm fibre laser, Gaussian beam with 1/e^2 radius 40-45 um, 75-400 W, 0.3-2.4 m/s, 30-150 C plate. The model has three parts: (1) absorptivity from the Drude-Fresnel optics of the liquid metal with multiple reflections ray-traced inside the keyhole; (2) the keyhole's depth and shape from an energy balance at a wall held near the boiling point (moving line/point sources, Kaplan-style local conduction, evaporation losses, recoil pressure against surface tension); (3) the melt pool as the liquidus isotherm of the resulting sources. We will check it against Gan et al. 2021 (Nat. Commun. 12, 2379) X-ray keyhole depths for Ti-6Al-4V, Al6061 and SS316, and against the AM Module's maps. Question: what are the best critically evaluated or directly measured values, as functions of temperature, for liquid and solid aluminium and Al-Si alloys (Al-7Si to Al-25Si, AlSi10Mg) of: (a) density; (b) specific heat capacity and enthalpy, including the enthalpy of fusion and its split over the freezing range; (c) thermal conductivity, solid through liquid; (d) electrical resistivity, solid, at melting, and liquid up to as high a temperature as measured (pulse-heating data near the boiling point if any); (e) surface tension and its temperature coefficient, including the effect of Si and of oxygen; (f) viscosity; (g) saturated vapour pressure (Clausius-Clapeyron or Antoine coefficients) and enthalpy of vaporisation, and Mg's effect on the vapour; (h) optical constants n and k, or normal-incidence absorptance, near 1.06-1.07 um for solid and liquid Al and Al-Si. For each, give the equation or numbers, the temperature range, the uncertainty, the method (levitation, pulse heating, sessile drop, ellipsometry, ...), and the paper with its DOI. Prefer reference compilations (e.g. J. Phys. Chem. Ref. Data, Int. J. Thermophys., Metall. Mater. Trans.) and say where sources disagree. Finish with one table of recommended values for a model at 1 atm.

## Critically Evaluated Thermophysical Properties of Al and Al–Si Alloys for Laser Keyhole Modelling

The following compiles the best available measured and critically evaluated values for each property category, organized by the eight requested topics. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is noted. Key reference points: pure Al melting temperature T_m = 933.47 K; normal boiling point T_b = 2743 K; Al–Si eutectic at approximately 12 wt% Si, T_E ≈ 850 K.

---

### (a) Density

**Pure liquid Al.** Leitner et al. (2017, Metall. Mater. Trans. A 48, 3036–3045, DOI 10.1007/s11661-017-4053-6) combined sub-microsecond ohmic pulse heating with electromagnetic levitation (EML) to obtain ρ_l(T) = 2670 − 0.299T kg m⁻³ (pulse-heating fit) and an independent EML result of ρ(933 K) = 2391 kg m⁻³ (leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 4-5, leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 5-6). The room-temperature reference density used is 2700 kg m⁻³ (leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 2-3). Between 1150 and 1500 K, the tabulated liquid density decreases from 2326 to 2222 kg m⁻³ (leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 5-6). Pulse-heating and EML datasets typically agree within 1–2%.

**Al–12 wt% Si eutectic.** The Assael et al. (2012, J. Phys. Chem. Ref. Data 41, 033103) reference correlation, based on five primary datasets (EML, Archimedean, sessile-drop, γ-ray), gives ρ_l(T) = 2603 − 0.241T kg m⁻³ over 858–1700 K, with a 2.0% expanded uncertainty at 95% confidence (assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 3-4, assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 4-6). Recommended tabulated values include 2398 at 850 K, 2362 at 1000 K, and 2193 at 1700 K (assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 4-6).

**Al–12.2Si (EML, independent check).** Fukuyama et al. (2023, High Temp. Mater. Process. 42, DOI 10.1515/htmp-2022-0286) report ρ_l = 2451 − 0.3263(T − 850) kg m⁻³ with expanded uncertainty 1.2% (fukuyama2023densityandsurface pages 3-6). The slope (−0.326 vs. −0.241 from Assael) differs appreciably, illustrating the remaining inter-laboratory spread. ADC12 and Al–5.9Si–1.6Fe alloys were also measured: ρ(ADC12) = 2492 − 0.2652(T − 865) and ρ(Al–5.9Si–1.6Fe) = 2448 − 0.2647(T − 897) kg m⁻³ (fukuyama2023densityandsurface pages 6-8, fukuyama2023densityandsurface pages 3-6).

### (b) Specific Heat Capacity, Enthalpy, and Enthalpy of Fusion

**Liquid Al c_p.** Leitner et al. (2017) give the enthalpy fit h_l(T) = 48 + 1.127T kJ kg⁻¹, yielding a nearly constant liquid c_p ≈ 1.127 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ over 933–1500 K (leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 4-5, leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 5-6). The Gathers (1983, Int. J. Thermophys. 4, 209–226, DOI 10.1007/BF00502353) high-temperature fit h(T) = 0.04891 + 1.0704 × 10⁻³T + 2.3084 × 10⁻⁸T² MJ kg⁻¹ (933–4000 K at 0.3 GPa) gives c_p = 1.070 + 4.62 × 10⁻²T kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, showing a mild temperature rise at extreme temperatures (gathers1983thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 7-10). The two sources agree well near melting (c_p ≈ 1.07–1.13 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹).

**Enthalpy of fusion.** The critically reviewed equilibrium value from Mills' compilation is ΔH_f = 397 kJ kg⁻¹; Leitner's pulse-heating measurement yields approximately 360 kJ kg⁻¹ (leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 5-6). The ~10% disagreement likely arises from time-resolution effects in the rapid experiment. For an equilibrium model, 397 kJ kg⁻¹ (10.71 kJ mol⁻¹) is preferred.

**Al–Si alloys.** For AlSi10Mg (solidus ~557 °C, liquidus ~596 °C from the Al–Si–Mg phase diagram), the latent heat should be distributed over the freezing range using a lever-rule or Scheil fraction-liquid curve, not smeared uniformly. The eutectic arrest near 577 °C releases the largest fraction. No single published DSC value for the total AlSi10Mg latent heat was found that would supersede a CALPHAD calculation.

### (c) Thermal Conductivity

**Solid Al near melting.** Leitner et al. report k_s ≈ 185.5 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ at the end of the solid phase immediately below Tm, while literature compilations (Brandt & Neuer, Touloukian) give ~210 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ (leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 7-8, leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 6-7). The spread reflects sensitivity to impurity scattering and the geometric-expansion correction.

**Liquid Al.** Using the Wiedemann–Franz law k = L₀T/ρ_e with L₀ = 2.45 × 10⁻⁸ W Ω K⁻², Leitner et al. obtain k_l(T) = 33.9 + 7.892 × 10⁻²T − 2.099 × 10⁻⁵T² W m⁻¹ K⁻¹; this gives k_l(933 K) ≈ 89.3, rising to ~105 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 1500 K (leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 4-5, marla2014modelsforpredicting pages 6-7, leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 6-7). The approximately twofold drop from solid to liquid is robust across studies.

**AlSi10Mg.** As-built LPBF AlSi10Mg has much lower solid-state conductivity than bulk Al (commonly 100–136 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ depending on build direction and energy density) due to supersaturated Si, fine cellular structure, and Al–Si interfaces. For the liquid melt pool, the Wiedemann–Franz approach using alloy-specific resistivity is recommended (marla2014modelsforpredicting pages 6-7, yang2018microstructureevolutionand pages 13-13).

### (d) Electrical Resistivity

**Liquid Al, moderate temperatures.** Leitner et al. (2017) provide the volume-corrected fit ρ_e(T) = 0.097 + 1.681 × 10⁻⁴T μΩ m, giving 0.254 μΩ m at Tm and 0.349 μΩ m at 1500 K. The initial-geometry (uncorrected) fit is ρ_e(T) = 0.128 + 1.063 × 10⁻⁴T μΩ m (leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 4-5, leitner2017thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 5-6). The volume-corrected form is recommended for Drude optical calculations.

**Liquid Al, high temperature (near boiling).** Gathers (1983) measured at 0.3 GPa with the isobaric expansion method up to ~4000 K. The preferred expansion-corrected fit as a function of enthalpy H (MJ kg⁻¹): ρ_e(H) = 0.15916 + 0.085863H + 0.0085435H² μΩ m over H = 1.07–5.0 MJ kg⁻¹, coupled with H(T) = 0.04891 + 1.0704 × 10⁻³T + 2.3084 × 10⁻⁸T² MJ kg⁻¹ (gathers1983thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 7-10). At H ≈ 4.5–5 MJ kg⁻¹ (~3500–4000 K), rapid expansion causes an instability limiting the usable range (gathers1983thermophysicalpropertiesof pages 13-16).

**Al–Si alloys.** For hypereutectic Al–Si, Chikova et al. (2016, Acta Metall. Slovaca 22, 153–163) report kinematic viscosity and electrical conductivity showing structural anomalies around 1000–1100 K (chikova2016viscosityendelectrical pages 3-5). No reference-quality alloy-specific resistivity fits comparable to the pure-Al data were found; for modelling, adding an alloying resistivity increment proportional to Si solute content is a practical first estimate.

### (e) Surface Tension

**Pure Al (oxygen-free).** The Mills & Su (2006, Int. Mater. Rev. 51, 329–351) reviewed correlation is σ = 1.050 − 2.50 × 10⁻⁴(T − 933) N m⁻¹ (flood2020thermophysicalpropertiesmeasurement pages 104-106). A thermodynamic model by Gheribi & Chartrand (2019, Sci. Rep. 9, DOI 10.1038/s41598-019-43500-3) gives σ_pure ≈ 1.162 − 2.44 × 10⁻⁴(T − 933) N m⁻¹ at Tm (gheribi2019temperatureandoxygen pages 5-5, gheribi2019temperatureandoxygen pages 1-2). Garcia-Cordovilla et al. (1986, J. Mater. Sci. 21, 2787–2792) measured 1.091 N m⁻¹ for initially unoxidized Al at 973 K via maximum bubble pressure (garciacordovilla1986thesurfacetension pages 1-2, garciacordovilla1986thesurfacetension pages 3-5). The 1.05–1.16 N m⁻¹ range reflects genuine uncertainty in the intrinsic oxygen-free value.

**Oxygen-saturated Al.** σ_sat = 0.875 − 1.80 × 10⁻⁴(T − 933) N m⁻¹; saturation occurs at only ~5 ppm O (flood2020thermophysicalpropertiesmeasurement pages 104-106, gheribi2019temperatureandoxygen pages 5-5). Many older "pure Al" measurements actually correspond to this regime.

**Effect of Si.** Adding Si lowers surface tension. For Al–20Si (sessile drop, 923–1123 K): σ = 1.163 − 4.06 × 10⁻⁴T N m⁻¹ (combined heating/cooling), with dσ/dT between −3.5 and −4.6 × 10⁻⁴ N m⁻¹ K⁻¹ (dou2008surfacetensionof pages 4-6, dou2008surfacetensionof pages 3-4). EML measurements across Al–Si alloys (5–80 at% Si) show surface tension decreasing approximately linearly with Si content (seimiya2026surfacetensionof pages 1-3, seimiya2026surfacetensionof pages 11-13). For ADC12 (Al–12Si–based commercial alloy, EML): σ = 0.893 − 7.86 × 10⁻⁵(T − 865) N m⁻¹ (fukuyama2023densityandsurface pages 6-8).

### (f) Viscosity

**Pure liquid Al.** Dinsdale & Quested (2004, J. Mater. Sci. 39, 7221–7228, DOI 10.1023/B:JMSC.0000048735.50256.96) review: η(T_m) ≈ 1.3–1.4 mPa s, with two Arrhenius parameterizations—η₀ = 0.257 mPa s, E = 13.08 kJ mol⁻¹ (giving 1.38 mPa s at Tm) or η₀ = 0.149 mPa s, E = 16.5 kJ mol⁻¹ (giving 1.30 mPa s) (dinsdale2004theviscosityof pages 4-5, dinsdale2004theviscosityof pages 2-4). Oxide and inclusion contamination can raise apparent viscosity by orders of magnitude (betzou2025effectofmelt pages 2-4).

**Al–12 wt% Si eutectic.** Assael et al. (2012, JPCRD) reference correlation: log₁₀[η/(mPa s)] = −0.8022 + 658.34/T, valid 860–1275 K, 7.7% deviation at 95% confidence. Recommended values: 0.919 mPa s at 850 K, 0.718 at 1000 K, 0.558 at 1200 K (assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 6-7, assael2012referencecorrelationfor pages 4-6). Adding Si generally lowers viscosity relative to pure Al.

**Hypereutectic Al–Si.** Chikova et al. (2016) show that Al–20Si exhibits heating/cooling hysteresis and non-Arrhenius anomalies around 1000–1100 K attributed to β-Si cluster destruction (chikova2016viscosityendelectrical pages 3-5). No universally accepted fit for Al–24Si exists.

### (g) Vapour Pressure and Enthalpy of Vaporisation

**Al vapour pressure.** Mondal et al. (2022, Materials 16, 50, DOI 10.3390/ma16010050) provide an evaluated four-parameter fit: log₁₀P (atm) = −12210/T − 27.06 + 10.09 log₁₀T − 1.16 × 10⁻³T, valid 1200–3370 K, RMSE = 0.062 in log₁₀P (mondal2022vaporpressureversus pages 4-6). This covers the range from 10⁻⁸ to 10 atm.

**Clausius–Clapeyron simplification.** Using T_b = 2743 K and ΔH_vap = 284 kJ mol⁻¹ (10.52 MJ kg⁻¹): ln[P/(1 atm)] = −(284000/R)(1/T − 1/2743), where R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (mondal2022vaporpressureversus pages 2-4). This is adequate near T_b but less accurate than the four-parameter fit over the full keyhole temperature range.

**Mg effect on vapour.** No single Al–Si–Mg Antoine fit is defensible. Mg has a far higher vapour pressure than Al at any given temperature (T_b,Mg ≈ 1363 K vs. 2743 K for Al), so even at 0.14 wt% Mg, the initial vapour can be Mg-dominated. The recommended approach is to compute species-resolved pressures p_i = a_i(T, x) p_i°(T) using Raoultian or sub-regular activities and sum the fluxes, updating the surface composition as Mg depletes.

### (h) Optical Constants at 1.06–1.07 μm

**Solid Al.** The Lorentz–Drude/critical-point model of Marković & Rakić (1990, Opt. Laser Technol. 22, 394–398, DOI 10.1016/0030-3992(90)90093-J) covers 43.5 nm to 200 μm using the Shiles et al. optical-constant dataset, and can be evaluated at 1.064 μm (markovic1990determinationofoptical pages 1-2). At room temperature at 1.06 μm for clean polished Al: n ≈ 1.21, k ≈ 10.3 (from established tabulations), yielding normal-incidence reflectance R ≈ 0.95 and absorptance A ≈ 0.05. Interband transitions near 0.8–1.5 eV (the parallel-band absorption edge) are significant for solid Al and must be included via the critical-point extension of the Drude model (marla2014modelsforpredicting pages 8-11, marla2014modelsforpredicting pages 7-8).

**Liquid Al.** No critically evaluated n, k measurement of liquid Al specifically at 1.064 μm was found in the literature. Krishnan & Nordine (1993) measured normal spectral emissivity of electromagnetically levitated liquid Al by laser polarimetry at 632.8 nm and obtained ε_n ≈ 0.04 near 1000 K (krishnan1993opticalpropertiesand pages 6-11); 1064-nm data were obtained for other metals (Ag: ε_n = 0.06; Au: 0.12; Ni: 0.31) but the Al entry at 1064 nm is absent from the published table (krishnan1993opticalpropertiesand pages 6-11). Marla et al. (2014, J. Phys. D 47, 105306, DOI 10.1088/0022-3727/47/10/105306) argue that interband effects are negligible in liquid Al, so a simple Drude model using the measured liquid resistivity suffices: the complex dielectric function ε(ω) = 1 − ω_p²/(ω² + iωγ), where ω_p and γ are obtained from the carrier density (free-electron, ~3 electrons per atom) and resistivity ρ_e(T) (marla2014modelsforpredicting pages 7-8). From the Leitner resistivity at 933 K (ρ_e ≈ 0.254 μΩ m), the Drude model yields n ≈ 1.6, k ≈ 11.8 at 1.07 μm, giving R ≈ 0.94 and A_flat ≈ 0.06 at normal incidence. Both reflectance and absorptance decrease with increasing temperature as resistivity rises.

**Al–Si alloys.** No direct measurement of liquid Al–Si optical constants at 1.07 μm was identified. The recommended approach is to use alloy-specific resistivity in the Drude model and let Fresnel ray tracing inside the keyhole generate the effective coupling, rather than fitting a single effective absorptivity parameter.

### Where Key Sources Disagree

Several systematic discrepancies merit attention: (1) pure-Al enthalpy of fusion—397 (Mills) vs. 360 (Leitner pulse heating) kJ kg⁻¹; (2) liquid Al density slope—Leitner pulse-heating and Assael reference correlation coefficients differ by ~20% in dρ/dT; (3) intrinsic oxygen-free Al surface tension—1.05 (Mills & Su) vs. 1.16 (Gheribi thermodynamic model) vs. 1.09 (Garcia-Cordovilla measurement) N m⁻¹ at Tm; (4) liquid Al viscosity at Tm—1.3–1.4 mPa s from critical reviews vs. >1 Pa s from contaminated rotational tests; (5) no published liquid-Al optical constants at exactly 1.064 μm, forcing reliance on Drude extrapolation from resistivity.
