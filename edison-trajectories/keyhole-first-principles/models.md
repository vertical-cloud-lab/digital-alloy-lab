## Physics-Based Reduced-Order Keyhole Models for Laser Welding and LPBF: Governing Equations, Assumptions, Validation, and Accuracy Limitations

### 1. Overview

Several physics-based, reduced-order models predict keyhole depth and shape from first principles—without fitting to keyhole depth data—by solving local energy, momentum, or pressure balances at the keyhole wall. The principal models, their governing equations, assumptions, and available validation against in-situ X-ray measurements are summarized in the table below and discussed in detail in the following sections.

### 2. Kaplan (1994): Point-by-Point Energy Balance

Kaplan's model (DOI: 10.1088/0022-3727/27/9/002) discretises the keyhole vertically and, at every wall point, equates absorbed laser power to the conductive heat flux into the workpiece (kaplan1994amodelof pages 3-5, kaplan1994amodelof pages 1-3). The conductive flux is obtained from Rosenthal's moving line-source temperature field, which uses modified Bessel functions K₀ and K₁ and the modified Péclet number Pe' = v/κ. The wall is held at the evaporation temperature Tᵥ, and the boundary condition determines the source strength P' and local heat flow at each depth element (kaplan1994amodelof pages 3-5). The local wall angle θₛ is obtained geometrically by requiring the projected absorbed intensity to balance the conductive loss. Front and rear walls are computed independently, producing an asymmetric profile because upstream conduction losses at the front wall are roughly twice those at the rear (kaplan1994amodelof pages 3-5).

**Absorptivity treatment:** A Gaussian-like beam intensity distribution is attenuated first by plasma-plume absorption (Beer–Lambert: η_pl = 1 − exp(−α_IB·h_pl)), then by first-pass inverse-Bremsstrahlung absorption inside the keyhole, and then by Fresnel absorption at the wall. The reflected fraction undergoes multiple reflections estimated from the mean wall angle; the number of reflections n_mr ≈ π/(2θₘ), and the remaining intensity after n reflections is I_n/I_0 = (ρ_Fr)^n_mr (kaplan1994amodelof pages 5-6). Direct Fresnel absorption and multiple-reflection absorption dominate, while plasma absorption is comparatively negligible for 1 μm wavelength (kaplan1994amodelof pages 9-11).

**Validation:** For 4 and 10 kW CO₂ laser welding of mild steel, predicted depths lay within experimental scatter except at low welding speeds, where the simplified plume model underestimated beam damping (kaplan1994amodelof pages 6-7, kaplan1994amodelof pages 7-9). Kaplan later extended the Fresnel analysis to 1 μm-wavelength beams with angle-dependent and wavy-surface absorptivity (DOI: 10.1016/j.apsusc.2011.08.086), and Coviello et al. (DOI: 10.3389/fphy.2021.754672) added iterative ray tracing with spatially varying plasma absorption to the Kaplan-style wall-balance framework (coviello2022numericalstudyon pages 3-4).

### 3. Lankalapalli, Tu & Gartner (1996): Conical Keyhole

This model (DOI: 10.1088/0022-3727/29/7/018) assumes a conical keyhole with top radius a₀ and linearly decreasing radius a(z) = a₀(1 − z/d̂), yielding a local Péclet number Pe(z) = v·a(z)/(2α) (lankalapalli1996amodelfor pages 5-7, lankalapalli1996amodelfor pages 3-5). Each infinitesimally thin horizontal slice is treated as a moving cylindrical heat source; the quasi-steady 2D advection–diffusion equation ∂²T/∂x² + ∂²T/∂y² − (v/α)∂T/∂x = 0 is solved with the cylinder surface at Tᵥ. The absorbed power per unit depth is Pₜ = k(Tᵥ − T₀)g(Pe), where g is a polynomial fit to the Péclet-dependent conductive function. The energy balance equates incident (absorbed) power to the integral: Pᵢ = ∫₀^d̂ Pₜ(z)dz (lankalapalli1996amodelfor pages 5-7).

**Key assumptions:** Axial conduction is neglected (Q_z ≪ Q_r); latent heat of fusion and evaporation are neglected; constant effective thermal properties; complete absorption of the stated incident power (lankalapalli1996amodelfor pages 11-12, lankalapalli1996amodelfor pages 3-5). These effective properties (α = 0.08 cm² s⁻¹, k = 0.765 W cm⁻¹ K⁻¹ for steel) were calibrated from weld data, so the published implementation is not fully parameter-free (lankalapalli1996amodelfor pages 9-11).

**Validation:** Tested on 25 power–speed combinations for 1010/1035 steel with a 5 kW CO₂ laser; predictions followed measured trends but diverged when the actual keyhole shape departed substantially from a cone (lankalapalli1996amodelfor pages 9-11).

### 4. Fabbro & Chouf (2000); Fabbro (2010, 2020): Front-Wall Drilling / Piston Model

Fabbro's model (DOIs: 10.2351/1.521924; 10.1088/0022-3727/43/44/445501; 10.3390/app10041487) treats the keyhole front as a quasi-stationary inclined surface. The drilling velocity is proportional to absorbed intensity: V_d = k_d·I_abs (fabbro2010physicalmechanismscontrolling pages 4-7). The front wall is stationary when V_d = V_w·cos α, yielding V_w·cos α = k_d·I₀·A(α)·sin α. For small inclinations and A(α) ≈ A₀(sin α)^q with q ≈ 0.1, this gives tan α ≈ V_w/(k_d·I₀·A₀) (fabbro2010meltpooland pages 6-9). For a uniform (top-hat) beam of diameter D, integrating gives the depth:

L = (4A₀k_d/π)·P/(D·V_w)

This linear scaling L ∝ P/(D·V_w) was experimentally confirmed in Nd:YAG laser welding of steel (fabbro2010physicalmechanismscontrolling pages 4-7). In a later generalisation, the aspect ratio R = R₀/(1 + V_w/V₀), incorporating Péclet-dependent conductive losses and a conduction scaling with g(Pe) ≈ mPe + n (fabbro2020depthdependenceand pages 3-5). The fuller piston model partitions absorbed power as P_abs = P_m + P_vap + P_cond + P_kin, with recoil pressure from Clausius–Clapeyron vapour pressure and a condensation factor (fabbro2020depthdependenceand pages 18-20). The threshold for surface depression satisfies p_r(T_th) = p_amb (fabbro2020depthdependenceand pages 20-22).

**LPBF validation:** Cunningham et al. (2019) applied the Fabbro-type relation tan θ = V_w/V_d to LPBF of Ti-6Al-4V. Using nominal 95 and 140 μm Gaussian beam diameters as top-hat widths overpredicted keyhole depth by approximately a factor of two; replacing them with measured effective depression widths of approximately 50 and 100 μm produced a very good fit across the full dataset (cunningham2019keyholethresholdand pages 2-3, cunningham2019keyholethresholdand pages 3-4). This demonstrates that the choice of effective beam diameter—not simply the 1/e² radius—is critical for LPBF-scale accuracy.

### 5. Steen, Dowden, Davis & Kapadia (1988): Point-plus-Line Source

This model (DOI: 10.1088/0022-3727/21/8/002) represents absorption in the keyhole as the superposition of a uniform line source (strength q_l per unit length along z) and a point source (strength q_p) located at depth c' below the surface. The steady moving-frame heat equation κ∇²T = v∂T/∂x is solved with an adiabatic free surface enforced by an image point source at z = −c' (steen1988apointand pages 3-4, steen1988apointand pages 1-3). The line-source contribution contains the modified Bessel function K₀ with the Rosenthal translating-source exponential. The melt boundary is the isotherm T = T_m.

**Key assumptions:** The keyhole is infinitesimally thin; thermophysical properties are constant; no recoil pressure, free-surface dynamics, evaporation loss, or self-consistent optical calculation. Source strengths are inferred from the observed weld profile rather than predicted from first principles (steen1988apointand pages 6-7).

**Validation:** Computed profiles agreed reasonably with two example welds; the point source accounted for 11–26% and the line source for 19–64% of absorbed laser power. The model could not reproduce the inward taper in the lower third of the weld, where line-source strength presumably diminishes with depth (steen1988apointand pages 4-6, steen1988apointand pages 6-7).

### 6. Semak & Matsunawa (1997): Recoil-Pressure Energy Balance

Semak and Matsunawa (DOI: 10.1088/0022-3727/30/5/013; recoil-energy paper DOI: 10.1088/0022-3727/30/18/008) model the front keyhole wall as a hydrodynamic melt layer driven by evaporation recoil. The local energy balance is:

I_abs = A(α)·I_inc·cos α = −k∇_nT + I_v

where I_v is the evaporation intensity, approximated as I_v = A(α)I_inc cos α − k(T_v − T_m)/√(α/v) (matsunawa1997thesimulationof pages 4-5, matsunawa1997thesimulationof pages 3-4). The evaporation mass flux is ṁ'' = I_v/L_v, and the recoil pressure is p_r = ṁ''·v_T, where v_T ≈ √(γR_sT_v) is approximately the sound speed at the boiling temperature. Melt is expelled laterally when p_r > σ/r_l (the capillary restoring pressure) (matsunawa1997thesimulationof pages 4-5).

**Key assumptions:** Only the front wall receives significant intensity; recoil pressure dominates surface tension; evaporated mass is negligible compared with expelled liquid; beam is Gaussian in x and uniform in y; melt ejection responds quasi-instantaneously to recoil changes (matsunawa1997thesimulationof pages 3-4, matsunawa1997thesimulationof pages 6-10).

**Validation:** Qualitative comparison with observed front-wall hump dynamics; the instantaneous-response assumption caused discrepancies, and introducing a finite melt-flow establishment time was identified as necessary (matsunawa1997thesimulationof pages 6-10).

### 7. Recoil Pressure: Anisimov (1968) and Knight (1979)

The recoil pressure exerted on the liquid surface during strong evaporation is not equal to the saturated vapour pressure p_sat(T_s) but is reduced by the Knudsen-layer backflow. At the condensed-phase boundary, emitted particles follow a half-range Maxwellian. Collisions within the Knudsen layer (a few mean free paths thick) convert this into a full Maxwellian downstream, with some fraction returning and condensing (klassen2018simulationofevaporation pages 73-77, klassen2018simulationofevaporation pages 77-81). The evaporation coefficient φ reaches a maximum of 0.82 when the outflow is choked (Mach 1), implying approximately 18% back-flux (1 − φ = 0.18) (klassen2018simulationofevaporation pages 84-88, klassen2018simulationofevaporation pages 81-84, klassen2017amulticomponentevaporation pages 5-8).

The recoil pressure in the strong-evaporation limit is commonly approximated as:

p_r ≈ 0.54 · p_sat(T_s)

This arises from evaluating the net momentum flux using the Knudsen-layer jump conditions for density ratio ρ_Kn/ρ_S and temperature ratio T_Kn/T_S (klassen2018simulationofevaporation pages 84-88). The saturated vapour pressure itself follows Clausius–Clapeyron: p_sat(T_s) = p_ref·exp[−L_v/(R_s)(1/T_s − 1/T_ref)].

Under atmospheric back-pressure or for multicomponent Al–Mg–Si evaporation, the standard 0.82/0.54 monatomic-gas limits must be replaced by a more general Knight-type treatment that accounts for ambient pressure, species-selective evaporation, and potentially subcritical Mach numbers (klassen2017amulticomponentevaporation pages 3-5).

### 8. Multiple-Reflection Absorptivity: Gouffé and Ray Tracing

**Gouffé's cavity formula** provides the apparent emissivity (and by Kirchhoff's law, absorptivity) of a diffuse-gray isothermal cavity by summing the geometric series of reflected fractions. The effective absorptivity α_eff = 1 − R_esc depends on the wall reflectivity ρ, the aperture-to-interior area ratio s/S, and the configuration factor F (the projected solid angle of the aperture divided by π). Kelly corrected Gouffé's treatment by replacing ω/π with the proper configuration factor (smith2006…graduatecouncil pages 23-28). Gouffé's approximation can be inaccurate for elongated cavities and assumes diffuse reflection, which is physically inappropriate for metallic keyhole walls (quinn1967thecalculationof pages 7-9).

**Specular Fresnel ray tracing** is more appropriate for laser keyholes. The incident beam is discretised into rays; each ray strikes the keyhole wall, deposits energy according to the angle-dependent Fresnel absorptivity A_F(θ), and is specularly reflected. The reflected ray is geometrically traced to the next wall intersection, where the process repeats (solana1997astudyof pages 3-4, jin2006multiplereflectionsand pages 4-6). For metallic surfaces, the Fresnel coefficients for s- and p-polarisation are computed from the complex refractive index (or equivalently, the Drude model), which is wavelength- and temperature-dependent. For high-absorptivity materials (e.g. glass), first-incidence Fresnel absorption dominates; for low-absorptivity metals like aluminium at 1 μm wavelength, multiple reflections significantly enhance total cavity absorption (jin2006multiplereflectionsand pages 1-3).

### 10. Which Assumption Most Limits Accuracy at LPBF Scales?

For LPBF with beam radii of 40–70 μm and scan speeds of 0.3–1.5 m/s, **the treatment of absorptivity is the single most limiting assumption** in reduced-order keyhole models.

**Magnitude of error:** Models using constant or oversimplified absorptivity produce depth prediction errors of ±35–50% (weaver2022laserspotsize pages 11-13). For aluminium alloys specifically, conduction-mode absorptivity at 1070 nm is approximately 0.15 for a smooth surface, while keyhole-mode absorptivity can reach approximately 0.7—a factor of approximately 4.7 (patel2021meltingmodesin pages 63-66). The Fabbro model applied with nominal Gaussian beam diameter overpredicted LPBF keyhole depth by approximately 2× (cunningham2019keyholethresholdand pages 3-4). An iterative physics-based absorptance model for Ti-6Al-4V still showed 36% discrepancy at P = 196 W, v = 1 m/s, r₀ = 50 μm, and for Al6061 at P = 540 W, v = 0.6 m/s, the analytical model failed to converge entirely (zhang2025machinelearningaidedoptimization pages 164-169).

**Why absorptivity dominates:** At LPBF scales, the keyhole aspect ratio is typically modest (1–5), meaning few multiple reflections occur and the transition from shallow depression to deep keyhole is rapid and fluctuating. The number of reflections and hence total absorption change dramatically with small perturbations in wall angle or depth. Simonds et al. showed that absorptance tracks cavity cross-sectional area in real time, so any model using a fixed absorptivity value cannot capture these dynamics (simonds2021thecausalrelationship pages 3-4, simonds2021thecausalrelationship pages 6-7).

**Secondary limitations** include:
- **Effective beam diameter vs. 1/e² radius:** The Gaussian intensity profile means that only the high-intensity core drives the keyhole. Using the 1/e² diameter rather than the effective depression width produces systematic overprediction (cunningham2019keyholethresholdand pages 3-4).
- **Steady-state assumption:** LPBF keyholes exhibit 2.5–10 kHz fluctuations; quasi-steady models cannot predict instantaneous collapse or porosity formation (patel2021meltingmodesin pages 40-43, vaglio2024anovelthermogeometrical pages 4-5).
- **Neglect of latent heat:** While generally a small fraction of total energy in steel, latent heat can be more significant for aluminium alloys where the high thermal conductivity makes conductive losses comparable to incident power (patel2021meltingmodesin pages 40-43).
- **2D conduction:** Neglecting axial heat flow underestimates losses at the keyhole tip, especially for low-aspect-ratio keyholes typical of LPBF (coviello2022studymodellingand pages 99-102).
- **Fixed boiling-point wall temperature:** At LPBF intensities (>1 MW cm⁻²), the actual surface temperature can exceed the normal boiling point, increasing recoil pressure exponentially; models assuming T_w = T_v underestimate drilling rate and depth (patel2021meltingmodesin pages 40-43, coviello2022numericalstudyon pages 3-4).

For aluminium alloys at 1070 nm wavelength, the absorptivity limitation is especially severe because (i) the flat-surface Fresnel absorptivity is very low (~0.06–0.15), making the cavity-enhancement factor critically important; (ii) aluminium's high thermal conductivity requires accurate absorbed power to predict correct melt-pool dimensions; and (iii) absorptivity scaling laws developed for steels and titanium alloys do not transfer to high-reflectivity aluminium (patel2021meltingmodesin pages 96-100, patel2021meltingmodesin pages 63-66).

### 11. Summary and Recommendations for a First-Principles LPBF Keyhole Model

For a Kaplan-style model targeting AlSi10Mg and Al-24Si-0.14Mg at 1070 nm, 40–45 μm beam radius, 75–400 W, and 0.3–2.4 m/s:

1. **Absorptivity** must be computed self-consistently from Drude–Fresnel optics with temperature-dependent complex refractive index, ray-traced through the evolving keyhole geometry, rather than assumed constant. The cavity enhancement from multiple reflections is essential for aluminium.

2. **Recoil pressure** should use the Knight/Anisimov Knudsen-layer model with p_r ≈ 0.54·p_sat(T_s), not p_sat directly, and account for multicomponent (Al, Si, Mg) evaporation under atmospheric argon back-pressure.

3. **Effective beam diameter** rather than the 1/e² radius should define the keyhole opening, as demonstrated by Cunningham et al.

4. **Wall temperature** should be solved from the energy balance rather than fixed at T_v, since the wall can be superheated at LPBF intensities.

5. **Validation** should be performed against the Gan et al. Keyhole-number scaling for Al6061 and Ti-6Al-4V at LPBF-relevant conditions, and against Cunningham's front-wall angle/depth relationship.
