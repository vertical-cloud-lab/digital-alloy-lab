\begin{abstract}
% Target <= 200 words (see journal-format.md). Numbers in brackets wait on data.
Laser powder bed fusion can print few aluminum alloys, and the strongest of
them depend on scandium and other elements with insecure supply. Finding new
ones is slow for two reasons: powder for an untested composition is hard to
obtain, and campaigns spend their budget in a fixed order, screening by
calculation and then testing the survivors. Here, a single laboratory makes
powder for each candidate by ultrasonic atomization of a dosed
\SI{250}{\gram} charge, characterizes it, prints it on an in-house desktop
system and tests it, while a cost-aware optimizer treats the whole workflow as
a network of models and decides, for each candidate, which step to run next.
Across \tbd{N} atomization runs on \tbd{M} compositions, the powder met a
pre-set repeatability bar (median size within \tbd{x}\%) and its composition
followed the charge to within \tbd{y} wt\%. Step costs spanned
\tbd{four} orders of magnitude. In a \tbd{k}-week campaign the optimizer
stopped \tbd{p}\% of candidates before printing and found \tbd{alloy}, with
\tbd{YS} yield strength and \tbd{EL} elongation and a \tbd{z}\% lower
supply-risk score than Scalmalloy. A digital twin calibrated on the campaign
shows the approach reaches that result at \tbd{k}-fold lower cost than
screen-then-test when the screen misses grain refinement, and matches it when
the screen is right.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} aluminum alloys; laser powder bed fusion;
ultrasonic atomization; self-driving laboratories; Bayesian optimization;
critical minerals

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) builds metal parts with internal channels and
lattices that no other process can make, but it prints few aluminum alloys
well.\cite{aboulkhair2019,kotadia2021} Most high-strength wrought alloys, such
as 7075 and 6061, crack as they solidify in the melt pool,\cite{martin2017,kou2015}
so printed aluminum parts mostly use the casting alloy AlSi10Mg, which is weaker
than the wrought alloys and loses strength quickly above about
\SI{200}{\celsius}.\cite{aboulkhair2019,michi2022} The aluminum alloys that do
print at higher strength rely mostly on grain-refining additions: scandium and
zirconium in Scalmalloy and Al--Mg--Zr alloys, for
example.\cite{schmidtke2011,spierings2017,croteau2018,martin2017} Scandium,
manganese and several other common additions are elements that the United
States imports in full or in large
part,\cite{usgs2025,nassar2020} \tbd{check each import-reliance figure against MCS 2025 before quoting numbers}
so choosing a printable alloy is a supply decision as well as a metallurgical
one.\cite{graedel2012,nassar2021ofr,bauer2023}

Two things make new printable aluminum alloys slow to find. The first is
powder. LPBF needs spherical powder in a narrow size range, typically
\SIrange{15}{63}{\micro\metre}, and gas atomizers make it in lots far larger
than a screening campaign needs;\cite{dawes2015} \tbd{source for typical minimum lot size and lead time}
a custom lot is costly and slow for a composition that may never print.
Campaigns therefore screen candidates by calculation and test only a few, blend
elemental powders and accept inhomogeneity, or move to processes such as
directed energy deposition that tolerate blended feedstock.\cite{nelaturu2024}
Ultrasonic atomization, which breaks up a melt on a vibrating plate, makes
spherical powder from charges of a few hundred grams\cite{rePowder2021,priyadarshi2024}
and was recently used to make powder of a machine-learning-designed aluminum
alloy for LPBF.\cite{taherimousavi2026} Parameter studies show how plate
amplitude and melt conditions set the size of Al and Al--Si powders,\cite{priyadarshi2024,yankin2025}
but run-to-run repeatability at fixed settings has been reported only from three
replicate runs of a refractory alloy,\cite{hinrichs2021} and how well the
powder's composition follows a dosed charge has not been reported for
aluminum.\tbd{re-check before submission; results-needed.md, A6} Both matter if
the atomizer is to be a step that an optimizer calls on demand.

The second is how a campaign spends its budget. The steps from a composition
to a tensile result differ in cost by orders of magnitude: minutes of computing
for a CALPHAD calculation, about a day for a powder batch, days for a tensile
test. Yet campaigns run the steps in a fixed order. A funnel that screens many
compositions by calculation and tests a few is efficient only if the screen is
right, and CALPHAD-based cracking indices, for example, do not capture the grain
refinement by Al$_3$Zr or Al$_3$Sc nucleants that makes Zr- and Sc-modified
alloys printable.\cite{kou2015,martin2017,croteau2018} Sending every candidate
through the whole chain is robust to a wrong screen, but it spends most of the
budget on candidates that a cheap early measurement could have ruled out.

Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) close the loop between choosing experiments
and running them, and they have accelerated discovery of molecules, thin films
and inorganic powders.\cite{tom2024,abolhasani2023,stach2021,tobias2025,burger2020,macleod2020,kusne2020,szymanski2023}
For structural metals they remain rare. Closed loops have optimized refractory
alloys made by arc melting,\cite{paramore2025} multi-principal-element alloys
made by directed energy deposition from purchased powders,\cite{nelaturu2024}
and printed NiCoCr alloys,\cite{talbot2026} and the closest physical
precedent designed an aluminum alloy by composition-level optimization before
atomizing, printing and testing it.\cite{taherimousavi2026} In these studies the
optimizer chooses compositions, and every chosen candidate then goes through a
fixed sequence of steps. Bayesian optimization of function
networks (BOFN) instead models a multi-step workflow as a network of surrogate
models, one per step,\cite{astudillo2021} and its partial-evaluation extension
chooses which step to run, and for which candidate, by the expected gain per
unit cost.\cite{buathong2024,buathong2025} These methods, and related
multi-stage and cost-aware schemes,\cite{torresi2026,alvi2026,snoek2012} have so
far been tested on synthetic or retrospective problems.

Here we close the loop for aluminum alloys in one laboratory and let a
cost-aware optimizer run it. Powder for each candidate is made by ultrasonic
atomization of a dosed charge, characterized, printed on an in-house desktop
LPBF system and tested; CALPHAD calculations, atomization, powder
characterization, printing, microstructure and mechanical tests are the nodes
of a function network, each with a measured cost (\cref{fig:loop}). We show
that (i) ultrasonic atomization makes LPBF-grade aluminum powder repeatably and
with composition that follows the charge, across \tbd{N} runs on \tbd{M}
compositions (\cref{sec:atomize,sec:fidelity}); (ii) a desktop LPBF system
prints these powders densely enough to rank candidates, with a measured offset
from an industrial machine (\cref{sec:print}); (iii) in a \tbd{k}-week campaign
the optimizer chose which step to run for each candidate, stopped \tbd{p}\% of
them before printing, and found \tbd{alloy} (\cref{sec:campaign}); and (iv) a
digital twin calibrated on the campaign shows when this beats screening and
when it does not (\cref{sec:twin}).
\note{Positioning check (outline.md \S3): the novelty claim is the optimizer
choosing among costed steps on a physical alloy workflow, with powder made for
every candidate. It holds only if Fig.~5b shows candidates stopped at different
steps. Neither ultrasonic atomization of a custom Al alloy nor the

\subsection{The loop and what each step costs}
\label{sec:loop}
\note{Claim: per-result costs span about \tbd{four} orders of magnitude, and in-house
powder and printing bring a candidate from composition to a measured cube in
\tbd{d} days, at \tbd{n} candidates per week. Supplied by: step logs in both
campaigns (results-needed.md, L1--L3).}

\Cref{fig:loop}a shows the workflow as the optimizer sees it. Composition enters
twice: at design, where CALPHAD calculations give the freezing range, a crack
susceptibility index, the fraction of strengthening phases and the fraction of
primary Al$_3$(Zr,Sc) available to nucleate grains; and at dosing, where the
charge is weighed out. Laser settings enter at printing. Each step returns
measured outputs that become inputs to the next, and each has its own surrogate
model and its own cost (\cref{tab:costs}). Across \tbd{N} results logged in the
two campaigns, the cost of a single result ranged from \tbd{minutes of computing
and no consumables} for a CALPHAD calculation to \tbd{x person-hours and \$y}
for a set of three tensile specimens, about \tbd{four} orders of magnitude
(\cref{fig:loop}b).\src{L1, L2} A powder run cost \tbd{x} person-hours and
\tbd{\$y}, most of it in \tbd{cleaning and powder handling} rather than in the
pour itself. With the desktop system in the loop, a candidate went from
composition to a measured cube in \tbd{d} days (median; range \tbd{a--b}),
against \tbd{d$'$} days when the cube was printed at an outside facility
(\cref{fig:loop}c), so the lab could take \tbd{n} candidates a week to the
cube stage.\src{L3} These costs are what the optimizer divides by, and their
spread is what makes stopping a candidate early worth anything.

\subsection{Making powder for any composition}
\label{sec:atomize}
\note{Claim: a dosed \SI{250}{\gram} charge gives LPBF-grade powder, and the
spread at fixed settings meets a bar set before the first counted run, once the
melt's landing point is checked before each run. This is where the held-back
atomization data goes. Supplied by: Blocks A and B, every campaign run
(results-needed.md, A1--A6).}

\Cref{fig:atomize}a follows one charge from cup to powder. The first ten runs on
the instrument (Figure~S1)
failed mostly where the melt meets the plate: the stream overshot the plate,
landed on the upper sonotrode, dripped onto a lump frozen on the plate's tip, or
met a plate set in the wrong place, and in one run a hot shot reached and
destroyed the collection cup. Two of the ten produced a usable lot. A run
therefore counted only if the landing point had been checked cold before
heating, the ultrasound was on before the pour, and O$_2$ was at most
\SI{30}{ppm} when the melt was released.

Before the first counted run we fixed what repeatable would mean: at least five
valid runs, with D$_{50}$ varying by at most \SI{10}{\percent} (coefficient of
variation), D$_{10}$ and D$_{90}$ by at most \SI{15}{\percent}, the
\SIrange{15}{45}{\micro\metre} yield by at most 5 percentage points (standard
deviation), and mean circularity by at most 0.02. Under these rules
\tbd{n$_\text{valid}$} of \tbd{n$_\text{attempted}$} runs of the same
\SI{250}{\gram} 6063 charge were valid. Their D$_{50}$ was \tbd{x}~\si{\micro\metre}
(CV \tbd{y}\%), the in-range yield \tbd{z}\% (SD \tbd{s} points) and the mean
circularity \tbd{c} (SD \tbd{s$'$}) (\cref{fig:atomize}b,c);\src{A1, A2}
\tbd{all / k of} the metrics met the bar. Measuring each run's powder in two
splits showed that measurement accounted for \tbd{q}\% of the variance, so the
run-to-run spread belongs to the process. The only replicate study we found, three
runs of a refractory alloy, reported a d$_{90}$ CV of about
\SI{11}{\percent}.\cite{hinrichs2021} Across all \tbd{N} runs, failed ones
included, in-range yield depended mostly on \tbd{plate material and superheat}
and D$_{50}$ on \tbd{amplitude} (\cref{fig:atomize}d),\src{A3, A4} consistent
with capillary-wave breakup on the plate.\cite{lierke1967,lang1962,priyadarshi2024}
Powder oxygen was \tbd{x}~wt ppm and varied by \tbd{y}\% between runs.\src{A5}

\subsection{Composition fidelity from charge to powder to print}
\label{sec:fidelity}
\note{Claim: dosed charges land within \tbd{x} wt\% of target; Si in the powder
follows the charge; Mg loss is \tbd{y} wt\%; EDS agrees with ICP-OES to
\tbd{z} wt\%. The remaining error becomes the composition node's noise.
Supplied by: dosing logs, Block B, ICP-OES, CALIBER (results-needed.md, C1--C4).}

An optimizer that chooses compositions needs the composition it chose to be the
one it gets. Weighed charges differed from their targets by \tbd{x} wt\% at most
(\cref{fig:fidelity}a).\src{C1} To test whether the melt carries a dosed
addition into the powder, we varied the amount of 4047 powder packed into 6063
cups from 0 to \SI{30}{wt\percent}, which spans about \SIrange{1.3}{3.9}{wt\percent}
Si. Measured Si followed the charge with a slope of \tbd{s} (R$^2$ = \tbd{r};
\cref{fig:fidelity}b), and particles from the top, middle and bottom of the
collection jar differed by \tbd{d} wt\% (\cref{fig:fidelity}c), so the powder is
mixed at the scale of a batch and of a particle.\src{C2} Magnesium, the most
volatile addition, was retained at \tbd{x}\% through atomization and \tbd{y}\%
through printing (\cref{fig:fidelity}d).\src{C3} Against ICP-OES, EDS on
particles read Si with a bias of \tbd{b} wt\% and a precision of \tbd{p} wt\%
(\cref{fig:fidelity}e),\src{C4} which sets the noise on the composition node; for
Si-bearing alloys this was \tbd{adequate / the limiting error}.

\subsection{Printing in-house}
\label{sec:print}
\note{Claim: in-house powders print to $\ge$\tbd{99}\% density on the desktop
system, which ranks candidates as an industrial machine does, with a measured
offset. Supplied by: desktop LPBF (\#260), Utah builds on the same powders
(results-needed.md, P1--P5).}

A loop that makes powder in a day gains little if every print waits for a
booking at an outside facility. We built a desktop LPBF system for single tracks
and small cubes (\cref{fig:print}a; \tbd{specifications}).\src{P1} On it,
\tbd{n} in-house powders printed to $\ge$\tbd{99}\% relative density within
\tbd{power and speed window} (\cref{fig:print}b).\src{P2} Because the desktop
system cannot make standard tensile specimens and differs from industrial
machines in spot size, plate heating and atmosphere, we printed the same powders
on an Aconity MIDI. Densities and hardnesses from the two machines ranked the
alloys alike (Spearman $\rho$ = \tbd{r}), with offsets of \tbd{d} in density and
\tbd{h} in hardness (\cref{fig:print}c).\src{P3, P4} Cracking did not follow the
CALPHAD crack index for alloys with Zr\tbd{/Sc}: \tbd{n} of them printed
crack-free despite an index above \tbd{threshold} (\cref{fig:print}d),\src{P5} the
grain refinement by primary Al$_3$Zr that equilibrium and Scheil calculations do
not capture.\cite{martin2017,croteau2018} This is the screen error that decides
whether letting the optimizer skip ahead to a print pays off (\cref{sec:twin}).

\subsection{A campaign in which the optimizer chooses the step}
\label{sec:campaign}
\note{Claim: the optimizer ran CALPHAD on \tbd{a} compositions, made powder for
\tbd{b}, printed \tbd{c} and tensile-tested \tbd{d}; \tbd{p}\% stopped before
printing; it found \tbd{alloy}. Outside checks agree. Supplied by: campaign 2,
with campaign 1 as prior data (results-needed.md, B1--B5, V1--V2).}

The campaign started from \tbd{six} seed compositions and the data of the
first campaign,\tbd{cite the campaign paper} with baselines, target and
stopping rule committed beforehand.\tbd{commit} Over \tbd{k} weeks the optimizer
made \tbd{N} decisions (\cref{fig:campaign}a): \tbd{a} CALPHAD calculations,
\tbd{b} powder runs, \tbd{c} prints and \tbd{d} tensile sets.\src{B2} \tbd{p}\%
of the candidates that reached the powder stage stopped before printing, and
\tbd{q}\% of printed candidates stopped before tensile testing, each because the
expected gain of the next step was lower per unit cost than that of another
candidate's step. Best-so-far performance rose with spend as in
\cref{fig:campaign}b.\src{B3} The best alloy, \tbd{composition}, reached
\tbd{YS}~MPa yield strength, \tbd{UTS}~MPa tensile strength and \tbd{EL}\%
elongation \tbd{as built / after heat treatment} ($n$ = \tbd{n}), against
\tbd{YS$_0$}~MPa for AlSi10Mg printed and tested the same way, at a mineral
burden \tbd{z}\% below Scalmalloy's (\cref{fig:campaign}c,d;
\cref{tab:alloys}).\src{B4, B5} An outside laboratory measured its composition
within \tbd{e} wt\% of ours.\src{V1}

\subsection{When it pays off}
\label{sec:twin}
\note{Claim: a twin fitted to both campaigns reproduces campaign 2's spend
within \tbd{x}\%; cost-aware network optimization reaches the target at
\tbd{k}-fold lower cost than screen-then-test when the screen is wrong, and ties
when it is right. Supplied by: the twin (\#276), refit on lab records
(results-needed.md, T1--T4).}

A physical campaign cannot be repeated often enough to compare strategies, so we
fitted a digital twin of the lab to the records of both campaigns: each step's
outputs, duration, cost, failure rate and noise (\cref{fig:twin}a).\src{T1}
Given campaign 2's decisions, the twin reproduced its spend to the target within
\tbd{x}\%.\src{T4} We then ran each strategy \tbd{20} times per condition. When
the CALPHAD screen missed grain refinement by Zr and Sc nucleants, cost-aware
network optimization reached the target with \tbd{k}-fold less spend than
screening then testing (90\% CI \tbd{lo--hi}); when the screen was right, the
two were within \tbd{x}\% and screening was \tbd{faster/slower}
(\cref{fig:twin}b).\src{T2} The gain grew with the cost ratio between making
powder and running a calculation and vanished below \tbd{r}
(\cref{fig:twin}c).\src{T3} \ifdraft\textit{(The synthetic benchmark in
\cref{fig:twin-draft}, which this panel will replace, gave 3.75-fold, 90\% CI
2.0--5.7, at $\kappa$ = 1 and favoured screening at $\kappa$ = 0; it is
noise-free and uncalibrated.)}\fi

\subsection{Limits}
\label{sec:limits}
\note{Claim: the gain depends on cost ratios and on how wrong the cheap steps
are; desktop coupons rank candidates but do not qualify them. Supplied by: T3,
P4.}
The advantage is conditional. It comes from steps whose cost differs by orders of
magnitude and from a cheap step that is wrong about the alloys that matter; with
an accurate screen, screening is as good or better (\cref{fig:twin}b). The
desktop system ranks candidates but does not qualify them: its coupons are small,
and its offset from an industrial machine (\cref{fig:print}c) must be measured
for each new alloy family. The design space was \tbd{elements and bounds}, and the
mineral-burden score depends on import reliance, which changes from year to year.
\tbd{Room-temperature properties only; no fatigue; one powder route.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\tbd{Write last.} A laboratory that makes its own powder in \SI{250}{\gram} lots
and prints in-house can let an optimizer decide, candidate by candidate, which
result is worth its cost. Here that found \tbd{alloy} after \tbd{N} decisions
and \tbd{\$}, and a twin fitted to the lab shows the approach pays off when the
cheap screen is wrong about the alloys that matter, and not otherwise. The
condition generalizes: an optimizer that can stop a candidate at any step needs
every step to be cheap and fast enough to call on demand, which here meant
bringing powder making and printing into the lab.

\section{Experimental Section}
\label{sec:methods}
\note{Skeleton, filled with what the run records and threads already fix. Values in
red are still to be read from run records rather than plans. Each paragraph ends
with the SOP or script it will cite in the SI.}

\paragraph{Design space and CALPHAD screening.}
Candidate compositions were drawn from Al with additions of \tbd{the frozen
palette: e.g.\ Mn, Cr, Zr, Mg, Si, Cu, Ti, Fe, Ni, Ce, Sc, Er, Zn} within
\tbd{bounds, wt\%; Al $\ge$ 85 wt\%}, excluding \tbd{Si-bearing alloys, if the
composition-measurement limit raised at the campaign kickoff holds}. For each
composition, equilibrium and Scheil--Gulliver solidification were calculated with
\tbd{Thermo-Calc and TCAL\,\textit{n}, or pycalphad with an open Al database}
\cite{thermocalc2002,otis2017} to give the freezing range, the crack
susceptibility index of Kou,\cite{kou2015} the fraction of strengthening
phases, and the fraction of primary L1$_2$ Al$_3$(Zr,Sc) available as
nucleant. Liquidus plus superheat was kept below the induction module's
\SI{1300}{\celsius} limit. \src{campaign 1 screen; SI S12}

\paragraph{Mineral burden.}
Supply risk was scored as the mass-weighted U.S.\ net import reliance of the
additions, $B=\sum_{i\neq\text{Al}} w_i\,\mathrm{NIR}_i/100$, with $w_i$ in wt\%
and NIR$_i$ in \% from the USGS \textit{Mineral Commodity
Summaries};\cite{usgs2025} $B$ is the weight percent of the alloy that the United
States imports, net. This is the definition used for the first
campaign.\tbd{cite the campaign paper; same USGS edition as it uses}
Raw-material cost of the additions and Sc content alone are reported as
sensitivity checks.\cite{nassar2020,graedel2012}

\paragraph{Charge preparation and dosing.}
Each \SI{250 \pm 2}{\gram} charge was a 6063 rod (\diameter\SI{20}{\milli\metre})
bored to hold a core of dosed elemental or master-alloy powder and closed with a
press-fit plug, or bar stock plus master-alloy pieces.\tbd{which, per campaign}
Every part was weighed, and the core powders were dosed with \tbd{doser model}
to \tbd{accuracy}. Charges were stored below \tbd{RH}\% until use.
\src{dosing SOP; charge drawing, byu-vcl PR 232}

\paragraph{Ultrasonic atomization.}
Powders were made in an AMAZEMET rePOWDER ultrasonic atomizer with an induction
melting module and a graphite crucible.\cite{rePowder2021} For Al alloys the melt
was poured through a \SI{0.5}{\milli\metre} \tbd{confirm} nozzle onto a Mo plate
driven through a 1.5:1 booster at \SI{90}{\percent} amplitude; Cu alloys used a
W-coated carbon-fibre plate and a 1:1.5 booster. The chamber was evacuated and
back-filled with argon \tbd{n} times, and a run counted only if O$_2$ was
$\le$\SI{30}{ppm} when the sealing rod was raised, the ultrasound was on before
the pour, and the melt's landing point on the plate had been checked cold
before heating. Melt temperature \tbd{schedule}, hold before the pour
\tbd{min} and pour pressure \tbd{0.19 bar, HMI reading} were fixed for each
block of runs.\cite{priyadarshi2024,lierke1967} Each run's mass balance was
closed in six parts (in-range powder, out-of-range powder, splats, puddle,
skull, unaccounted). \src{atomizer SOP, issues \#251/\#253; run-record template}

\paragraph{Powder characterization.}
Each run's powder was split in two by \tbd{riffler or cone-and-quarter} and
sieved at 250, 63 and \SI{45}{\micro\metre} on sieves used only for Al alloys.
Particle size ($\ge$1,000 particles) and shape ($\ge$400 particles, at
500$\times$) were measured on SEM images following ISO~13322-1 and ISO~9276.
Composition was measured by EDS at \tbd{kV} on particles from the top, middle
and bottom of the collection jar (at \SI{5}{\kilo\volt}, W M$\alpha$ overlaps
Si K$\alpha$ and Fe K is not excited, so W and Fe pick-up were checked in
backscattered images), and by ICP-OES at \tbd{outside laboratory}; oxygen and
nitrogen by inert-gas fusion on sealed \SI{5}{\gram} samples. Flowability was
measured on the doser.\cite{spierings2016flow,slotwinski2014}
\src{powder SEM preparation SOP, byu-vcl PR 284; \texttt{sem\_psd.py}, PR \#226}

\paragraph{Laser powder bed fusion.}
\tbd{Desktop system as built: e.g.\ a \SI{400}{\watt} single-mode
\SI{1070}{\nano\metre} fibre laser with a galvanometer scanner and f-theta lens
(spot \tbd{\SIrange{75}{96}{\micro\metre}}), a \diameter\tbd{30--50}~mm build
plate heated to \SI{200}{\celsius}, argon atmosphere at \tbd{O$_2$ ppm}, layer
camera and coaxial photodiode.} Single tracks and
\SIrange{5}{10}{\milli\metre} cubes were printed over \tbd{power and speed
ranges}, starting for AlSi10Mg-type powders from \SI{255}{\watt},
\SI{1.25}{\metre\per\second}, \SI{0.10}{\milli\metre} hatch and
\SI{30}{\micro\metre} layers.\cite{king2014,tang2017} Reference builds of the
same powders were made on the Aconity MIDI at the University of Utah.
Density was measured by the Archimedes method and by image analysis of polished
sections.\cite{spierings2011} \src{desktop LPBF, issue \#260; SI S10}

\paragraph{Microstructure and mechanical testing.}
Sections were ground and polished \tbd{SOP} and examined by optical microscopy
and SEM/EDS\tbd{/EBSD}. Vickers hardness \tbd{load, dwell, n}. Tensile
specimens \tbd{geometry: e.g.\ the flat \SI{6}{\milli\metre} $\times$ \SI{6}{\milli\metre} reduced section
with \SI{32}{\milli\metre} parallel length used for the AlSi10Mg baseline;
orientation; surface} were tested at room temperature at \tbd{strain rate,
within ASTM E8/E8M}, $n\ge3$ per alloy and condition, beside AlSi10Mg printed
and tested the same way.

\paragraph{Corrosion.}
\tbd{If kept (decision D6): open-circuit potential, linear polarization and a
potentiodynamic scan in \SI{3.5}{wt\percent} NaCl on the CubXL with an EZstat-Pro
potentiostat.}

\paragraph{Function-network model and acquisition.}
Each step (CALPHAD, atomization, powder characterization, printing,
microstructure and density, mechanical tests) was a node with its own Gaussian
process, linked as in the workflow so that a node's outputs are inputs to its
children.\cite{astudillo2021} The next experiment (a candidate and a node whose
parents had already been evaluated) maximized the partial-evaluation knowledge
gradient divided by the node's cost,\cite{buathong2024,buathong2025} with costs
from Table~1. Pending experiments entered as fantasies, a failed step was
modelled at the step where it failed,\tbd{per issue \#274} and the campaign
started from \tbd{six} seed compositions.\tbd{per issue \#275} Models were
implemented in BoTorch.\cite{balandat2020} Baselines (screen-then-test,
full-chain network BO, standard BO and random), the target and the stopping rule
were fixed and committed before the campaign started.\tbd{date, commit}
\src{optimizer code; SI S12--S13}

\paragraph{Digital twin and benchmarks.}
A simulator of the lab produced each step's outputs, duration, cost, noise and
failures (atomization: droplet size from Lang's relation,\cite{lang1962}
usable fraction and failure rate; printing: melt-pool, keyhole and
lack-of-fusion criteria\cite{king2014,tang2017}), with parameters fitted to the
run records of both campaigns.\tbd{fit, validation metric}\cite{mukherjee2019,gunasegaram2021}
Each policy was run for $\ge$\tbd{20} seeds per condition; acceleration factors
are ratios of spend (or days) to reach the target, with matched-seed bootstrap
confidence intervals. \src{twin, issue \#276; SI S14}

\paragraph{Independent verification.}
\tbd{Blind-labelled samples of the finalists went to \tbd{laboratory} for
ICP-OES and O/N/H, and \tbd{if funded} tensile tests.}
