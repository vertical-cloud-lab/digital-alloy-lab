We make small batches of aluminium-alloy powder by ultrasonic atomization (AMAZEMET rePOWDER, induction-heated module). We are deciding how to turn **pure aluminium powder** (gas-atomized, roughly 15-63 µm, alone or blended with alloying-element or master-alloy powders) into **green parts** that we then melt in the atomizer crucible together with solid alloy (6063 rod, 4047). We want a **pros-and-cons comparison of two consolidation routes**, (A) powder-metallurgy pressing under vacuum and (B) cold isostatic pressing (CIP), judged on **safety** first, then simplicity, speed, cost, and whether the green part will melt, coalesce and mix into the rest of the melt. Please answer from the literature, with citations (DOIs where possible), give numbers wherever the sources have them, and say explicitly which conclusions come from direct evidence for aluminium and which are extrapolated from other metals or from general PM practice.

**Set-up.** Graphite crucible (BN-coated), induction heated, 10-11 cm deep, with a sealing (stopper) rod down the middle; the melt is bottom-poured through a ~0.7 mm hBN nozzle onto a 40 kHz vibrating molybdenum plate. The charge is loaded cold. The chamber is then evacuated and back-filled with argon several times ("gas washes"), including while the charge is held at about 500 °C and still solid. It is then melted under argon at about 0.2 bar overpressure (13-20 ppm O2), set point 810-840 °C, held about 2 minutes (we may extend this to 5-10 minutes), with no mechanical stirring beyond the induction field. Charges are 170-350 g. Every piece must be under 20 mm across to fit between the stopper rod and the crucible wall, so a green part would be a slug about 19 mm in diameter and 20-60 mm long (roughly 10-40 g).

**What we already know** (our own runs, and an earlier literature review on 2026-11-02 that you do not need to repeat). Loose gas-atomized powder sealed in a 6063 cup did not consolidate in one run (AlSi10Mg) and did in another (4047); the oxide shell on each particle, plus MgO or spinel when Mg is present, keeps the molten droplets apart. Cold uniaxial pressing of Al powder reaches about 85 % relative density at about 100 MPa and 92-98 % at 250-580 MPa; it cracks the oxide but does not remove it. Pores start to close above roughly 85-90 % density, so a denser compact can seal in air, adsorbed water and water from hydrated oxide. The equipment maker warned that powder compressed inside a closed aluminium cup could eject molten metal, presumably from trapped gas. Die lubricants add gas and carbon. Cast master alloys, or an arc-melted button made from the weighed powders, were ranked above any pressed compact.

**The two routes to compare.**
- **(A) "Powder metallurgy under vacuum":** uniaxial die pressing with the die cavity evacuated before and during pressing (an evacuable die with a vacuum port and O-ring seals, or a die inside a vacuum chamber or glovebox), with or without vacuum drying or degassing of the loose powder beforehand. Please also cover the natural extensions: pressing followed by vacuum sintering or degassing, and vacuum hot pressing (for example 300-550 °C).
- **(B) Cold isostatic pressing (CIP):** powder filled into a flexible elastomer mould (polyurethane, latex, silicone or rubber bag), optionally evacuated and sealed, and pressed hydrostatically (typically 100-400 MPa) by a water-based fluid or oil. Wet-bag versus dry-bag; buying a small laboratory CIP versus sending parts to a toll-pressing service. Also the combination of A and B: powder vacuum-degassed first, sealed in an evacuated bag, then cold isostatically pressed.

**Please address:**

1. **Pros and cons of each route (and its sub-variants) for pure aluminium powder**, ideally as a table covering: process safety; safety of the green part when melted in our crucible; green density achievable versus pressure, and its uniformity (density gradients and low-density zones in uniaxially pressed slugs with height-to-diameter ratios of about 1-3, versus isostatic pressing); green strength, edge integrity and powder shedding during handling; the need for lubricants and their effect; contamination (lubricant residue, bag material, pressing fluid, die wear); dimensional control (our 20 mm limit, CIP shrinkage and shape tolerance); oxide-film disruption (does hydrostatic loading in CIP break the particle oxide films more or less than the shear in a uniaxial die?); residual gas and moisture in the part; simplicity and the skill needed; time per part and per batch; and cost (equipment capital, tooling and consumables, and toll-service pricing if published).
2. **Which route makes the safer green part to melt in the atomizer, and why.** Quantify where possible: adsorbed and chemically bound water on gas-atomized Al powder (mass %, or mL H2 per 100 g Al) and the temperatures at which it is released; the open-to-closed porosity transition for Al compacts; how much evacuating the die or the CIP bag before pressing reduces the trapped gas; and whether an open-pore green compact heated slowly through our vacuum and argon washes up to about 500 °C would degas in place before its pores close. What are the failure modes: a CIP bag leak or puncture that lets water or oil into the powder (and the hydrogen that water generates with freshly exposed Al), lubricant decomposition, trapped air expanding on heating, lamination or end-capping cracks from uniaxial pressing?
3. **Process hazards of each route** when handling fine pure Al powder: combustible-dust and ignition risks while filling dies or bags, drawing fine powder into a vacuum line or pump, venting an evacuated die to air, contact with the CIP fluid, stored energy in the high-pressure vessel, and safe practice for each (inert handling, filters, grounding, PPE).
4. **Melt-in and mixing feasibility.** Would a green part from either route (at what density) melt, coalesce and mix into a 6063 melt at 810-840 °C under argon within 2-10 minutes with only induction stirring? Does CIP versus uniaxial pressing versus vacuum hot pressing change coalescence, metal yield, dross or oxide inclusions on remelting? Use evidence from remelting Al powder compacts, chip or swarf briquettes, PM parts and foundry alloying tablets. If alloying powders (Si, Cu, Mg, Mn, Fe, Ni, Ti, Zr) are blended into the pure Al before pressing: segregation while filling, and exothermic reactions between Al and these powders on heating (for example the self-propagating Al-Ni, Al-Ti, Al-Fe or Al-Zr reactions in pressed compacts, and how compact density affects ignition), and whether these help or hurt safety and dissolution.
5. **A practical recipe for each route** for a 19 mm diameter by 20-60 mm slug: pressure, dwell, evacuation level, drying or degassing temperature and time, lubricant (or none), mould or die material and sizing, expected density, and how to check a part before it goes into the crucible (Archimedes density and open porosity, a weight-loss or gas-release test on heating). What press force, or what CIP vessel size and pressure rating, does this need?
6. **Recommendation:** which route, or neither, would you choose for a university lab making a few such parts per week, and when would each be the wrong choice? Compare briefly with buying cast master alloys or arc-melting a button from the weighed powders.
