Question: We make small batches of aluminium-alloy powder by ultrasonic atomization (AMAZEMET rePOWDER, induction-heated module). We are deciding how to turn **pure aluminium powder** (gas-atomized, roughly 15-63 µm, alone or blended with alloying-element or master-alloy powders) into **green parts** that we then melt in the atomizer crucible together with solid alloy (6063 rod, 4047). We want a **pros-and-cons comparison of two consolidation routes**, (A) powder-metallurgy pressing under vacuum and (B) cold isostatic pressing (CIP), judged on **safety** first, then simplicity, speed, cost, and whether the green part will melt, coalesce and mix into the rest of the melt. Please answer from the literature, with citations (DOIs where possible), give numbers wherever the sources have them, and say explicitly which conclusions come from direct evidence for aluminium and which are extrapolated from other metals or from general PM practice.

**Set-up.** Graphite crucible (BN-coated), induction heated, 10-11 cm deep, with a sealing (stopper) rod down the middle; the melt is bottom-poured through a ~0.7 mm hBN nozzle onto a 40 kHz vibrating molybdenum plate. The charge is loaded cold. The chamber is then evacuated and back-filled with argon several times ("gas washes"), including while the charge is held at about 500 °C and still solid. It is then melted under argon at about 0.2 bar overpressure (13-20 ppm O2), set point 810-840 °C, held about 2 minutes (we may extend this to 5-10 minutes), with no mechanical stirring beyond the induction field. Charges are 170-350 g. Every piece must be under 20 mm across to fit between the stopper rod and the crucible wall, so a green part would be a slug about 19 mm in diameter and 20-60 mm long (roughly 10-40 g).

**What we already know** (our own runs, and an earlier literature review on 2026-11-02 that you do not need to repeat). Loose gas-atomized powder sealed in a 6063 cup did not consolidate in one run (AlSi10Mg) and did in another (4047); the oxide shell on each particle, plus MgO or spinel when Mg is present, keeps the molten droplets apart. Cold uniaxial pressing of Al powder reaches about 85 % relative density at about 100 MPa and 92-98 % at 250-580 MPa; it cracks the oxide but does not remove it. Pores start to close above roughly 85-90 % density, so a denser compact can seal in air, adsorbed water and water from hydrated oxide. The equipment maker warned that powder compressed inside a closed aluminium cup could eject molten metal, presumably from trapped gas. Die lubricants add gas and carbon. Cast master alloys, or an arc-melted button made from the weighed powders, were ranked above any pressed compact.

**The two routes to compare.**
- **(A) "Powder metallurgy under vacuum":** uniaxial die pressing with the die cavity evacuated before and during pressing (an evacuable die with a vacuum port and O-ring seals, or a die inside a vacuum chamber or glovebox), with or without vacuum drying or degassing of the loose powder beforehand. Please also cover the natural extensions: pressing followed by vacuum sintering or degassing, and vacuum hot pressing (for example 300-550 °C).
- **(B) Cold isostatic pressing (CIP):** powder filled into a flexible elastomer mould (polyurethane, latex, silicone or rubber bag), optionally evacuated and sealed, and pressed hydrostatically (typically 100-400 MPa) by a water-based fluid or oil. Wet-bag versus dry-bag; buying a small laboratory CIP versus sending parts to a toll-pressing service. Also the combination of A and B: powder vacuum-degassed first, sealed in an evacuated bag, then cold isostatically pressed.

**Please address:**

1. **Pros and cons of each route (and its sub-variants) for pure aluminium powder**, ideally as a table covering: process safety; safety of the green part when melted in our crucible; green density achievable versus pressure, and its uniformity (density gradients and low-density zones in uniaxially pressed slugs with height-to-diameter ratios of about 1-3, versus isostatic pressing); green strength, edge integrity and powder shedding during handling; the need for lubricants and their effect; contamination (lubricant residue, bag material, pressing fluid, die wear); dimensional control (our 20 mm limit, CIP shrinkage and shape tolerance); oxide-film disruption (does hydrostatic loading in CIP break the particle oxide films more or less than the shear in a uniaxial die?); residual gas and moisture in the part; simplicity and the skill needed; time per part and per batch; and cost (equipment capital, tooling and consumables, and toll-service pricing if published).
2. **Which route makes the safer green part to melt in the atomizer, and why.** Quantify where possible: adsorbed and chemically bound water on gas-atomized Al powder (mass %, or mL H2 per 100 g Al) and the temperatures at which it is released; the open-to-closed porosity transition for Al compacts; how much evacuating the die or the CIP bag before pressing reduces the trapped gas; and whether an open-pore green compact heated slowly through our vacuum and argon washes up to about 500 °C would degas in place before its pores close. What are the failure modes: a CIP bag leak or puncture that lets water or oil into the powder (and the hydrogen that water generates with freshly exposed Al), lubricant decomposition, trapped air expanding on heating, lamination or end-capping cracks from uniaxial pressing?
3. **Process hazards of each route** when handling fine pure Al powder: combustible-dust and ignition risks while filling dies or bags, drawing fine powder into a vacuum line or pump, venting an evacuated die to air, contact with the CIP fluid, stored energy in the high-pressure vessel, and safe practice for each (inert handling, filters, grounding, PPE).
4. **Melt-in and mixing feasibility.** Would a green part from either route (at what density) melt, coalesce and mix into a 6063 melt at 810-840 °C under argon within 2-10 minutes with only induction stirring? Does CIP versus uniaxial pressing versus vacuum hot pressing change coalescence, metal yield, dross or oxide inclusions on remelting? Use evidence from remelting Al powder compacts, chip or swarf briquettes, PM parts and foundry alloying tablets. If alloying powders (Si, Cu, Mg, Mn, Fe, Ni, Ti, Zr) are blended into the pure Al before pressing: segregation while filling, and exothermic reactions between Al and these powders on heating (for example the self-propagating Al-Ni, Al-Ti, Al-Fe or Al-Zr reactions in pressed compacts, and how compact density affects ignition), and whether these help or hurt safety and dissolution.
5. **A practical recipe for each route** for a 19 mm diameter by 20-60 mm slug: pressure, dwell, evacuation level, drying or degassing temperature and time, lubricant (or none), mould or die material and sizing, expected density, and how to check a part before it goes into the crucible (Archimedes density and open porosity, a weight-loss or gas-release test on heating). What press force, or what CIP vessel size and pressure rating, does this need?
6. **Recommendation:** which route, or neither, would you choose for a university lab making a few such parts per week, and when would each be the wrong choice? Compare briefly with buying cast master alloys or arc-melting a button from the weighed powders.


# Consolidating aluminium powder for an induction-heated atomizer

**Recommendation.** For a few charges per week, use cast master alloys or an arc-melted button where possible. If a powder-derived slug is necessary, I would trial **small, lubricant-free, vacuum-dried, moderately die-pressed slugs**, provided they pass handling, open-porosity and heating tests. I would **not buy a CIP solely for this purpose**. Outsourced CIP is reasonable if die-pressed slugs shed powder or crack, but only with a documented, completely dry bag process. Neither route has been demonstrated to give reliable coalescence and composition control in *this* 170–350 g, bottom-poured atomizer within 2–10 minutes. This recommendation is an engineering inference, not the outcome of a head-to-head melt trial. (puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 5-7, chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 47-52, estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 6-8)

## What the experiments establish—and do not

Direct studies of **gas-atomized pure Al** establish moisture-dependent gas release and substantial CIP densification. A study of Al-containing *alloy blends* compares evacuated-bag CIP with die pressing; studies of *machining-chip briquettes* examine remelting in substantially larger induction charges. Those last two bodies of evidence cannot establish powder-slug metal yield or melt time in your crucible. No located experiment directly compares vacuum-die-pressed and CIP-pressed pure-Al slugs during remelting, or measures oxide-film fracture by the two routes at matched green density. (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 1-2, eksi2007effectofsintering pages 1-2, chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 47-52, puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 5-7, chua2014developmentofaluminumf pages 43-47)

| Criterion | **A. Evacuated uniaxial die; preferably dry powder** | **B. Evacuated-bag wet-bag CIP** |
|---|---|---|
| **Most important melt-safety trade-off** | Avoids any pressing liquid near the charge. Evacuating the loaded die reduces *free air* if communication with the vacuum port persists, but does not remove oxide-bound water; a long, dense slug can retain closed pockets. Capping or laminations can expose loose powder or create poorly characterized cavities. | More nearly uniform compression and potentially fewer low-density zones, but **bag puncture, imperfect seal or wet powder is a distinct reject condition**. Evacuation similarly removes accessible free gas, not chemically bound moisture; very high density may obstruct later outgassing. These are mechanism-based judgments, not measured comparative incident rates. (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 1-2, chua2014developmentofaluminumd pages 31-34, chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 43-47) |
| **Density and uniformity** | The approximate 85% at 100 MPa and 92–98% at 250–580 MPa in the question are useful *prior observations*, not promises for a 19 mm × 60 mm slug. Die-wall friction makes the region away from a single punch and the central/lower zones particularly uncertain at height/diameter ≈1–3; opposed punches or shorter slugs improve the geometry. | Direct Al evidence: CIP of a larger, 106–150 µm gas-atomized Al fraction achieved **98% green density at 600 MPa** (initial packing **48%**); a separate pure-Al/Al–Ni study reports **>95%** for pure Al within its explored range up to 1,000 MPa. Neither result guarantees a particular density at **200–300 MPa** for your finer powder. Evacuated-bag CIP of alloy blends had higher green density than die pressing across **100–400 MPa**, but did **not** eliminate all integrity problems: low-pressure PM7075 CIP bodies were too fragile. (eksi2007effectofsintering pages 1-2, andasmas2011phenomenologicalstudyof pages 2-4, chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 47-52, chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 52-57) |
| **Strength, edges, size and contamination** | Precise die diameter and comparatively rapid repetition; edges may chip during ejection. Die-wall lubricant can assist ejection but introduces a potential gas/carbon source; avoid admixed wax for melt feed. Die or punch wear may contaminate the charge. | Pressure on all sides generally reduces die-wall gradients and needs no die-wall lubricant, but flexible tooling gives poorer as-pressed dimensional control, possible rounded ends, bag residue and fluid-ingress risk. Measure *every* maximum diameter before loading. Neither method guarantees adequate green edge strength without a trial. Chua's comparable alloy-blend CIP used **1.5 wt% wax** and an additional **420 °C** de-lubrication hold: its sintering outcomes are not evidence that wax is harmless in your short melt. (kim1998effectoffriction pages 1-2, meluch2010warmcompactionof pages 51-58, chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 43-47, chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 47-52) |
| **Oxide and later melt-in** | Particle deformation and local shear can break oxide at contacts, without removing its oxygen. | Hydrostatic *external* loading still rearranges and plastically deforms Al particles: it does **not** leave all oxide intact. Conversely, uniform pressure alone is not proof of *more* oxide rupture than die shear at equal density. Pure Al subjected to a different process—combined high radial pressure **plus axial deformation**—reached **99.3–99.9%** but still had amorphous Al₂O₃ at particle boundaries. Neither cold route guarantees a single coalesced drop on heating. (andasmas2011phenomenologicalstudyof pages 2-4, wei2011fulldensityconsolidation pages 2-4) |
| **Workflow and cost** | Existing rated hydraulic press and a suitable die are potentially the least equipment-intensive option; vacuum seals, a dust-safe port and controlled ejection add design and operator work. A short press stroke is fast, but powder preparation, evacuation and inspection dominate a small batch. | Wet-bag: inexpensive replaceable moulds, but filling, evacuating, individually sealing, pressurizing, controlled depressurization, drying and removing bags take longer. Dry-bag CIP keeps the mould installed and can suit repetitive production, but offers little evident advantage for a few slugs weekly. Buying a rated vessel adds high-pressure maintenance and institutional approval; outsourcing avoids that capital expenditure but adds transport, lead time and contamination-control documentation. **No defensible small-laboratory machine price or toll price was located; obtain quotations rather than inventing per-slug costs.** (meluch2010warmcompactionof pages 51-58, vicenzi2020powdermetallurgyin pages 4-5, chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 43-47) |

**Process variants.** Evacuating a die during pressing is principally *air management*, whereas drying loose powder under vacuum *before* pressing also addresses removable moisture. Pressing followed by **vacuum degassing** can remove gases only while pores communicate with the outside; subsequent vacuum **sintering** may make a more robust body but can close those paths and preserve oxide. In an Al-alloy study at **590 °C**, vacuum (<10⁻² torr) sintered better than argon but less than nitrogen; the authors calculate that ordinary vacuum cannot reduce Al₂O₃. These are sintering results, **not** evidence of superior remelting. Vacuum hot pressing is a substantially different, slower route: an Al-matrix powder-composite study first CIPed at **280 MPa**, then used approximately **10⁻⁵ torr and 550 °C**, soaking **6 h** and pressing **5 h at 175–245 MPa**; its results cannot be transferred directly to pure Al or a short atomizer hold. It may produce a strong, dense billet while reducing opportunities for gas escape if heating/degassing precedes pore closure inadequately. **Do not put an elastomer bag in either furnace or crucible.** (schaffer2006theeffectof pages 1-2, schaffer2006theeffectof pages 2-4, tanga2004purealmatrix pages 2-3)

## Which compact is safer to melt?

**A verified dry, sufficiently coherent *and demonstrably gas-permeable* compact is preferable to an unverified higher-density one, regardless of press type.** For otherwise comparable preparations, die pressing removes the CIP-fluid-ingress failure mode; that is why I rank it first for this small lab. Conversely, if a long die-pressed slug caps, laminates or sheds powder, a sound, dry CIP compact can be the safer *part*. No published pure-Al experiment provides a universal **85–90% density pore-closure threshold**. In fact, powder-processing guidance commonly limits a preform intended for efficient *subsequent vacuum degassing* to about **75% density**, and an Al–20Si study deliberately prepressed to about **65%** to retain interconnected pores. This is not a mandate to feed a fragile 65%-dense slug into your atomizer: it shows that **bulk density is not a permeability test**. A compact at 85%, 90% or above may still have open and/or isolated pores depending on powder and pressing path. (chua2014developmentofaluminumd pages 31-34, estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 6-8)

In the most directly applicable moisture experiment, **99.99% pure, approximately 25 µm gas-atomized Al** exposed for **24 h** to humid air released H₂O beginning near **480 K (207 °C)**, peaking near **720 K (447 °C)**; H₂ rose around **620–660 K (347–387 °C)** and peaked near **725 K (452 °C)**. Dry-air and high-purity-Ar exposures did not give detectable H₂ in that test. Heating was **5 K/min under 10⁻³ Pa**. The measured water-adsorption-derived surface area was **0.125 m²/g**. This study **does not supply a water mass percent or H₂ volume** for your powder. Its temperature-programmed H₂O and H₂ traces are relevant evidence for why a deliberate, slow ~500 °C solid-state stage *could* help, rather than proof that your actual wash sequence has completely outgassed every slug. (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 1-2, yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 2-4, yamasaki2006changesinthe media 9836cbd3, yamasaki2006changesinthe media 9a4cdf6a)

For scale only, separate **air-atomized Al–20Si-based alloy** powders contained **13.09–24.58 ppm H**, reported as **14.66–27.53 cm³ H₂ per 100 g powder** upon analysis at 550 °C. These values **are not measurements of pure gas-atomized Al** and should not be used as its specification. Water physically adsorbed on surfaces, water in hydrated oxide/hydroxide and H₂ produced when that water reacts with newly exposed Al or Mg are different inventories. An alloy-powder experiment found that **12–72 h room-temperature vacuum pumping** reduced physically bound water; higher-temperature release persisted. Thus, a die/bag vacuum can markedly lower the *initial connected-pore air inventory* but cannot be assigned a measured reduction in total evolved gas without a heating test. (estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 6-8, davis1995degassingandsurface pages 3-5)

For an **illustrative ideal-gas calculation**, if 10 cm³ of connected void space is sealed at 1 bar and 20 °C, its air would reach roughly **3.7 bar absolute at 810 °C** at unchanged volume; evacuating that space to **1 mbar before sealing** would reduce its *initial air molecule count* about **1,000-fold**. If it remains connected through the 500 °C evacuation, it need not be trapped at all. Real pore volumes and pressures change, and newly generated H₂/H₂O can dominate either calculation. At **0.057 torr ≈7.6 Pa**, Chua demonstrated that evacuation of an *alloy-powder* CIP bag is feasible, but did not demonstrate zero residual gas after compaction. Water entering a failed wet bag is especially consequential: stoichiometrically **1 g H₂O can produce about 1.24 L H₂ at 0 °C, 1 atm** through 2Al + 3H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂, *if the reaction proceeds completely*. It is not a prediction of the actual kinetics in a passivated slug. Reject suspected wet or oily parts; do not attempt to “dry them safe” by assuming a specified oven time. (chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 43-47, yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 2-4, estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 5-6)

**Your preheat is helpful, with conditions:** charge the slug cold and unsealed, keep a genuine evacuation/venting path through the temperature range above, allow pressure and gas-release signals to settle at ~500 °C, and avoid a rapid jump into melting. An Ar *backfill* alone is not active vacuum extraction, and a closed pore or an enclosed cup cannot be repaired by gas washes. Additional failure modes include wet powder turning into steam/H₂, lubricant/bag residue decomposing, pressure release from sealed voids, die-press capping or lamination, and green fragments entering the bottom outlet. The small **0.7 mm nozzle** makes loose powder and oxide-rich fragments an operational concern even if no violent expulsion occurs. The assessment of this exact atomizer is necessarily extrapolated. (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 2-4, estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 6-8, chua2014developmentofaluminumd pages 63-67)

## Powder-handling and pressing hazards

A passivated **15–63 µm Al powder is not intrinsically non-combustible**: suspension, air, ignition and confinement make a dust-deflagration scenario during die/bag filling, evacuation, leaks or venting. Published Al-dust tests include representative **KSt 515 bar·m/s**, **Pmax 11.2 bar** and ignition energy **<10 mJ**; these depend on powder and test conditions and **must not be taken as specifications for your lot**. Inerting, grounding, avoiding airborne clouds and controlling deposited dust therefore matter more than the mere use of vacuum. Inert gas also presents an asphyxiation hazard. (taveau2019metaldustsexplosion pages 1-2, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 3-4, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 2-3)

Use approved small-quantity procedures: transfer and blend in a dry inert enclosure if available; bond/ground conductive equipment; avoid sparks, frictional heating and compressed-air cleanup; use eye/face protection and suitable dust PPE under the institutional dust-hazard assessment. Place a **powder-rated, inspectable capture/filter arrangement ahead of the vacuum pump**, prevent a filter or pump from becoming a confined Al-dust accumulator, and **vent a die slowly with dry inert gas**, not a blast of room air. Obtain the site's appropriate combustible-metal firefighting procedure; do not assume that applying water to an Al-powder fire is safe. These are precautions extrapolated from metal-dust hazard literature, not a validated design drawing for your vacuum line. Wet-bag CIP adds bag inspection, segregated clean fluid, vessel guarding, rated hoses/fittings, controlled depressurization, trained operators and pressure-vessel inspection; **a conventional hydraulic press is not a substitute for a certified CIP vessel**. (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 3-4, taveau2019metaldustsexplosion pages 2-5, kim1998effectoffriction pages 1-2)

## Will it coalesce and mix in 2–10 minutes?

**Al metal will exceed its melting point; full particle coalescence and alloy assimilation are the unproved steps.** In a directly relevant *but much larger* study, compacted Al-alloy **machining swarf**, immersed into an initial molten pool and induction melted under Ar near **850 °C**, required about **50–70 min**, not 2–10 min. At one favorable condition (approximately **15 kg charge**, **33% initial molten ingot**, **300 bar chip-briquette compaction**) metal incorporation was **88–91%**; raising chip-compaction pressure to **500 bar** lowered incorporation to **75–78%**, as penetration/coalescence worsened. Chip briquettes contained **2.42–2.83% moisture** and have different oxide area, cleanliness, porosity and geometry from your dry fine powder: neither their times nor their yields predict yours. Induction stirring improved swarf recovery over a comparatively static furnace, showing that magnetic stirring is useful, **not that this small induction field will break every powder-particle oxide network**. (puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 2-3, puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 5-7, puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 4-5, puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 3-4)

Thus neither higher CIP density nor vacuum hot pressing should be sold as a proven melt-yield improvement. A coherent slug immersed among 6063/4047 solid pieces and surrounded by an early molten pool is a more promising trial than an exposed powder-rich raft; a **5–10 min hold** offers more opportunity than **2 min**, with possible costs in Mg loss, dross and contamination. First run **pure-Al-only** slugs, quantify recovered charge chemistry and inspect a quenched/recovered crucible residue and nozzle. Increase hold time or change feed form only on observed evidence; do not assume an unseen slug has assimilated. Pressing density should be optimized against **handling, gas escape and remelt recovery**, not maximized in isolation. These are proposed tests, not literature-validated performance predictions for your machine. (puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 5-7, schaffer2006theeffectof pages 2-4, chua2014developmentofaluminumd pages 63-67)

Blending introduces a separate chemical risk. Filling can segregate dissimilar size/density powders; CIP's more uniform *pressure* does not remix an already segregated charge. Si/Cu-bearing additions may become incorporated in the melt, whereas Mg can oxidize or be lost and Mn/Fe/Ni/Ti/Zr additions can leave slowly assimilating particles or intermetallics; confirm each with chemical analyses and residue inspection. Crucially, **near-stoichiometric reactive mixtures are not ordinary dilute master-alloy additions**: pressed Ti–Al mixtures showed a first exotherm around **520–570 °C**, while a Ni–Al combustion-synthesis review places commonly triggered thermal explosion around **640 °C**. An Al–Ti–C compact had density-dependent ignition as low as **~450 °C**, but was a *different, TiC-forming composition*. These data justify separate thermal screening of Al–Ni, Al–Ti and other concentrated, intimately mixed blends before your 500 °C wash; they **do not demonstrate self-propagation of dilute Fe/Ni/Ti/Zr additions in excess Al**. Avoid mechanical activation of an uncharacterized reactive mixture and do not count on its exotherm to improve dissolution. (yi1992effectofheating pages 2-4, morsi2026reviewcombustionsynthesis pages 2-4, chaubey2010effectofcompact pages 2-4, li2008assimilationofmnal pages 49-53)

## Small-batch trial specifications—not validated safe operating limits

For **19 mm diameter**, cross-sectional area is **2.835 cm²**; at **2.70 g/cm³** fully dense Al, a **20–60 mm** cylinder contains **15.3–45.9 g**. At **85–90% density**, it contains about **13.0–41.3 g**: consequently a desired **40 g, 20 mm-long** pure-Al slug is geometrically impossible, while **40 g at 90%** needs approximately **58 mm** length. The nominal **19 mm** diameter leaves just **1 mm** below a 20 mm entry limit; specify clearance against the *actual rod–wall gap*, not simply the diameter of the crucible. These figures are geometry calculations, not experimental yields.

| Trial | Starting procedure and expected outcome | Equipment and release checks |
|---|---|---|
| **A: die/vacuum** | Begin with **pure Al only**, with no admixed binder/lubricant. Consider an off-line loose-powder vacuum drying trial at **120–150 °C for several hours**, cooled and transferred under dry Ar; this is a **provisional operating trial**, not proven to remove bound hydroxide. Use a clean rigid **~19 mm** die, preferably opposed punches, and start **100–200 MPa**, **~30–60 s** at pressure, with staged filling or shorter slugs if a long part caps. Evacuate the connected cavity before/during compression; **≤10 mbar absolute** is a practical *suggested target*, not a demonstrated gas-removal threshold. Increase pressure only if handling fails and heating/permeability tests remain satisfactory. Expected relative density: **measure it**; the stated ~85% at 100 MPa is a baseline from your prior review, not a guarantee. | Force = pressure × area: **28.4 kN at 100 MPa; 56.7 kN at 200; 85.1 kN at 300; 113.4 kN at 400 MPa**, excluding ejection/tooling allowances. Select and guard a die and load frame rated above the actual operating loads. No lubricant is preferred; if essential, trial a *minimal external die-wall treatment* with separately qualified heating/gas results, not a wax addition. Reject caps, laminations and shedding. (chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 47-52, chua2014developmentofaluminumd pages 63-67) |
| **B: wet-bag CIP** | Vacuum-dry the loose powder as above; put it into a **clean, dry, inspected polyurethane or comparable compatible mould**, evacuate without drawing powder into the line, and seal. Published Al-alloy work evacuated a rubber bag to **5.7 × 10⁻² torr (~7.6 Pa)**, a feasibility example rather than a necessary set point. Trial **200–300 MPa**, hold **~1 min**, then depressurize slowly. *Do not claim a numerical green density until tested*: the direct **98% at 600 MPa** Al result used coarser powder. Aim for sound handling while retaining measurable venting; do not treat ≥95% as the target. Remove all bag material before heating. (chua2014developmentofaluminum pages 43-47, eksi2007effectofsintering pages 1-2, andasmas2011phenomenologicalstudyof pages 2-4) | For a ~19 mm × 60 mm finished part, a **roughly 25–35 mm inside-diameter, ~80–100 mm-long mould** and a vessel accommodating several such filled moulds are *starting estimates*, **not validated shrinkage/tooling dimensions**. Establish fill mass and final diameter experimentally, accounting for mould wall, closures, deformation and press manufacturer's clearances; buy only a certified **≥300 MPa rated** system if repeated demand warrants it. Reject *any* suspected liquid ingress, staining or retained elastomer. |

For either route, record powder batch, composition, time exposed to air, mass, dimensions and visible defects. **Geometric mass/volume density** is a first screening measure for a regular green cylinder; a conventional unprotected **water-Archimedes immersion is inappropriate as routine release testing** for an open-pore Al green part intended to stay dry. Measure open porosity on a *sacrificial* specimen with a validated compatible method; a separate pressure-decay or inert-gas-flow test can establish connectivity, but a flow result is not itself a moisture assay. Conduct a **sacrificial, instrumented heating/weight-loss or evolved-gas test through ~550 °C** on representative slugs, including H₂O/H₂ if possible, under a reviewed safe procedure; compare with the loose starting powder and set an empirical acceptance limit *before* exposing the atomizer. Do not return a water-immersed or otherwise wetted test piece to service. The same tests should check whether the proposed 500 °C hold actually outgasses the *centre* of the longest slug. (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 1-2, davis1995degassingandsurface pages 3-5, estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 6-8, chua2014developmentofaluminumd pages 31-34)

**Decision rule:** choose **neither** if dry, sound, permeable slugs cannot be made and validated, if a powder blend has an uncharacterized pre-melt exotherm, or if a test melt leaves oxide-clad powder or obstructs the stopper/nozzle. Prefer a certified cast master alloy or pre-alloyed/arc-melted, inspected button where its composition and handling are suitable: those alternatives avoid asking cold compaction to solve oxide-film coalescence and gas-removal problems it has not been shown to solve in this machine. (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 2-4, puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 5-7, yi1992effectofheating pages 2-4)
