| Criterion | Route A — Vacuum uniaxial pressing | Route B — Cold isostatic pressing (CIP) | Preferred |
|---|---|---|---|
| Process safety during pressing | Powder can remain in an evacuable rigid die or inert glovebox, limiting transfers. Hazards include dust entrainment into the vacuum line, electrostatic ignition, pinch loads, and dust release during venting. Reported Al-powder values span MIE <1–13 mJ, MEC 45–170 g/m³, KSt 162–1100 bar·m/s, and Pmax 8–12.9 bar. Use grounded conductive equipment, filtered vacuum lines, and slow inert-gas venting. (pqac-00000022, pqac-00000023, pqac-00000029) | Adds high-pressure stored energy and more powder handling. A punctured wet bag can expose Al to water or oil. Water contacting exposed Al can generate hydrogen by 2Al + 3H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂. Use an evacuated, leak-tested, double-contained bag and an oil rather than water medium where practicable. (pqac-00000022, pqac-00000054, pqac-00000057) | **A**, if engineered for combustible metal powder. |
| Safety of green part during remelting | Evacuating the loose bed before pressing removes much free pore gas, but high-density pressing can isolate remaining air, water, or lubricant. A lubricant-free, measurably open-pore compact that is slowly heated under dynamic vacuum is preferable to a nearly closed, un-degassed compact. | An evacuated dry bag can remove free gas before densification and CIP avoids weak low-density zones. A leaking wet bag can introduce water or oil; a sealed but non-evacuated bag merely compresses the original gas. | **A by default**; **B only with an evacuated, dry, leak-tested bag**. |
| Green density achievable | Direct Al-alloy evidence: single-action pressing produced about 2.14 g/cm³ at 100 MPa and 2.57 g/cm³ at 500 MPa. That powder contained lubricant, so it is not a pure-Al calibration. Vacuum changes gas inventory more than the pressure–density curve. (pqac-00000053) | Direct Al evidence: initial packing near 48% theoretical density reached 98% at 600 MPa; other pure-Al/Al–Ni work exceeded 95% at sufficiently high pressure. Typical CIP practice is 200–400 MPa. (pqac-00000001, pqac-00000002, pqac-00000054) | **B** for maximum density, although maximum density before degassing is not the safest target. |
| Density uniformity | Die-wall friction attenuates pressure. Single-action pressing leaves the remote end least dense; double-action pressing moves the minimum toward mid-height. In Cu–Al compacts, raising H/D from 0.7 to 2.12 increased maximum relative-density difference from 0.0851 to 0.1206; application to pure Al is an extrapolation. (pqac-00000046, pqac-00000048, pqac-00000052) | Hydrostatic loading and the moving elastomer wall provide much better uniformity. Direct Al–Ni/pure-Al work found that a 1 min pressure dwell produced good homogeneity, whereas immediate decompression produced a dense surface and lower-density core. (pqac-00000001, pqac-00000005) | **B**, especially for long slugs with H/D around 2–3. |
| Green strength, edge integrity, and shedding | Good at adequate pressure, but tall slugs are vulnerable to laminations, end-capping, weak central regions, and ejection damage. Rigid tooling gives clean edges. | Usually higher and more uniform handling strength, without rigid-die ejection. Bag wrinkles and rounded edges are possible; slow decompression may be needed to prevent springback cracking. (pqac-00000054, pqac-00000058) | **B** for handling; **A** for precise edges. |
| Lubricant need and contamination | Die friction encourages lubricant use, but internal wax or stearate adds carbon and gas. For remelting, use no admixed lubricant; if unavoidable, apply the minimum external die-wall film and validate its removal. | Normally needs no internal lubricant because the bag moves with the powder, avoiding wax decomposition and carbon pickup. | **B**. |
| Bag or pressure-fluid contamination | No bag or pressure fluid. Possible contamination comes from die wear, vacuum grease, O-rings, pump backstreaming, or external die lubricant. | Possible elastomer fragments, mould-release residue, oil permeation, or catastrophic fluid ingress. Literature describes rubber, neoprene, or urethane moulds with water, oil, glycerine, or boron oil. (pqac-00000054, pqac-00000055, pqac-00000057) | **A**. |
| Oxide-film disruption | Axial plastic flow and die-wall shear can crack native oxide locally, but do not remove it. | Hydrostatic compression deforms particles but produces less macroscopic shear. It probably fractures oxide at contacts, but direct evidence that ordinary CIP disrupts pure-Al oxide more effectively than die pressing was not found. | **A, slightly**, for cold oxide cracking; the distinction is uncertain. |
| Residual gas and moisture | Direct evacuation of the loose bed removes free interstitial gas. Al powders have shown about 13–25 ppm H, equivalent to approximately 14.7–27.5 cm³ H₂ per 100 g, mainly associated with water on the oxide. Physisorbed water evolves mainly near 100–200°C; hydroxide-related water and H₂ evolve broadly through 200–450°C. Hydrated oxide transforms near 350°C (623 K). (pqac-00000006, pqac-00000008, pqac-00000011, pqac-00000067, pqac-00000069) | Evacuating the bag removes free gas but not oxide-bound water. Fluid exposure can add moisture after drying. A non-evacuated bag traps the initial gas inventory. | **A**, unless B uses vacuum-degassed powder and dry evacuated-bag sealing. |
| Dimensional control and 20 mm limit | Best control. A nominal 19 mm rigid die produces a near-final diameter; springback should be calibrated and every slug checked with a go/no-go gauge. | Flexible-tool shrinkage depends on fill density, bag stiffness, closure geometry, and powder flow. Curvature, taper, and wrinkles are possible, and oversize mould dimensions must be established experimentally. (pqac-00000054, pqac-00000056) | **A**. |
| Simplicity and skill | Straightforward if a guarded press exists, although vacuum ports, filters, inert venting, seals, and preferably double-action tooling add complexity. | Wet-bag CIP requires bag filling, evacuation, sealing, vessel loading, pressure cycling, controlled decompression, stripping, and leak inspection. Dry-bag equipment is faster but difficult to justify for a few parts weekly. | **A** in a university laboratory; toll CIP is preferable to buying a unit for trials. |
| Time per part or batch | Filling, evacuation, pressing, decompression, and ejection can take minutes. Several short slugs are easier than one H/D≈3 slug. Separate thermal degassing dominates total time. | Pressure hold may be seconds to 1 min, but bag preparation and vessel cycling dominate; decompression may take several minutes. Several bags can be processed together. (pqac-00000001, pqac-00000054) | **A** for one-offs; **B** for batches. |
| Equipment capital and consumables | Lowest likely capital if a hydraulic press is available. Requires a hardened die, punches, vacuum hardware, filtration, and guarded/inert venting. Rigid tooling has low recurring consumable cost. | A 200–400 MPa vessel, intensifier, interlocked enclosure, inspection program, and disposable bags impose substantial capital and operating cost. Wet-bag tooling is cheaper but labor intensive. No reliable published toll-service price was found. | **A**; use toll CIP for occasional development work. |
| Vacuum sintering or post-press degassing | A lubricant-free compact can be heated under vacuum while pores remain connected, but vacuum sintering alone cannot remove stable Al₂O₃. Degassing near 350°C is supported for hydrated-oxide conversion; higher temperatures risk pore closure and volatile-alloy-element loss. (pqac-00000010, pqac-00000038, pqac-00000040) | Post-CIP degassing is possible, but very high density can reduce gas pathways. Degassing loose powder before CIP is preferable. | **A**, or pre-degassed evacuated-bag CIP. |
| Vacuum hot pressing extension | Direct Al-composite evidence: about 90%-dense CIP preforms were hot pressed at 550°C, 10⁻⁵ Torr, and 175–245 MPa for 5 h, producing essentially full density. This improves bonding and degassing but is slow and unnecessary for simple remelt feed. (pqac-00000064, pqac-00000065) | CIP can provide a uniform preform before vacuum hot pressing, but this is no longer a simple cold-CIP route. | **Neither for routine feedstock**. |
| Overall recommendation | Best practical cold route for a few parts per week: lubricant-free, double-action vacuum die pressing of short slugs after controlled vacuum drying or degassing, targeting adequate strength with retained open porosity rather than maximum density. | Best cold route for density uniformity and low shedding, but only with pre-degassed powder, a dry evacuated double bag, controlled decompression, and rejection after any suspected leak or fluid contact. | **A over B**, but cast master alloy or an arc-melted button remains safer and more reliable than either green compact. |
