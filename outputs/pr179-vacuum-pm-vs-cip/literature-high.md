### 2. Which Route Produces the Safer Green Part to Melt, and Why

**Adsorbed and chemically bound water.** Gas-atomized aluminium powder carries an amorphous hydrated oxide layer approximately 2–10 nm thick (kowalski1992somenewaspects pages 2-4, kowalski1992somenewaspects pages 1-2). Total hydrogen content is typically 13–25 ppm (by weight), equivalent to approximately 14.7–27.5 cm³ H₂ per 100 g of powder, which is 50–100 times higher than that of a degassed casting (estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 6-8). Nearly all of this hydrogen originates from water that is physically adsorbed or chemically bonded as hydroxides in the surface oxide layer (kowalski1992somenewaspects pages 2-4). Physisorbed water is released broadly around 100–200 °C; water associated with hydroxide decomposition (Al₂O₃·3H₂O → Al₂O₃·H₂O → γ-Al₂O₃) evolves over approximately 150–500 °C, with a major transition near 350 °C (623 K) (estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 5-6, yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 2-4, yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 4-4). Hydrogen from the reaction 2Al + 3H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂ evolves sharply above approximately 350–400 °C and peaks near 450 °C (725 K for pure Al) (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 2-4, kowalski1992somenewaspects pages 10-11). The optimum vacuum degassing temperature for Al powder consolidation is identified as 623 K (350 °C); for applications requiring minimal blistering, temperatures above 673 K (400 °C) are recommended (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 4-4).

**Open-to-closed porosity transition.** As aluminium compacts are pressed above approximately 85–90% relative density, pores begin to disconnect and become closed, trapping whatever gas remains inside (meluch2010warmcompactionof pages 45-51). This is the critical safety threshold: a compact pressed to, say, 95% density without prior degassing can seal in air, adsorbed water, and hydroxide-derived hydrogen. When subsequently heated past 350–450 °C, water reacts with freshly exposed aluminium to generate hydrogen gas in a sealed cavity, potentially building enough pressure to eject molten metal—consistent with the equipment maker's warning.

**Evacuating the die versus evacuating the CIP bag.** Evacuating the die cavity to ≤10 mbar before pressing removes approximately 99% of the equilibrated free interstitial gas (atmospheric pressure to 10 mbar represents a 100-fold reduction). However, evacuation does not remove oxide-bound water, which requires thermal treatment. A CIP bag can also be evacuated before sealing, but a sealed but non-evacuated bag merely compresses the original gas inventory. A wet-bag CIP leak introduces water or oil directly into the powder, potentially exposing freshly cracked aluminium surfaces to water—a severe hydrogen-generation hazard (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 6-7).

**Degassing during the vacuum/argon-wash cycle.** An open-pore green compact (below roughly 85–90% density) heated slowly through the vacuum and argon washes up to 500 °C would indeed lose most of its physisorbed and hydroxide-bound water before pore closure occurs at the melting point, because the dehydration temperatures (100–500 °C) fall well below the aluminium solidus (660 °C). The interconnected porosity at 80–88% density permits gas transport to external surfaces under dynamic vacuum. This is a significant safety advantage over a nearly fully dense compact that cannot degas in situ.

**Failure modes.** Route A's primary failure modes are: (i) pressing too densely without prior degassing, sealing gas inside; (ii) lamination or end-capping cracks from ejection, creating weak zones that may fragment unpredictably during heating; (iii) lubricant decomposition producing gas and carbon if an admixed lubricant is used; and (iv) re-adsorption of moisture between pressing and crucible loading. Route B adds: (v) CIP bag leak or puncture that admits water or oil to the powder, generating hydrogen on freshly exposed Al surfaces; (vi) residual bag or mould-release material contributing organic contamination; and (vii) high-pressure stored-energy release in the event of vessel failure. For the specific application of remelting, **Route A (vacuum-assisted uniaxial pressing) produces the safer green part**, provided the compact is pressed lubricant-free to a moderate density that retains open porosity, and the powder is pre-degassed or the compact is degassed in the atomizer's vacuum/argon-wash cycle before melting.

### 3. Process Hazards

**Aluminium dust explosion characteristics.** Aluminium powder in the 15–63 µm range is classified in Dust Hazard Class 3 (St3), with reported explosion parameters including: minimum ignition energy (MIE) as low as <1 mJ and typically 3–13 mJ; minimum explosible concentration (MEC) of 45–170 g/m³; deflagration index (K_St) of 162–1100 bar·m/s depending on particle size, shape, and test conditions; and maximum explosion pressure (P_max) of 8–12.9 bar (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 6-7, taveau2019metaldustsexplosion pages 1-2, benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 3-5, cadwallader2003dustcombustionsafety pages 34-38). Metal dust fires cannot be fought with water, CO₂, or nitrogen; only Class D dry-powder agents, dry sand, or dry sodium chloride should be used (cheremisinoff2014dustexplosionand pages 229-232).

**Route A hazards.** Filling an open die generates airborne dust; drawing fine powder into a vacuum line can deposit it in the pump and downstream piping; venting an evacuated die to air disperses fine dust. Mitigation: fill in a fume hood or glovebox under argon; use sintered-metal filters on the vacuum line with an isolation valve; ground all conductive components; vent slowly with dry argon rather than air; wear appropriate PPE including conductive anti-static clothing, safety glasses, and a P3 dust respirator (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 2-3, cheremisinoff2014dustexplosionand pages 59-63).

**Route B hazards.** CIP adds high-pressure stored energy (a 25 mm bore × 100 mm long vessel at 300 MPa stores substantial elastic energy in the fluid and vessel), requiring a certified vessel, pressure relief, and barricade or blast containment. Bag filling disperses dust; a punctured bag in a water-based system brings water into contact with freshly cracked aluminium powder, generating hydrogen. Mitigation: use oil-based or boron-oil fluid rather than water; double-bag the powder in a glovebox; leak-test the bag before insertion; operate the CIP behind a blast screen; inspect bags and seals after every cycle (eksi2007effectofsintering pages 1-2, kim1998effectoffriction pages 1-2).

**Safe practices common to both routes:** inert-atmosphere handling (argon glovebox or bag-in/bag-out procedure); electrical grounding of all equipment and the operator; anti-static, conductive flooring; filtered local exhaust ventilation; no compressed-air cleaning; housekeeping to prevent dust accumulation; approved storage in sealed conductive containers away from ignition sources (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 2-3, cheremisinoff2014dustexplosionand pages 59-63).

### 4. Melt-In and Mixing Feasibility

**Remelting evidence.** No study was found that directly reports remelting a pressed pure-aluminium-powder compact into an aluminium melt under the specific conditions described. However, closely analogous evidence exists from aluminium chip/swarf briquettes. Cold-pressed Al–Si–Mg chip briquettes (density 2.29 g/cm³, 12.1% porosity) remelted in an induction furnace at approximately 850 °C achieved a metallurgical yield of 94.2%, with only 2.25% dross, and the resulting casting was chemically homogeneous with no significant segregation (baron2026characterizationofcoldpressed pages 18-20, baron2026characterizationofcoldpressed pages 16-18, baron2026characterizationofcoldpressed pages 20-21). Puga et al. (2009) found that Al swarf briquettes incorporated at 81–85% under argon at 850 °C, with induction melting outperforming resistance melting (83–85% vs 63–69% incorporation), and dross at 10–14% under argon versus 27–35% without protection (puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 4-5). Higher melting temperatures (up to 950 °C) slightly improved incorporation and reduced melting time from 65 to 50 minutes for their charge sizes.

**Applicability to pressed powder compacts.** A pressed powder compact differs from a chip briquette in having a much higher specific oxide area (each 15–63 µm particle is individually oxide-coated), but the oxide thickness is only 2–10 nm (kowalski1992somenewaspects pages 2-4), much thinner than the heavily oxidised machining chips. In the user's crucible at 810–840 °C under argon with induction stirring, a 10–40 g compact submerged in a 170–350 g melt should heat through its 19 mm cross-section within approximately 1–2 minutes and melt within the 2–10 minute hold. The induction field provides electromagnetic stirring that, while not mechanical, promotes convective mixing. A compact at 85–90% density with open porosity will fragment as aluminium melts and wets inward, liberating individual oxide skins as fine γ-Al₂O₃ particles that should float to the surface as dross. By analogy with briquette remelting, metal yield of approximately 90–95% is a reasonable expectation for a pre-degassed, lubricant-free compact under argon, with the principal loss being oxide-skin dross (extrapolated from chip-briquette data). Achieving yield at the higher end of this range requires minimising re-oxidation during loading and melting.

**CIP versus uniaxial versus vacuum hot pressing.** The consolidation method primarily affects remelting through its influence on residual gas and porosity distribution. A uniformly porous CIP compact and a uniaxially pressed compact with some density gradient should both melt and coalesce, but the CIP compact may break up more uniformly. A vacuum-hot-pressed compact approaching full density would melt in the same manner as a solid piece of aluminium—no gas ejection risk—but vacuum hot pressing at 550 °C and 175–245 MPa for hours is disproportionate effort for a simple remelt feedstock (tanga2004purealmatrix pages 2-3, tanga2004purealmatrix pages 3-5).

**Alloying-powder blends: segregation and exothermic reactions.** If Si, Cu, Mn, Fe, Ni, Ti, or Zr powders are blended into the Al before pressing, segregation during die filling (density- and size-driven) must be controlled by careful blending and incremental filling. More critically, binary Al–Ni compacts can undergo self-propagating high-temperature synthesis (SHS) with ignition near 640 °C for Ni+Al→Ni₃Al, well below the atomizer melt temperature of 810–840 °C (morsi2026reviewcombustionsynthesis pages 6-7). Al–Ti compacts have a lower exothermicity (adiabatic temperature ~1518 K for TiAl) and may not self-sustain without preheating, but the onset of solid-state reaction is composition- and density-dependent (adeli2016theselfpropagatinghigh pages 1-3, adeli2016theselfpropagatinghigh pages 3-6). The optimal compact green density for SHS is approximately 60–70% of theoretical; higher density improves particle contact and can raise combustion temperature (adeli2016theselfpropagatinghigh pages 3-6, morsi2026reviewcombustionsynthesis pages 6-7). Moisture in compacts promotes porosity and can slow or disrupt the combustion wave (morsi2026reviewcombustionsynthesis pages 9-10). For the user's application, SHS reactions are a potential safety hazard during the slow heating ramp: if the compact reaches the ignition temperature before being submerged in the melt, a runaway exotherm could eject material. **Use of Ni, Ti, Fe, or Zr elemental powders blended with Al in a pressed compact is therefore strongly discouraged for this application.** Master-alloy powders or pre-alloyed powders, where the elements are already in solid solution or as stable intermetallics, do not support SHS and are safer.

### 6. Recommendation

For a university laboratory making a few 19 mm × 20–60 mm aluminium-powder slugs per week for remelting in an ultrasonic atomizer:

**Route A (vacuum-assisted uniaxial die pressing) is recommended over Route B (CIP).** Route A is simpler, requires less capital (a guarded hydraulic press, hardened die, and vacuum hardware versus a certified high-pressure CIP vessel and ancillaries), introduces no fluid-contact failure mode, and provides better dimensional control for the tight 19 mm clearance. With lubricant-free pressing at 200–300 MPa after vacuum drying of the powder, targeting 85–90% theoretical density with retained open porosity, the compact can degas in situ during the atomizer's vacuum and argon-wash cycles before its pores close at the melting point. The compact should be pressed as short slugs (H/D ≤ 1.5, i.e. ≤ 28 mm long) using double-action punches to minimise density gradients (wang2018numericalsimulationof pages 16-22, beck2012onanovel pages 20-26). Every slug should be verified by Archimedes density and a go/no-go gauge before loading.

**When Route A is the wrong choice:** (i) if a tall slug (H/D > 2) is required and density uniformity is critical—CIP is then better for structural uniformity, but use pre-degassed powder in a dry evacuated bag; (ii) if the laboratory already has access to a CIP facility or toll-pressing service and not a suitable press, sending occasional parts out for CIP may be faster than building tooling in-house.

**When Route B is the wrong choice:** (i) if no CIP facility is available and the cost of a vessel is not justified for a few parts per week; (ii) if only water-based CIP fluid is available, because any bag leak exposes Al powder to water; (iii) if the operator cannot reliably verify bag integrity and vacuum seal before and after pressing.

**When neither route is the best choice:** Cast master alloys or an arc-melted button from the weighed elemental charges remain safer and more reliable than any green pressed compact. A fully pre-melted and solidified charge piece contains no trapped gas, no adsorbed water, no individual particle oxide shells, and no risk of powder ejection—it simply remelts. The trade-off is that arc melting requires an arc furnace and produces only one composition per melt, while pressing is a room-temperature process that can incorporate custom powder blends. For standard alloy compositions (6063, 4047) where commercial master alloys are available, the recommendation is to **prefer cast or arc-melted charge pieces over either pressed-powder route**, and to reserve powder pressing for compositions where no suitable master alloy exists and where the added risks can be justified and managed.

### Evidence Basis and Limitations

Direct aluminium-specific evidence is available for: green density versus pressure in CIP (eksi2007effectofsintering pages 1-2, andasmas2011phenomenologicalstudyof pages 2-4); degassing temperatures and hydrogen content of gas-atomised Al powder (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 2-4, yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 4-4, estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 6-8, kowalski1992somenewaspects pages 2-4, estrada1991gasentrapmentand pages 5-6, kowalski1992somenewaspects pages 7-8); Al-powder explosion parameters (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 6-7, taveau2019metaldustsexplosion pages 1-2, benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 3-5); SHS ignition in Al–Ni and Al–Ti compacts (morsi2026reviewcombustionsynthesis pages 6-7, adeli2016theselfpropagatinghigh pages 3-6); vacuum hot pressing of Al composites (tanga2004purealmatrix pages 2-3, tanga2004purealmatrix pages 3-5); and remelting of Al chip briquettes (puga2009recyclingofaluminium pages 4-5, baron2026characterizationofcoldpressed pages 18-20, baron2026characterizationofcoldpressed pages 16-18). Density-gradient data for uniaxially pressed compacts at various H/D ratios are extrapolated from Cu–Al mixed-powder simulations (wang2018numericalsimulationof pages 16-22, wang2018numericalsimulationof pages 6-9); general CIP descriptions are from PM textbook-level sources applied to aluminium (meluch2010warmcompactionof pages 51-58, beck2012onanovel pages 20-26); and the specific behavior of a pressed Al-powder green compact during remelting in a small induction-heated crucible has not been directly studied in the published literature and is inferred from briquette remelting studies and PM degassing work. The oxide-disruption mechanism in CIP versus uniaxial pressing for pure aluminium powder has not been experimentally compared in any study identified in this review; the conclusion that uniaxial pressing introduces slightly more shear-induced oxide cracking is a reasonable inference from general PM principles but is not validated for this system.
