# Classification of Aluminium Alloy Powder for LPBF: Achieving a 15–25 µm Lower Cut in a University Laboratory

## 1. Context and the Core Challenge

The AMAZEMET rePOWDER ultrasonic atomizer operating at approximately 40 kHz produces highly spherical aluminium alloy powders with a relatively narrow particle size distribution (PSD). Published data on similar systems report number-based D50 values of approximately 40–50 µm and volume-based D50 values of approximately 50–100 µm, with the majority of the powder below 100 µm (ciftcli2025alloydevelopmentfrom pages 3-6, zach2023sphericalpowderscontrol pages 1-2). The standard PBF-LB/M-relevant fraction is 20–63 µm, as used in multiple published studies (pennacchio2026effectofprocess pages 92-97, cui2023gasatomizationof pages 2-4, cui2023gasatomizationof pages 4-5). LPBF suppliers typically provide aluminium-alloy powders in the 15–45 µm range (munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 1-3). Achieving a lower cut at 15–25 µm from 200–500 g lots is the central challenge, because dry sieving methods become deficient below 45 µm (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4).

## 2. Fine Woven-Wire Test Sieves (No. 635 / 20 µm and No. 450 / 32 µm) on a Mechanical Shaker

### Charge Limits, Throughput, and Standards

Per the Handbook of Non-Ferrous Metal Powders, the standard sieve analysis procedure (ISO 4497, ASTM B 214) uses a powder charge of 100–300 g on a sieve stack placed on a shaker with rotary and tapping action, sieving for 15–20 min (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). DIN 66165 Part 2 and ISO 2591-1 specify similar procedures for analytical test sieving and describe endpoint criteria based on mass change per unit time (typically <0.2% of the initial charge passing in an additional minute of sieving).

For 200 mm (8 in) sieves at 20 or 32 µm, the recommended analytical charge per fine sieve is typically 25–75 g, well below the maximum for coarser meshes, to prevent overloading and blinding.

### Blinding and Sieving Aids

At apertures below 45 µm, dry sieving becomes "deficient" regardless of sieve type (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). The main problems are: (a) overloading causes near-mesh particles to wedge into openings; (b) electrostatic charges cause fine particles to adhere to each other and to larger particles, forming agglomerates; and (c) irregularly shaped particles may pass only in certain orientations (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). Recommended aids include commercial antistatic agents, rubber sieving balls or cubes to provide percussion and clearing action, and ultrasonic washing with a wetting agent if brushing fails to clear embedded particles (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). Micromesh sieves (photo-etched electroformed nickel, per ASTM E 11-87, with ±2 µm tolerance from 125 to 5 µm) can be used on an air-jet sieve to extend the operational range down to approximately 10 µm (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4).

### Practical Assessment for 15–25 µm

A 20 µm woven-wire sieve on a tapping shaker would experience severe blinding from the Al-Si powder's 40–60 µm median fraction, with near-size particles rapidly plugging the mesh. A 32 µm sieve is marginal. For a 200–500 g batch, multiple passes at reduced charge (25–75 g per pass) with interim cleaning would be needed, giving a throughput of roughly 50–200 g/h. Sieve damage and shortened mesh life are significant concerns at these fine apertures, as damaged mesh openings distort and can stretch (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4).

## 3. Wet Sieving of Aluminium Powder

### Oxidation and Hydrogen Evolution

Aluminium reacts with water, evolving hydrogen gas and forming aluminium hydroxide. Published chemical surface treatment of AA2017 powder with NaOH and water documented hydrogen evolution and dramatic oxygen increases from 0.166 wt% in gas-atomized powder to approximately 3.65–3.69 wt% after aqueous treatment (batistao2026surfacefunctionalizationof pages 85-90). Drying subsequently converts hydroxide to oxide, permanently altering the surface chemistry.

### Solvent Choice

For aluminium sedimentation analysis, the Handbook of Non-Ferrous Metal Powders lists water (pH 3–4), cyclohexanol, cyclohexanone, isopropanol, and vegetable oil as suitable suspension liquids, with sodium tartrate (0.1 wt%) as dispersant (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 4-6). For wet sieving, isopropanol or ethanol would avoid the hydrogen-evolution risk of water.

### Drying and Oxygen Pickup

Vacuum drying at 80 °C for 48 h has been used after wet treatment of Al powder (batistao2026surfacefunctionalizationof pages 69-73). Even controlled drying can accelerate oxide-layer growth: one study showed that furnace or vacuum drying of AlSi10Mg powder caused oxide thickness to increase from approximately 4 nm to 38 nm over reuse cycles, while bulk oxygen content rose from 500 ppm to 1250 ppm (lopes2026recyclabilityinmetal pages 9-11). Post-drying agglomeration can cause significant additional losses; after resieving, reported overall yields were only 69–78% (batistao2026surfacefunctionalizationof pages 69-73).

### LPBF Printing of Wet-Sieved Al

No published study was identified in which conventionally wet-sieved aluminium alloy powder was subsequently used for LPBF printing. The oxidation risk makes this approach unsuitable for producing qualified LPBF feedstock without rigorous oxygen analysis and process validation.

## 4. Air-Jet Sieves and Sonic Sifters

### Air-Jet Sieve (Hosokawa Alpine e200 LS)

The Alpine air-jet sieve uses a vacuum exhaust coupled with a rotating blowback nozzle that redistributes powder and disintegrates agglomerates, permitting sieving down to approximately 10 µm (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). The Alpine 200LS-N has been used in pharmaceutical and food-science PSD analysis for fine fractions below 50 µm (as documented in granule-size analysis studies). Standard analytical sample mass is approximately 10–50 g per run, with a typical 3–10 min cycle. For preparative separation of 200–500 g, 10–50 repetitive cycles would be required, making this instrument impractical for routine production but valuable for verifying a cut or processing small development samples.

**Critically for aluminium dust safety,** the air-jet sieve's vacuum system presents a significant hazard. The vacuum cleaner motor, filter, and collection container must be rated for combustible metal dust. Standard laboratory vacuum cleaners are ignition sources. Grounding of all components and use of conductive collection vessels are mandatory.

### Sonic Sifter (ATM/Advantech L3P)

The sonic sifter uses oscillating air columns and mechanical pulses rather than continuous airflow. Its analytical capacity is typically 5–20 g per run. For 200–500 g, it would require dozens of cycles. The equipment can achieve cuts down to approximately 20 µm with appropriate meshes, but it is fundamentally an analytical instrument.

## 5. Benchtop Air Classifiers

### Published AM-Powder Classification Examples

Air classification is the technically preferred method for sharp fine cuts. Two key documented examples from the powder-bed-fusion literature:

- **Cui et al. (2023)** used a **Hosokawa Alpine Multiprocess 50 ATP** to remove fine particles below approximately 20 µm from gas-atomized duplex stainless steel powder, followed by mechanical sieving at 63 µm to produce a 20–63 µm PBF-LB/M fraction (cui2023gasatomizationof pages 2-4).

- **Spurek et al. (2021)** used a **Hosokawa Alpine air classifier** together with ultrasonic sieves to produce six monomodal 316L powder distributions with D50 values ranging from 10 to 60 µm and narrow widths of approximately 15 µm (D90–D10), specifically for PBF research (spurek2022influenceofthe pages 2-4, spurek2022influenceofthe pages 1-2).

### Operational Parameters

The Hosokawa Alpine 50 ATP / 100 MZR class systems can classify from approximately 2 to 100 µm. Once stabilized, throughput can reach several kg/h for typical powders. However, for 200–500 g batches, set-up, purging, and cleaning dominate the process time, and equipment hold-up losses (feeder, classifier rotor, cyclone, filter) can consume 5–20% or more of a sub-kilogram lot. This makes them best suited for batches exceeding approximately 1 kg.

### Inert-Gas Operation and ATEX/NFPA Compliance

Nitrogen or argon closed-loop operation is available on Hosokawa Alpine systems and is **mandatory** for aluminium powder classification. Aluminium dust in the relevant size range is classified as **St 3 (highly explosive)**: with a 29 µm mass-median diameter, it exhibits a minimum explosible concentration (MEC) of approximately 30 g/m³, maximum explosion pressure (Pmax) of 12.4 bar, and Kst of 415 bar·m/s (sezerUnknownyearalümi̇nyumtozpatlamalarinin pages 212-216). Fine aluminium powders have extremely low minimum ignition energies (MIE) of 3–4 mJ, making electrostatic discharge a credible ignition source (sezerUnknownyearalümi̇nyumtozpatlamalarinin pages 208-212). The limiting oxygen concentration (LOC) is approximately 10–12% (sezerUnknownyearalümi̇nyumtozpatlamalarinin pages 208-212). Any air classifier processing aluminium must be ATEX Zone 20 or NFPA 484 compliant, with all components grounded, inerted below LOC, and equipped with explosion-proof collection systems.

## 6. Toll Classification of Small Lots

Commercial toll classifiers (Hosokawa Alpine, AVEKA, Elcan Industries, Tekna) typically require minimum lots of 5–50 kg. A 200–500 g lot is far below any standard commercial minimum. Equipment hold-up alone on a pilot classifier may consume 50–200 g. The primary barrier is not technical capability but economic: setup, cleaning, qualification, and documentation costs are disproportionate to the lot size. A toll provider would need a micro-pilot or bench-scale classifier and a written mass-balance/recovery commitment for such small quantities.

## 7. Hand Sieving vs. Mechanical Shaker at 45 and 63 µm

### Standards Requirements

The standard sieve analysis procedures for metal powders (ISO 4497, ASTM B 214, MPIF Standard 05) specify a **mechanical shaker with rotary and tapping action** (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). ASTM B 214 does not endorse hand sieving as an equivalent method. DIN 66165 similarly requires controlled mechanical agitation with defined amplitude and frequency. In published AM-powder preparation, AA2017 powder was mechanically sieved for 30 min using a vibrator with sieves from 20 to 250 µm (batistao2026surfacefunctionalizationof pages 69-73); AlSi7Mg0.6 powder was sieved after each LPBF build cycle using standardized procedures (smolina2022influenceofthe pages 6-8).

### Hand Sieving Limitations

Hand sieving at 63 µm is marginally acceptable for spherical powder whose median is well above 63 µm, since most particles easily fall through. However, with the described Al-Si powder (smallest particles mostly 40–60 µm, median roughly 40–50 µm), the 45 µm sieve performs a near-median cut where sieving completeness is most sensitive to agitation intensity and duration. Hand sieving cannot provide the reproducible rotary-plus-tapping motion needed, resulting in:
- Poor completeness: 10–30% of near-mesh particles may be retained that should pass
- No objective endpoint criterion
- Uneven charge distribution and inconsistent particle reorientation
- Lower recovery and higher variability between operators

### Recommendation

**A mechanical sieve shaker is essential**, at minimum for the 45 µm sieve, and strongly recommended for any standardized operation at 63 µm. A Ro-Tap type (rotary + tapping, per ASTM B 214) is the standard for metal powders. Vibratory 3D shakers (e.g., Retsch AS 200) are also effective and widely used in AM-powder studies. For a university lab with 200 mm sieves, a compact vibratory shaker handling 1–3 sieves would be sufficient, at a cost of approximately $3,000–8,000.

## 8. Aluminium Dust Safety for All Options

### Explosion Characteristics

Aluminium dust is among the most hazardous combustible dusts. Key parameters for fine Al powder in the relevant 15–50 µm range (sezerUnknownyearalümi̇nyumtozpatlamalarinin pages 212-216, sezerUnknownyearalümi̇nyumtozpatlamalarinin pages 208-212):
- **Dust explosion class: St 3** (most severe)
- **Kst: 300–415+ bar·m/s** (depending on particle size and morphology)
- **Pmax: 10–12+ bar**
- **MIE: 3–10 mJ** (comparable to a static-electricity spark from a person)
- **MEC: approximately 30–60 g/m³** (a thin haze, easily achieved during pouring or sieving)
- **LOC: 10–12%** (requires robust inerting)

### Safety Requirements for Each Option

- **Dry sieving (test sieves or shaker):** Ground all equipment, the shaker, sieve frames, and the operator (conductive footwear, wrist strap). Use an enclosed sieve stack with a lid and receiver. Avoid vigorous open-air transfers. Work in a well-ventilated area or fume hood. Clean up all spills immediately (lee2016particletechnologyand pages 4-7, lee2016particletechnologyand pages 1-4).

- **Wet sieving:** Eliminates airborne-dust and static risks but introduces solvent-flammability and Al–water reactivity hazards. Never use water with Al powder. IPA or ethanol are flammable; ventilation and spark-free environments are essential (kaya2026calculationsforhazardous pages 41-45, batistao2026surfacefunctionalizationof pages 85-90).

- **Air-jet sieve:** The integral vacuum system is a major concern. Standard vacuum cleaners have brush motors (sparking) and non-conductive plastic components. An Al-rated collection system with conductive grounded components is required. The fines fraction is concentrated in the vacuum stream (lee2016particletechnologyand pages 4-7).

- **Air classifier:** Must operate in inert gas (N₂ or Ar) at O₂ below LOC (~10%). Collection cyclone and filter must be ATEX-rated. Grounding of all components is mandatory (sezerUnknownyearalümi̇nyumtozpatlamalarinin pages 212-216, lee2016particletechnologyand pages 4-7).

- **General lab handling per NFPA 484 / NFPA 652:** Housekeeping to prevent dust accumulation > 1/32 in (0.8 mm); bonding and grounding of all conductive equipment and containers; no open ignition sources; local exhaust ventilation at dust-release points; conductive containers for powder storage and transfer; moisture control to avoid H₂ generation from water-reactive metals (lee2016particletechnologyand pages 4-7, lee2016particletechnologyand pages 1-4, kaya2026calculationsforhazardous pages 41-45).

## 10. Recommendation for a Lab Making 100–250 g per Run

### Primary Recommendation: Relax the Lower Cut to 20 µm and Use a Two-Stage Approach

Given the constraints (100–250 g per atomization run, no glovebox, 200 mm sieves, university budget), the most realistic approach is:

**Stage 1 — Upper cut with a mechanical sieve shaker (essential purchase):** Acquire a compact vibratory or tapping shaker for 200 mm sieves. Use the 63 µm and 45 µm sieves on the shaker with 100–200 g charges and 15–30 min cycles, per ISO 4497 / ASTM B 214 (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). This reliably produces the upper cut at 45 or 63 µm. For 200–500 g lots, run 1–3 passes. This is the minimum necessary investment and addresses the question of whether a shaker is needed: **yes, unequivocally.**

**Stage 2 — Lower cut:** The 15–25 µm lower cut is the difficult part. Given the SEM evidence that the smallest particles from the rePOWDER are mostly 40–60 µm, the fraction below 20 µm is likely very small (<2–5% by volume, based on the nickel-aluminium bronze data provided). Therefore:

- **If the sub-20 µm fraction is negligible (<2% by volume as measured by laser diffraction):** A lower cut may not be necessary. Simply use the 45 µm or 63 µm upper sieve and accept the full fines content for initial LPBF trials. Measure the PSD by laser diffraction to confirm.

- **If a lower cut is required:** The most practical laboratory-scale option is a **32 µm woven-wire or micromesh sieve on the same shaker**, used with small charges (25–50 g), sieving aids (rubber balls, antistatic spray), and extended sieving times (20–30 min per pass). This will not produce a sharp 32 µm cut—the transition zone will span roughly 20–45 µm—but it will remove the majority of sub-25 µm fines. Expect throughput of approximately 50–150 g/h and losses of 2–5%.

- **For a true 15–20 µm cut:** This requires access to an **air classifier (Hosokawa Alpine 50 ATP or equivalent) operating under inert gas**. This is best arranged through collaboration with a university or research institute that has such equipment, rather than purchasing one (cost >€50,000, plus ATEX compliance). Alternatively, toll classification can be attempted if lots of 2–5 kg can be accumulated. The documented approach of Cui et al. (2023), who used an Alpine 50 ATP to remove <20 µm fines followed by 63 µm sieving (cui2023gasatomizationof pages 2-4), is the closest published workflow to what this lab needs.

### What Not to Do

- **Do not wet-sieve Al powder in water:** Hydrogen evolution and severe oxidation render the powder unsuitable for LPBF (batistao2026surfacefunctionalizationof pages 85-90).
- **Do not use a standard vacuum cleaner with an air-jet sieve for Al powder:** This is an explosion risk (lee2016particletechnologyand pages 4-7).
- **Do not rely on hand sieving at 45 µm:** It is unreproducible and standards-noncompliant for near-median cuts (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4).

### Practical Starting Point

For immediate use: purchase a sieve shaker (~$3,000–8,000), stack the 250 µm / 63 µm / 45 µm sieves, process the 100–250 g rePOWDER output in a single shaker cycle (15–20 min), collect the −45 µm fraction, and measure its PSD by laser diffraction. If the sub-20 µm content is <2–3%, proceed to LPBF trials with the 20–45 µm window. If further classification is needed, add a 25 or 32 µm sieve and reduce the charge per pass. Ground all equipment, work in a ventilated area, and follow NFPA 484 dust-handling precautions throughout.
