| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages | Best suited for | Risk of nozzle clogging |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loose powder in cup (current approach) | Simple weighing; cup keeps powder together initially; convenient for composition screening. | Powder bed is thermally insulating; each particle retains a refractory oxide shell; the cup may melt before its contents coalesce, releasing floating agglomerates. Hydrous oxide, moisture, and trapped gas can evolve suddenly. Mg-bearing powder can additionally contain MgO/MgAl₂O₄. | Only low-oxide, readily melting pre-alloyed powder already demonstrated to consolidate—e.g., the successful 4047 case—and only after a separate melt test. | **High.** Oxide films and clusters can range from micrometres to millimetres, whereas the nozzle is only 0.7 mm; powder-derived Al₂O₃, MgO, or spinel may remain suspended (pqac-00000003, pqac-00000028, pqac-00000053). |
| Cold-pressed freestanding compact | Raises bulk density, thermal conductivity, strength, and probability of submergence; particle deformation cracks some oxide films. Aluminium powders reached about 94.5–97.0% relative density under load at 246–386 MPa in warm-compaction tests. | Pressing cracks but does not remove oxide; closed porosity can trap gas. Excessive density can inhibit melt infiltration, while low pressure gives weak compacts. No direct evidence guarantees complete dissolution during a 2 min hold. | Initial screening of 4047/6063 or Al-rich blends; use moderate density rather than maximum density, heat from cold beneath solid charge, and validate before atomization. | **Medium–high.** Better than loose powder, but all particle-surface oxide remains in the charge as fragments or networks; filtration literature targets inclusions as small as 1–30 µm (pqac-00000010, pqac-00000047, pqac-00000049). |
| Sintered, vacuum-hot-pressed, or SPS compact | Strongest and most conductive powder-derived slug; heat plus pressure increases oxide rupture and metal-to-metal bonding. SPS at 500 °C gave 92.5–98.4% density at 20–50 MPa. Vacuum processing can desorb gas before pores close. | More equipment and process development; oxide is dispersed, not eliminated. Near-full density can retain closed gas if degassing is inadequate. Conventional sintering alone is difficult because alumina inhibits diffusion. | When powder chemistry cannot be obtained as a cast master alloy and a repeatable, mechanically robust slug is essential; vacuum hot pressing is preferable to lubricant-bearing green pressing. | **Medium.** Lower risk of releasing intact powder agglomerates, but fractured Al₂O₃/MgO/spinel remains as inclusions and can reach the nozzle (pqac-00000015, pqac-00000041, pqac-00000046). |
| Commercial master-alloy additions (e.g., Al–50Si, Al–20Si, Al–50Mg, Al–50Cu) | Cast metallic pieces melt or dissolve much more predictably than powder; low surface-area-to-volume ratio minimizes oxide introduced per gram of alloying element. Al–50Si has been used experimentally to make Al–1Si through Al–8Si melts at 850 °C. | Limited available chemistries and minimum order quantities; mass additions become small for trace targets; volatile Mg still needs controlled handling, and high-solute master alloys can segregate if incompletely dissolved. | **Preferred route** for Si, Mg, Cu and other elements available in suitable master alloys, especially for 100–350 g charges requiring low oxygen and short processing time. | **Low**, provided pieces are clean, dry, completely dissolved, and the melt is sampled before pouring (pqac-00000057). |
| Pre-alloyed powder or wire | Pre-alloying removes the need for long-range dissolution of separate elemental particles; wire has much less oxide area and can be submerged readily. Pre-alloyed atomization feed also gives the most reliable nominal composition. | Pre-alloyed powder still has one oxide shell per particle and may reproduce the consolidation problem; custom wire or powder can be costly. | Wire or rod for commercially available chemistries; pre-alloyed powder only if it is first consolidated or converted into a dense master button. | **Low for clean wire/rod; medium–high for powder.** Powder retains high oxide area even when chemically homogeneous (pqac-00000003, pqac-00000046). |
| Arc-melted master-alloy button | Converts a weighed powder blend into a dense, inspectable button before it enters the atomizer crucible; repeated flipping/remelting can improve button homogeneity; eliminates loose powder and most open porosity. | Requires an arc melter; Mg and other volatile elements may be lost; reactive elements may oxidize or react with the hearth; segregation and composition loss must be checked analytically. This recommendation is an engineering extrapolation rather than direct evidence from the cited aluminium-remelting studies. | Small experimental additions of Cu, Mn, Fe, Ni, Zr or Sc where no suitable commercial master alloy exists; especially attractive when preserving the atomizer nozzle is more important than minimizing preparation steps. | **Low–medium.** Much lower than a green compact if the button is sound and surface oxide is removed, but refractory reaction products can remain. |
| Higher superheat and longer hold time | Increases heat transfer, diffusion, and dissolution kinetics; requires no new forming equipment. Commercial Ti-powder tablets dissolved at 730–750 °C in roughly 25–40 min, showing that time can overcome some compact-dissolution limitations. | Your 810–840 °C set point is already substantially superheated; a 2 min hold is far shorter than published tablet times. Longer/hotter holding increases oxidation, hydrogen pickup, Mg loss, crucible/nozzle reactions, and cycle time. It cannot remove alumina shells. | A secondary optimization after proving complete melting; useful for dense cast master-alloy pieces, not as the primary remedy for loose powder. | **Medium–high for powder-derived charges; low for clean cast additions.** Extra time may float some inclusions but can also form more oxide (pqac-00000032, pqac-00000034, pqac-00000051). |
| Compact inside cup with vented sliding plug | Combines improved powder thermal contact with an aluminium envelope; a freely sliding, positively vented plug can maintain containment while permitting gas escape. | If the cup melts before the core, the assembly reverts to a floating compact. A press-fit or obstructed 1 mm vent can behave as a pressure vessel once molten metal seals it; moisture, hydrated oxide, or lubricant can cause rapid gas evolution and metal ejection. | Only as a staged cold-charge experiment with dry, lubricant-free powder, moderate compaction, an unobstructed vent path, and the assembly buried beneath solid feed—not dropped into an existing bath. | **Medium–high.** Better containment than loose powder, but failure of the envelope can release the compact’s oxide inventory abruptly; the safety consequence of blocked venting is also greater (pqac-00000026, pqac-00000027, pqac-00000028). |
