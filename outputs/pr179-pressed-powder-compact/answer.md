## Compacting Aluminium-Alloy Powder Before Melting for Ultrasonic Atomization: A Literature-Based Assessment

---

### 1. Why Loose or Lightly Packed Aluminium-Alloy Powder Fails to Melt into a Bath

The root cause is the tenacious native oxide shell on every gas-atomised aluminium powder particle. Air-atomised aluminium powder typically carries an amorphous, hydrous alumina (Al₂O₃·3H₂O) layer approximately 50–150 Å (5–15 nm) thick; this layer crystallises to γ-Al₂O₃ above approximately 350 °C but does not dissolve or dissociate (ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 53-57, ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 32-36). Because Al₂O₃ is thermodynamically extremely stable—its equilibrium oxygen partial pressure is of the order of 10⁻⁴³ Pa—simply immersing the powder in a melt at 810–840 °C under argon does not strip the films (tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 40-45). Each particle therefore remains individually encapsulated; the oxide prevents metal-to-metal contact, blocks diffusion and wetting, and inhibits coalescence even when the aluminium core is above its liquidus (tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 40-45, tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 199-204).

**Low thermal conductivity of the powder bed** compounds the problem. Measurements on gas-atomised steel powder beds show effective thermal conductivities below 1/100 of the corresponding solid-metal value—for example, approximately 0.03–0.05 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ versus 13–15 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ for solid stainless steels, measured in argon at reduced pressure (wei2018thermalconductivityof pages 7-11). Although direct aluminium powder data at atmospheric argon pressure were not extracted from these sources, solid aluminium has a conductivity of ~238 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ (montes2025porosityeffecton pages 6-7), and the powder-bed effective conductivity would be at least one to two orders of magnitude lower, making the powder an effective thermal insulator. Even at higher porosities, compaction was shown to improve conductivity monotonically by increasing the solid fraction and interparticle contact area (montes2025porosityeffecton pages 4-6).

**Entrapped gas** in the interparticle voids and in internal porosity within each particle adds buoyancy, reduces the effective density below that of the surrounding melt, and can cause powder agglomerates to float rather than submerge (ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 32-36, ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 25-29). Gas expansion upon heating can crack a compact or eject material before consolidation (ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 25-29).

**Role of Mg in AlSi10Mg versus 4047.** In Mg-bearing aluminium alloys, Mg reacts with the native Al₂O₃ film to form MgO or MgAl₂O₄ spinel. These oxides are *more* refractory than the original amorphous alumina: MgAl₂O₄ is thermally stable and was confirmed by XRD at sintered Al–Mg interfaces (tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 45-49). While Mg can *assist* sintering by disrupting the alumina and enabling liquid-phase penetration at appropriate concentrations (an optimum around 0.15% Mg was cited in one Al–Sn system), the spinel and MgO that form on the powder surface in storage and during atomisation create a tougher, more chemically resistant shell (tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 40-45, tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 45-49, tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 149-153). This plausibly explains why AlSi10Mg powder failed to consolidate while 4047 (Al-12Si, no Mg) melted: the 4047 particles retained only the thinner, weaker amorphous Al₂O₃ film, which could crack or be penetrated more readily at the melt temperature.

### 2. Does Uniaxial Compaction Fix It, and at What Pressure and Relative Density?

**Compaction pressures and densities.** Uniaxial cold pressing of pure aluminium powder at approximately 246 MPa (80 °C warm pressing) produced a relative density of 94.5% under load, rising to 96.7% after a constant-load hold; at 386 MPa the values were 97.0% and 98.2% respectively (moreno2011densificationofal pages 3-4). A second study reported 92% of theoretical density at 464 MPa and 97.25% at 580 MPa for room-temperature pressing (mohtar2008developingcarbonparticle pages 26-33). Aluminium powder crosses approximately 85% relative density near 90–100 MPa, at which point porosity begins transitioning from open to closed (moreno2011densificationofal pages 2-3). Under spark-plasma sintering at 500 °C, much lower pressures sufficed: 20 MPa → 92.5%, 30 MPa → 95.2%, 40 MPa → 96.6%, 50 MPa → 98.4% (tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 98-103, tuncay2016evaluationofthe pages 2-3).

**Does particle deformation break the oxide?** The literature is consistent that compaction *cracks* and *displaces* oxide films but does not *remove* them. Plastic deformation of the soft aluminium relative to the rigid alumina creates discontinuities and some metal-to-metal contact; this is enhanced at elevated temperature because the hardness ratio of alumina to aluminium increases and aluminium softens (tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 86-92, tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 109-114, tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 103-109, tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 199-204). However, "mechanical disruption produced by conventional compaction pressure is generally insufficient for extensive solid-state sintering" (tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 40-45). Supplementary mechanisms—liquid-phase penetration, Mg-assisted oxide reduction, or current-assisted heating (SPS)—are usually needed for strong particle bonding (tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 199-204, tuncay2018evaluationofspark pages 40-45). For the user's purpose (remelting, not sintering), even partial oxide cracking should help by improving thermal conductivity and allowing the surrounding melt to penetrate the compact once the aluminium core begins to liquefy.

**Evidence from remelting aluminium briquettes.** Cold-pressed aluminium-alloy machining-chip briquettes with a density of 2.29 g cm⁻³ (about 88% of theoretical) were remelted by induction at approximately 850 °C, producing a metallurgical yield of 94.2% with 2.25% dross (baron2026characterizationofcoldpressed pages 18-20, baron2026characterizationofcoldpressed pages 16-18). Properly compacted briquettes typically achieve 80–96% yield depending on density and contamination, with densities of 2.20–2.40 g cm⁻³ identified as optimal for steel-deoxidation applications (baron2026characterizationofcoldpressed pages 18-20, baron2026characterizationofcoldpressed pages 9-11). In a salt-flux remelting study of aluminium scrap, uncoated loose chips and uniaxially compacted briquettes both achieved coalescence above 97% and yields of 97–100%; compaction had a stronger effect on coalescence than on yield per se (vallejoolivares2022thermaldecoatingpretreatment pages 8-10). However, *over*-compacted briquettes (by moderate-pressure torsion, producing nearly zero internal porosity) showed worse de-coating, more dross, and lower coalescence (vallejoolivares2023effectsofcompaction pages 1-3).

**Evidence from foundry alloying tablets.** Titanium-powder alloying tablets for aluminium foundries dissolve in 25–40 minutes at 730–750 °C (bazhin2016specificityofthe pages 4-5, bazhin2016specificityofthe pages 1-2). The recommended compaction pressure is 25.5–29.8 MPa (250–300 kg cm⁻²) to achieve a density of 2.5–5.0 g cm⁻³, preserving enough interconnected porosity for melt infiltration. Pressures of 350–500 kg cm⁻² (34–49 MPa) were termed "overconsolidated" and gave dissolution times of 35–40 minutes because the aluminium melt could not infiltrate the tablet; conversely, pressures below ~10–20 MPa gave tablets that crumbled, losing 5–15% of their mass (bazhin2016specificityofthe pages 2-4). This demonstrates a clear density window: too dense impedes dissolution, too porous gives inadequate integrity.

### 3. Safety and Quality Risks of Melting a Compact

**Is the "ejects molten metal" warning justified?** Yes, under specific conditions. The aluminium–water explosion literature confirms that violent steam-driven metal ejection requires liquid water enclosed by a compact mass of aluminium at a temperature sufficient for rapid vaporisation and oxidation (zima1960pyrophoricityofuranium pages 17-21). Sources of moisture in an aluminium-powder compact include:

- **Hydrated oxide on the powder surface:** Air-atomised aluminium powder carries an amorphous hydrous alumina layer, Al₂O₃·3H₂O, which decomposes and releases water vapour above approximately 350 °C (ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 53-57).
- **Adsorbed atmospheric moisture** on high-surface-area powder.
- **Die lubricants**, if used, which decompose into gas during heating.
- **Cutting fluid residues** on recycled chips.

If the compact has closed porosity (relative density above approximately 85–90%), gas generated inside cannot escape and the internal pressure rises until the compact bursts, potentially ejecting molten or semi-molten aluminium. This is precisely the mechanism observed in aluminium PM foam manufacture: blowing-agent decomposition (TiH₂, ZrH₂, CaCO₃) generates gas that expands the compact, and hydrogen can begin evolving while the matrix is still solid, producing cracks perpendicular to the compaction direction (ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 25-29, rodinger2024theeffectof pages 2-4, ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 29-32).

**How conditions change from green to sintered to hot-pressed:**
- *Green compacts:* Retain the most moisture and hydrated oxide; interconnected porosity at moderate density allows gas venting, but a high-density green compact can trap gas. Dropping a green compact into an existing melt is the highest-risk scenario.
- *Sintered compacts:* Heating to 550 °C burns off organics and decomposes hydrated oxide, reducing the gas inventory substantially (vallejoolivares2022thermaldecoatingpretreatment pages 8-10, vallejoolivares2023effectsofcompaction pages 1-3). However, if sintering closes the remaining pores, the degassing benefit is lost.
- *Vacuum hot-pressed or SPS:* Vacuum processing can desorb gas before the pores close, providing the safest consolidated form—provided the compact is subsequently stored dry and clean.

**Oxide inclusions and nozzle clogging.** Compaction does not eliminate oxide; it fragments the films and redistributes them as dispersed particles within the compact. Common inclusions from aluminium powder include Al₂O₃ films and clusters (which can be several millimetres long), MgO, MgAl₂O₄ spinel, and Al₄C₃ (wu2022meltrefiningand pages 1-3, wu2022meltrefiningand pages 3-4, koont2013intéractionentreles pages 45-50). In aluminium filtration practice, inclusions as small as 1–30 µm are targeted for removal (wu2022meltrefiningand pages 4-6). With a 0.7 mm nozzle, any particle or agglomerate above approximately 0.2–0.3 mm in its longest dimension poses a clogging risk. Flux treatment can reduce inclusion counts by approximately 70% (kovacec2025removalofinclusions pages 3-4), but fluxes themselves may introduce chloride or fluoride residues.

**Hydrogen pickup.** Molten aluminium absorbs hydrogen from water vapour; hydrogen constitutes approximately 70–90% of the total gas in an aluminium melt (wu2022meltrefiningand pages 1-3). Every gram of powder introduces more oxide surface area (and thus more adsorbed water) per unit of metal than bulk material, making hydrogen pickup a particular concern when melting powder-derived charges.

**Recommended mitigation:** Always heat the compact *with* the charge from cold (never drop it into an existing melt), dry or degas the powder before pressing (80–100 °C minimum, or vacuum bake), avoid die lubricants if possible (use die-wall lubrication only), and provide vent paths during heating.

A press-fit or obstructed 1 mm vent can behave as a pressure vessel once molten metal seals it; moisture, hydrated oxide, or lubricant can cause rapid gas evolution and metal ejection. | Only as a staged cold-charge experiment with dry, lubricant-free powder, moderate compaction, an unobstructed vent path, and the assembly buried beneath solid feed—not dropped into an existing bath. | **Medium–high.** Better containment than loose powder, but failure of the envelope can release the compact’s oxide inventory abruptly; the safety consequence of blocked venting is also greater (zima1960pyrophoricityofuranium pages 17-21, ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 25-29, ibrahim2005effectofmaterial pages 53-57). |

**Recommendation hierarchy:**

1. **First choice: commercial master alloys.** For Si, Mg, Cu, and Mn—the most commonly adjusted elements—cast master alloys (Al-50Si, Al-20Si, Al-50Mg, Al-50Cu, Al-10Mn, etc.) dissolve predictably at 810–850 °C, introduce minimal oxide per gram of alloy addition, and are available in small pieces that fit the crucible. Al-50Si has been documented for making small-batch Al-Si melts at 850 °C (nowak2011developmentofniobium pages 57-61). This avoids the powder-melting problem entirely.

2. **Second choice: arc-melted master-alloy button.** When the desired composition or blend is not commercially available (e.g. multi-element blends for novel alloy development), weigh out the powders, arc-melt them into a dense button under argon (with multiple flips), and add the button to the crucible charge as a cast piece. This is a well-established laboratory practice for titanium, nickel, and high-entropy alloys and can be adapted to aluminium. *Note: this recommendation is an engineering extrapolation; no cited aluminium-specific study directly validated it.*

3. **Third choice: cold-pressed compact,** used as an intermediate step when neither master alloys nor arc melting are available or practical. Press at 200–300 MPa (or warm press at 100–150 MPa and 80–100 °C) targeting 90–95% relative density. Dry the powder beforehand, use die-wall lubricant only, place the compact beneath solid charge pieces, heat from cold, and extend the hold time to at least 5 minutes. Validate with the quick test described in Section 6.

4. **When a pressed compact is the *wrong* choice:** (a) When the powder is heavily oxidised, Mg-rich, or moisture-contaminated and cannot be adequately dried; the compact will trap gas and eject. (b) When the target composition is available as a cast master alloy or pre-alloyed wire. (c) When atomisation quality demands are very tight and any oxide inclusion could clog the 0.7 mm nozzle—in this case, cast alloy + degassing is safer.

### 6. A Quick Test Before Committing an Atomiser Run

The following screening protocol requires only a laboratory furnace, a small graphite or alumina crucible, an analytical balance, and sectioning/polishing equipment:

1. **Press three compacts** of the target powder blend at 200, 250, and 300 MPa (19 mm diameter). Record mass, dimensions, and density.

2. **Prepare crucible melts.** In each of three small graphite crucibles, load approximately 50 g of 6063 alloy pieces. Place one compact at the bottom of each crucible, beneath the 6063 pieces.

3. **Heat in a resistance furnace** under flowing argon (or in the atomiser's induction module if convenient) to 860 °C. Hold for 5 minutes after the thermocouple reaches set point. If a compact has not merged with the melt in 5 minutes, extend to 10 minutes for comparison.

4. **Cool in the crucible** and section the solidified ingot longitudinally.

5. **Assess:**
   - **Visual:** Is the compact boundary visible? Are there unmolten powder regions, oxide veins, or macro-porosity?
   - **Macro-etch:** A 10% NaOH etch reveals grain flow and dissolution fronts.
   - **SEM/EDS:** Map Si, Mg, Cu (or other target elements) across the section. A successful melt-in produces a homogeneous composition profile; failure leaves a composition step at the former compact boundary.
   - **Weigh dross:** Collect surface oxide and dross; report as a percentage of total charge.

6. **Pass/fail criteria:**
   - ✅ Compact fully incorporated, no macro-porosity, composition uniform within ±0.5 wt% of target across the section.
   - ⚠️ Compact partially dissolved, visible but diffuse boundary, acceptable for a longer hold time.
   - ❌ Compact remains as a distinct region, oxide vein visible, or unmelted powder core present—do not use in an atomiser run.

This test directly mimics the crucible environment (graphite, argon, induction heating) and can be completed in a single day. It avoids wasting an atomiser run on an unproven charge design.

### Basis and Limitations of Evidence

The conclusions above draw on direct evidence from aluminium systems except where noted. The oxide-shell barrier mechanism, compaction pressure–density relationships, foundry alloying-tablet dissolution data, aluminium briquette remelting yields, and inclusion/hydrogen concerns are all from aluminium-specific literature. The thermal-conductivity-of-powder-bed data are from steel powders but are physically analogous; the expected ratio for aluminium powder beds is consistent with theoretical models fitted to aluminium data (montes2025porosityeffecton pages 4-6, montes2025porosityeffecton pages 6-7). The arc-melting recommendation is an engineering extrapolation from practice with other metals. The steam-explosion evidence is from aluminium–water interaction studies in nuclear-safety contexts (zima1960pyrophoricityofuranium pages 17-21). No literature was found describing exactly the user's scenario—remelting a pressed aluminium-powder compact in a small induction furnace for ultrasonic atomisation—so the recommendations integrate evidence from the closest analogues: PM aluminium sintering, aluminium scrap briquette remelting, and foundry alloying-tablet dissolution.
