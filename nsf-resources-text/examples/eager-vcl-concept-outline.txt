EAGER Concept Outline — A Vertical Cloud Laboratory as a Hardware
Backbone for an Asynchronous, Multi-Material FDM Desktop 3D-Print Farm

PI: Sterling Baird (Brigham Young University, Mechanical Engineering /
Materials).
Unit: ENG/CMMI Advanced Manufacturing.
Type: EAGER, ~$300K, 2 yr.
Keywords: self-driving lab, vertical lift module, fused deposition
modeling, print farm, generative design, robotic integration.


Problem
-------
Self-driving laboratories (autonomous experimental systems that plan,
run, and analyze their own experiments) for manufacturing research keep
hitting the same wall: physically moving samples and consumables among
many different instruments. Mobile manipulators (wheeled robots with
arms) are flexible but slow and unreliable in cluttered rooms. Linear-
rail and SCARA robots (fixed-track and jointed industrial arms) are
fast but lock the layout in place and are hard to extend. A 2024
community survey of autonomous laboratories (Hung 2024) and the NIST
self-driving lab roadmap (Trautt 2024, NIST SP 1320) both find that
integration overhead, not algorithms, is the main bottleneck. The
community needs a denser, more reliable, and easier-to-maintain physical
backbone that lets a single robot serve many parallel workstations.


Idea
----
A Vertical Cloud Laboratory (VCL) repurposes an industrial vertical
lift module (VLM) — a warehouse-style elevator whose trays carry power
and data — as the integration backbone for a self-driving lab. A single
UR5e collaborative robot arm (~$35K) sits in front of the VLM and
reaches any tray on demand. To stress-test the backbone, the EAGER will
load the trays with a print farm of 50 Bambu Lab A1 Mini multi-material
desktop 3D printers (~$15K total). Side-by-side, those 50 printers
would take ~100 linear feet of bench — the footprint problem the VLM is
meant to solve — so the build directly tests whether a vertical, robot-
served farm can produce, retrieve, and characterize parts asynchronously
at a useful rate. [Figure 1 contrasts the VCL backbone with three
competing integration paradigms.]

The PI's prior feasibility study at U. Toronto's Acceleration Consortium
(Baird 2025) showed that one VLM provides ~3× the automation-friendly
working surface of equivalent floor space (273 ft^2 in 92 ft^2),
retrieves any tray in ≤60 s, and keeps an optical microscope in focus
at 40× during belt-driven motion. The consortium asked the PI to
derisk at smaller scale after the move to BYU; the proposed VCL — one
VLM, one arm, 50 printers — is that step, replicable elsewhere for
under $300K.

Hypothesis: a VLM with electrified trays, served by a single
collaborative arm, can asynchronously orchestrate a desktop print farm
and a small characterization suite as the backbone of a manufacturing
self-driving lab, with higher reliability and lower integration
overhead than mobile-robot or rigid-rail approaches.


Why EAGER
---------
The work is genuinely untested: no scientific deployment has used an
electrified-tray VLM as an instrument backbone, and no published
autonomous lab pairs a single collaborative arm with a 50-station
asynchronous print farm. The thermal, vibration, and electromagnetic-
interference (EMI) coupling among the VLM, the UR5e, and continuously
running desktop printers cannot be settled by simulation alone. The
work is transformative if it succeeds: a sub-$300K, replicable backbone
would lower the barrier to autonomous manufacturing labs by an order of
magnitude. And the central question — can warehouse-class material-
handling hardware serve as a science-grade integrator — sits between
Advanced Manufacturing, robotics, and digital-design programs, which
makes it awkward for any standard solicitation but a natural fit for
EAGER.


Approach
--------
Year 1 commissions the hardware backbone (Figure 2). We procure and
install the VLM and UR5e, then load the trays with the 50 Bambu Lab A1
Mini printers (multi-material AMS Lite), filament spools, build-plate
magazines, and a small characterization tray (digital calipers, optical
inspection camera, scale). The PI and an integration buyout lead
systems integration: tray fixturing, build-plate hand-off poses,
AprilTag fiducials (printed visual markers the robot uses for pose
estimation), safety interlocks, and a unified scheduler that talks to
the printers' Bambu API and the UR5e simultaneously. Two part-time
undergraduate research assistants handle daily maintenance — nozzle
changes, build-plate prep, filament swaps, jam recovery — on a rotating
schedule that itself stress-tests how the system tolerates asynchronous
human and robot activity.

Year 2 measures the backbone under realistic loads. One half-time MS
student leads the campaigns: print throughput across the farm vs. a
side-by-side baseline; print quality on a standard test artifact;
UR5e/VLM motion effects on adjacent in-progress prints (tray-mounted
MEMS accelerometer); queue and retrieval bottlenecks; and the practical
gain of asynchronous over synchronous batch operation. As a generative-
design demonstration, we couple the farm to a topology-optimization
pipeline that prints candidate lattices and brackets in parallel and
retires designs from inspection feedback — a workflow impractical with
one or two printers.

Year 1 go/no-go gates (instrument in parentheses): (i) UR5e build-plate
pick/place repeatability ≤ ±1 mm across all 50 trays (laser tracker,
fiducial on each tray); (ii) <50 µm peak vibration at an in-progress
print bed during a worst-case adjacent retrieval (tray-mounted MEMS
accelerometer at 1 kHz); (iii) ≥200 fully autonomous print→retrieve→
inspect cycles with no human between job dispatch and data export;
(iv) ≥24 h of continuous powered cross-tray operation with no operator
intervention.

Risk and mitigation. If electrified-tray power proves unreliable, we
fall back to umbilical power that retracts during retrieval. If a
tray-pick is out of UR5e reach, we operate that tray manually with
operator hand-off. If scheduler latency dominates, we drop to a one-
printer-per-tray static schedule that still tests the core hypothesis.
The print farm itself — the most replaceable component in the build —
is the dependent variable, so any single printer failure is absorbed by
the asynchronous schedule rather than blocking the experiment.


Future direction (out of scope)
-------------------------------
A natural follow-on is to swap a tray or two of FDM printers for
desktop laser powder-bed fusion (e.g. Mastrex MX-100, ~$39K) and re-run
the same orchestration on metal additive manufacturing with autonomous
post-print characterization. This EAGER deliberately stops short of
that step.


Use of funds (2 yr, ~$300K)
---------------------------
Equipment (no F&A) covers the VLM ($120K) and UR5e ($35K). Materials &
supplies cover 50× Bambu A1 Mini printers ($15K), AMS/filament/build
plates ($8K), and the characterization tray ($5K). Personnel funds one
half-time MS student ($25K), two part-time undergraduate research
assistants ($10K), and an integration buyout ($10K). Other direct costs
cover cloud/software ($5K) and travel ($4K). F&A is computed at BYU's
federally negotiated rate of 51.5% on MTDC. The total fits comfortably
under the $300K EAGER ceiling, leaving headroom for contingency at
full-proposal stage.

[Table 1: Two-yr budget. Equipment (no F&A) $155K; M&S (MTDC) $28K;

Total ≈ $279K.]
