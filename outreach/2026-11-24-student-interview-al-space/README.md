## In short

- **8 of the 20 hold, 7 hold with a qualification, and 5 don't hold.** The five that don't are the ones worth fixing
  before the student quotes them:
  - **9, Navy ships.** Navy ships do use aluminum: in deckhouses and superstructures for decades, and in the
    whole hulls of some fast ships. Steel makes the big hulls. Copper alloys go where seawater flows: pipes, heat
    exchangers, propellers.
  - **13, rockets.** Rocket corrosion is mostly a problem on the ground, at seaside launch pads, not on the way up.
  - **15, cold and brittleness.** Aluminum alloys don't have a ductile-to-brittle transition. That is part of why
    liquid-hydrogen and liquid-oxygen tanks are made of aluminum.
  - **17, price.** Copper costs three to five times as much as aluminum per kilogram, and bronzes are mostly
    copper. The student had it right.
  - **19, aluminum-copper alloys.** They are everywhere, at the two ends of the range: aluminum with a few
    percent copper is the classic aerospace family (the review's own example, AA2024, is one), and copper with
    about 10% aluminum is the bronze of Navy propellers. A literal 50-50 mix is brittle.
- **Two of the qualifications matter for a piece about this review:**
  - **4, radiation against strength.** The review's point is different: radiation is what strips strength from
    today's alloys, and the new alloys are built to keep it.
  - **8, "a quadrillion dollars".** It's an order-of-magnitude illustration of the size of the composition
    search space, not a measured cost.
- **Edison's verdicts are mostly right, but check its sources before reusing them.** It caught 9, 13, 15 and 17.
  It called 19 only partly supported, because its query left out the "I don't know anything using Al–Cu" part.
  Of the 40 sources it names, all but one exist. But eight have wrong details, six don't say what Edison cites
  them for, and it gets the littoral combat ship hulls wrong. See [Edison's answer, checked](#edisons-answer-checked).

**Claims in Edison's answer that are wrong or unsupported.** The first six come from checking its sources (below):

1. **Littoral combat ships.** "LCS combine steel hulls with aluminum superstructures" is true of the Freedom variant
   only. The Independence variant is an all-aluminum trimaran. Edison's own source (the Whitley thesis) says LCS
   are built "both steel with Aluminum superstructures and all aluminum"; Edison dropped the second half.
2. **"Aluminum saves ~60% of hull/superstructure weight."** It can't be checked: Wahid 2019 is paywalled. The
   Whitley thesis puts the net saving at about 22% once aluminum's extra fire insulation is counted.
3. **"35 kg per car (1970s) → 152 kg (2023) → 250 kg (2025)".** The only cited source with these numbers
   (Trzepieciński 2024) takes them from an aluminum producer's promotional website. Czerwinski 2021 gives North
   America 154 kg (2010), 208 kg (2020) and a 233 kg forecast for 2026, which matches the 514 lb in row 6.
4. **"Corrosion of 2A02 peaked at 60 °C".** Cao 2018 does show this, but in an accelerated lab test (salt deposits,
   98% humidity, 30–80 °C, 200 h). Cao used the Arrhenius relation only to estimate oxygen diffusivity; it didn't
   observe Arrhenius behavior.
5. **"Pitting of aluminum in seawater has been analyzed with Arrhenius-type plots" (Itoh 2022).** Not in Itoh. Its
   aluminum data are at one temperature (21 °C), and its only Arrhenius plot is for mild steel.
6. **Friction-extruded composites.** Edison attributes them to Tunes pp. 17–18. The review never mentions friction
   extrusion; the result is Kalsar 2026, published after the review.
7. **2195 "retains good toughness at liquid-hydrogen temperatures" (Edison 9).** Too simple. Orion's 2195 part is
   the crew-module pressure vessel, not a cryogenic tank. On the Shuttle tank, NASA had to develop a special aging
