Thanks again for the interview. I answered from memory, and a few things I said were wrong or loose:

1. **Navy ships do use aluminum.** Some cruisers have aluminum upper structures, and some fast Navy ships have
   all-aluminum hulls. Copper alloys go where seawater flows (pipes, propellers), because they resist corrosion and
   marine growth. Read: [the Navy's all-aluminum Independence-class ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence-class_littoral_combat_ship).
2. **Copper costs more than aluminum, not less.** You were right. Copper is about three times the price per
   kilogram. Read: [USGS copper summary, 2026](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-copper.pdf).
3. **Aluminum doesn't turn brittle in the cold.** That is a steel problem. Aluminum gets stronger when cold, which is
   why rocket tanks for liquid hydrogen and oxygen are aluminum. Read:
   [the ductile-brittle transition (Cambridge)](https://doitpoms.ac.uk/tlplib/mechanical_properties/ductile.php).
4. **Rockets corrode mostly on the launch pad, not on the way up.** Seaside launch sites have salty air, and rocket
   exhaust adds acid. The trip to orbit takes minutes, and space has no water. Read:
   [NASA: Corrosion at KSC](https://public.ksc.nasa.gov/corrosion/).
5. **Aluminum-copper alloys are everywhere.** AA2024, the aerospace alloy in your article, is one. So is the bronze of
   Navy propellers (mostly copper, about 10% aluminum). A 50-50 mix would be brittle. Read: [Duralumin, the first aluminum-copper aircraft alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duralumin).
6. **Radiation and strength.** The article's point is that radiation weakens today's alloys, and new alloys are
   designed to keep their strength. Read:
   [Leoben: a radiation-resistant aluminium alloy for space](https://www.unileoben.ac.at/en/newsdetail/new-radiation-resistant-aluminium-alloy-for-space-missions/).

The "quadrillion dollars" in my talk was a rough illustration of how many alloy recipes are possible, not a measured
cost.
