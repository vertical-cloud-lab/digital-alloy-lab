Question: Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) of aluminium alloys (AlSi10Mg, 6061, 7075) with a single-mode CW Yb fibre laser at 1070 nm, 200-500 W, a 96 um (1/e^2) spot, 0.6-1.1 m/s, in argon at 1 atm with a 2-3 m/s cross-flow over the bed. Our model gives a vapour jet leaving a shallow depression at ~2,760 K and ~480 m/s (Mach ~0.4), about 1 g of metal vapour per hour of lasing, of which ~0.5 g/h condenses (AlSi10Mg). We need to know how large the condensate grows while it is still in the beam path, within the first ~0.1-1 ms and the first ~1-5 mm above the melt pool, because that sets how much of the 1070 nm beam the plume removes: as ~20 nm primaries our column (19 mg/m^2) absorbs ~0.1 %, but as ~300 nm compact agglomerates it would remove ~3-11 %.

Please answer with numbers and citations, preferring LPBF measurements over welding:
1. Measured laser-beam attenuation or extinction by the plume in LPBF or in low-power (< 1 kW) CW fibre-laser processing at 1 atm: probe-beam extinction, transmitted-power or calorimetric measurements, and how they change with cross-flow speed, power and scan speed. Include any measurement on Al alloys.
2. Measured size distributions of the condensate in LPBF plumes or fumes (SMPS, TEM of samples taken close to the pool, laser-scattering or extinction tomography): primary-particle size, agglomerate size and fractal dimension, and especially how they change with height above the pool or with time after emission. Include stainless steel, Ti-6Al-4V, Ni alloys and Al alloys.
3. Time scales: how fast metal vapour nucleates and coagulates in a jet that mixes with argon (laser-ablation plume and welding-fume studies are useful here), and when agglomerates of 100-300 nm form (microseconds, milliseconds or later).
4. Published aerosol models of LPBF or laser-welding plumes (nucleation, condensation, coagulation, sectional or moment methods), what particle sizes they predict at 1 ms and 10 ms, and how well they match measurements.
5. For Al specifically: whether the condensate is metallic Al, Al oxide or Mg oxide at the O2 levels of LPBF (100-1000 ppm), and its optical constants at 1070 nm if published.
6. Any evidence of plasma or significant inverse-Bremsstrahlung absorption at 1070 nm and ~10 MW/cm^2 in LPBF of Al.
Say where the literature is thin or conflicting, and which single measurement would best settle whether the beam loss in our case is ~0.1 % or ~10 %.

## Plume-Induced Laser-Beam Attenuation in LPBF of Aluminium Alloys: Evidence Review

### 1. Measured Laser-Beam Attenuation by the Plume

Direct measurements of beam loss through the vapour/condensate plume at near-infrared wavelengths (~1.07 µm) are sparse for LPBF specifically and derive primarily from laser welding studies at higher power.

**Welding measurements.** Shcheglov et al. (2011) used transverse 1.3 µm probe-beam extinction tomography during 5 kW CW Yb-fibre-laser welding of mild steel and calculated approximately 9% total plume attenuation (87–95% of power reached the keyhole inlet), with the lower plume (0–5 mm above the workpiece) excluded from the integral (shcheglov2011plumeattenuationof pages 5-6, shcheglov2011plumeattenuationof pages 1-2). Wahl et al. (2026) employed a coaxial 1064 nm probe beam transmitted through the keyhole during full-penetration welding and measured median attenuation of approximately 13% for stainless steel, 3% for aluminium (EN AW-5083), and 5% for copper at atmospheric pressure (wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 4-6, wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 10-11). Nearly all attenuation occurred within 10 mm above the sheet surface (wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 4-6). Reducing ambient pressure from 1000 to 100 hPa lowered stainless-steel attenuation from ~13% to ~8% (wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 10-11). Lou et al. reported ~23% average attenuation for Yb-fibre welding at 1 atm, dropping to ~2% at 3 kPa (brown2024atmosphereeffectsin pages 10-12).

**LPBF-specific values.** Ye et al. (2019) adopted an average 10% plume attenuation for LPBF of Ti-6Al-4V, IN625, and 316L based on Shcheglov's measurement, finding good agreement between simulated and calorimetrically measured absorptivity after applying this correction; they noted that Greses reported an upper bound near 40% for high-power Nd:YAG welding (ye2019energycouplingmechanisms pages 4-6). Dowling et al. (2022) used extinction coefficients from Hansen and Duley (absorption 2.7 m⁻¹, scattering 0.761 m⁻¹, combined 3.46 m⁻¹) with an assumed 5 mm plume height, giving a calculated Beer–Lambert loss of ~1.7% (dowling2022effectofa pages 2-3, dowling2022effectofa pages 1-2).

**Al-specific finding.** Aluminium consistently shows the lowest plume attenuation of the metals tested—approximately 2–3% in full-penetration welding—partly attributed to rapid oxidation of Al nanoparticles to nearly transparent Al₂O₃ (wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 8-9, wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 4-6). No published study has directly measured the plume transmission during LPBF of AlSi10Mg at 200–500 W with a 96 µm spot.

**Cross-flow dependence.** No quantitative measurement of attenuation versus cross-flow speed was found in the retrieved literature. However, multiple studies qualitatively confirm that effective cross-flow removes the plume from the beam path and restores melt-pool depth (patterson2021areviewof pages 37-39).

### 2. Measured Size Distributions of Plume Condensate

**Stainless steel (LPBF).** Noskov et al. (2020) characterised ultrafine particles from three laser AM processes (including SLM/LPBF) using TEM of filter-collected samples. Primary-particle equivalent projected-area diameters were predominantly 4–16 nm, with process-specific medians of 8.0, 9.4, and 11.2 nm (noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 4-7, noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 1-2, noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 3-4). Particles formed fractal-like aggregates; primary particles >30 nm showed metallic cores with oxide shells (noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 10-11, noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 9-10). Numerical modelling indicated particles were trapped in toroidal recirculation zones near the laser-heated region with an estimated residence time of ~0.5 ms in PBF-LB/M (noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 4-7).

**Aluminium (laser ablation).** Kautz et al. (2023) studied ns-laser ablation of AA6061 at atmospheric pressure. SMPS measurements showed a bimodal aerosol with a narrow vacuum-aerodynamic mode near 50 nm and broader mobility-diameter distributions. The fractal dimension of agglomerates was approximately 2 (kautz2023impactofenvironmental pages 5-7, kautz2023impactofenvironmental pages 4-5). Air-formed agglomerates were larger than those in N₂ (kautz2023impactofenvironmental pages 1-1). HR-TEM identified both Al₂O₃ and AlN nanoparticles within the fractal structures (kautz2023impactofenvironmental pages 8-9, kautz2023impactofenvironmental pages 9-11).

**Welding fume.** In GMAW fume, primary particles are 10–100 nm and agglomerates have aerodynamic mass-median diameters of ~149–240 nm with chain-like morphology (isaxon2008controlledhumanexposures pages 28-32). Number concentration decreases ~4-fold between 4.8 and 19.2 cm from the source, and sub-40 nm particles are rapidly scavenged by coagulation (isaxon2008controlledhumanexposures pages 28-32).

**Welding plume (fibre laser).** Shcheglov's model predicted ~80 nm particles in the steel plume; 82% of measured particles were <55 nm (shcheglov2011plumeattenuationof pages 5-6).

**Height-resolved LPBF data.** No height- or time-resolved condensate-size measurements within the first few millimetres above an LPBF melt pool were found. This is the single most important gap.

### 3. Time Scales for Nucleation and Coagulation

**Nucleation.** Jenkins and Eagar (2003) showed that in welding-arc metal vapour, the temperature drops ~10 K in one microsecond, which substantially changes nucleation conditions; primary iron particles in the 1–100 nm range nucleate during this rapidly evolving interval (jenkins2003submicronparticlechemistry pages 3-4, jenkins2003submicronparticlechemistry pages 2-3). Classical-nucleation-theory models (Tashiro, Zeniya, Vishnyakov) predict that homogeneous nucleation occurs once the vapour cools below ~1700–2100 K and supersaturation becomes large (tashiro2013numericalanalysisof pages 5-7, zeniya2010numericalsimulationof pages 2-3).

**Coagulation.** Vishnyakov et al. (2019) calculated a coagulation number-halving time of ~8 µs at 2000 K, with aggregated droplets solidifying after ~0.9 ms and nuclei continuing to evolve to ~1.2 ms, yielding primary particles of ~4–8 nm and "inhalable" (secondary/agglomerate) particles of 185–239 nm (vishnyakov2019formationofparticles pages 7-9). Tashiro et al. (2013) found that secondary chain-like particles approach ~100 nm near 1500 K during cooling from 3000 K at 3.43 × 10⁵ K/s (tashiro2013numericalanalysisof pages 5-7). Zeniya et al. (2010) predicted an average secondary-particle diameter of ~141 nm at 300 K (zeniya2010numericalsimulationof pages 3-4).

**When do 100–300 nm agglomerates form?** The welding-fume models converge on ~0.5–2 ms for secondary particles to reach 100–200 nm, depending on cooling rate and initial vapour density. In the LPBF context, the near-melt-pool residence time is only ~0.5 ms (noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 4-7), and the vapour jet velocity of ~480–930 m/s carries material 1–5 mm in 1–10 µs. At those early times, only primary particles of a few nanometres to a few tens of nanometres are expected; 100–300 nm agglomerates would require additional milliseconds of coagulation, which corresponds to tens of millimetres of transport—well outside the beam path. This is the central argument favouring the ~0.1% (20 nm primaries) estimate over the ~3–11% (300 nm agglomerates) estimate.

### 4. Published Aerosol Models of Laser Plumes

**Wen et al. (2007)** coupled gas dynamics with a moment-method aerosol model (classical nucleation, condensation, Brownian coagulation) for pulsed-laser ablation of copper in 1 atm Ar. Condensation ended after ~1–3 ms. The predicted primary-particle distribution showed modes near 3 nm and 30 nm in Ar; the largest particles (~100 nm) formed in the hot plume core where prolonged coagulation occurred (wen2007experimentalandtheoretical pages 18-23, wen2007experimentalandtheoretical pages 14-18, wen2007experimentalandtheoretical pages 10-14). This model did not include post-solidification agglomeration.

**Vishnyakov et al. (2019)** modelled the full sequence from vapour emission to inhalable particle in arc welding, predicting ~4–8 nm primaries and ~185–239 nm secondaries at 300–600 K shielding-gas temperature (vishnyakov2019formationofparticles pages 7-9).

**Tashiro et al. (2013) and Zeniya et al. (2010)** used explicit particle-tracking with nucleation and coagulation for iron GTA welding. Tashiro's model included electrostatic charging (which suppressed coagulation) and Brownian motion; final secondaries were ~100 nm chain-like aggregates of ~10 nm primaries (tashiro2013numericalanalysisof pages 5-7, tashiro2013numericalanalysisof pages 1-3). Zeniya's model gave ~141 nm average secondaries (zeniya2010numericalsimulationof pages 3-4).

**No dedicated LPBF plume aerosol model** was found. Noskov et al. (2020) performed CFD of the LPBF gas jet and vortex flow but did not couple it to an aerosol dynamics solver (noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 4-7, noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 7-9). This is a critical modelling gap.

### 5. Aluminium Condensate Composition and Optical Constants

**Composition.** Kautz et al. (2023) demonstrated that ns-laser ablation of AA6061 at atmospheric pressure (even with trace O₂ in nominally pure N₂) produces primarily Al₂O₃ and AlN nanoparticles; metallic Al signals were stronger in N₂ but oxide species dominated whenever O₂ exceeded ~100 ppm (kautz2023impactofenvironmental pages 5-7, kautz2023impactofenvironmental pages 1-1, kautz2023impactofenvironmental pages 8-9). In LPBF chambers with 100–1000 ppm O₂, the condensate is therefore expected to be predominantly Al₂O₃, with possible AlN and sub-surface metallic Al cores in larger particles. By analogy with stainless-steel data, particles <30 nm are likely fully oxidised (noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 10-11, noskov2020characterizationofultrafine pages 9-10). Mg preferential evaporation from AlSi10Mg is well documented (sargioti2025physicochemicalandtoxicological pages 2-4), potentially producing MgO condensate, but no LPBF fume study was found that specifically identified MgO nanoparticles. Sargioti et al. (2025) noted oxygen accumulation and oxidized spatter in Al LPBF powder recycling, supporting an oxide-rich fume environment (sargioti2025physicochemicalandtoxicological pages 2-4).

**Optical constants at 1064 nm.** Wahl et al. (2026) used the following complex refractive indices at 1064 nm for Mie calculations: **metallic Al: m = 1.376 + 10.245i; Al₂O₃: m = 1.726 + 0.019i** (wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 8-8). The imaginary part k differs by a factor of ~540, meaning that if the condensate is fully oxidised to Al₂O₃, Rayleigh-regime absorption (which scales as Im[(m²−1)/(m²+2)]) is drastically reduced compared to metallic Al. This directly supports the observation that aluminium plume attenuation is the lowest among the metals tested (wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 8-9). No published optical constants for MgO nanoparticles at 1070 nm in the LPBF context were found, though bulk MgO is a wide-bandgap dielectric (k ≈ 0 at 1 µm).

**Implication.** For 20 nm Al₂O₃ primaries, Rayleigh absorption efficiency at 1070 nm is very small (k = 0.019), consistent with the user's calculated ~0.1% column attenuation. Only if the particles were metallic Al (k = 10.2) or had grown to >200 nm would attenuation approach the percent level. The 100–1000 ppm O₂ environment of LPBF strongly favours rapid oxidation of nanometre-scale Al particles, making the low-attenuation scenario more probable.

### 6. Plasma and Inverse-Bremsstrahlung Absorption at 1070 nm

The literature is consistent that inverse-Bremsstrahlung (IB) absorption is negligible at ~1 µm for LPBF-class irradiances (~10 MW/cm²):

- Shcheglov et al. (2011) noted that the IB absorption coefficient at 1.07 µm is approximately 100× lower than for CO₂ lasers at 10.6 µm, because IB scales as λ² or λ³ (shcheglov2011plumeattenuationof pages 1-2).
- Ye et al. (2019) neglected plasma absorption entirely for LPBF, citing an estimated plasma-breakdown threshold of ~10⁸ W/cm², far above the ~10⁷ W/cm² typical in LPBF (ye2019energycouplingmechanisms pages 4-6).
- Brown et al. (2024) noted that Al has a relatively low first ionisation energy (6.0 eV), but the dominant cover gas Ar requires 15.7 eV, limiting sustained ionisation (brown2024atmosphereeffectsin pages 2-5).
- Bidare et al. (2018) observed increased plume ionisation in Ar at high pressure (5 bar) but not at 1 atm with standard LPBF powers.

**Conclusion:** At 200–500 W with a 96 µm spot (~3–7 MW/cm²), plasma/IB absorption at 1070 nm in Ar at 1 atm is expected to be negligible (<< 1%). The plume absorption mechanism is entirely particulate extinction.

### Where the Literature Is Thin or Conflicting

1. **No LPBF-specific plume transmission measurement** exists for any aluminium alloy. All quantitative values are transferred from welding (higher power, deeper keyhole, more vapour) or are model assumptions.
2. **No height- or time-resolved condensate-size measurement** has been performed within the first 1–5 mm above an LPBF melt pool. The critical question—whether particles are still ~20 nm primaries or have already agglomerated to ~200 nm at beam-crossing height—is unanswered.
3. **No dedicated LPBF plume aerosol model** (nucleation + condensation + coagulation coupled to the LPBF gas dynamics) has been published.
4. **MgO condensate** from AlSi10Mg has not been identified or characterised optically.
5. The ~10% value from Ye et al. is widely cited but was adopted from steel welding data, not measured for LPBF Al.

### Which Single Measurement Would Best Settle Whether Beam Loss Is ~0.1% or ~10%?

A **coaxial probe-beam transmission measurement at 1070 nm during LPBF of AlSi10Mg** at 200–500 W, combined with **height-resolved SMPS or in-situ light-scattering sizing of the condensate within the first 5 mm above the melt pool**, would definitively resolve the question. The Wahl et al. (2026) coaxial-probe method demonstrated on welding of EN AW-5083 (measuring ~3% attenuation) could be adapted for LPBF geometry. If the probe simultaneously measured scattered and transmitted light at two or more angles, one could extract both the extinction and the dominant particle size in real time, distinguishing the ~20 nm primary regime from the ~200 nm agglomerate regime. The measurement should be performed with and without cross-flow at 2–3 m/s to quantify the cross-flow benefit.

Based on the available evidence, the most probable outcome for LPBF of Al alloys at 200–500 W with 2–3 m/s cross-flow is that the plume attenuation is **closer to 1–3% than to 10%**, because: (a) direct welding measurements on aluminium give ~2–3% (wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 10-11, wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 4-6); (b) the condensate is predominantly Al₂O₃, which is nearly transparent at 1070 nm (wahl2026measurementofbeam pages 8-8); (c) the LPBF vapour rate (~0.5 g/h) and cross-flow together limit the condensate column density; and (d) in the first 0.5 ms and 1–5 mm, particles are unlikely to have coagulated beyond ~20–50 nm. The user's estimate of ~0.1% for 20 nm Al₂O₃ primaries may be slightly optimistic if the fraction of metallic Al cores is non-negligible, while the ~3–11% estimate for 300 nm compact agglomerates appears to overestimate the degree of coagulation achievable within the beam path.
