**NIST AMMT/TEMPS.** The NIST Additive Manufacturing Metrology Testbed uses a 500 W fiber laser (with published datasets at 195 W) at 1070 nm, directed by a galvanometer scanner through SiC mirrors over a 100 × 100 mm build area (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 2-4, lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 4-6). The sealed chamber (1750 × 1200 × 780 mm) is evacuated and backfilled with argon at 300 L/min flow (lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 1-4). FPGA-based acquisition controls scanner motion and laser power; in-situ monitoring includes a coaxial Mikrotron EOSens 3CL1 melt-pool camera at 10,000 fps and a Basler acA3800-10gm layer camera (lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 1-4). Scan commands use the XY2-100 protocol at 100 kHz (10 µs timesteps), with each row containing X, Y, power, and trigger columns (lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 6-8). A critical lesson was that scattered laser light caused a folding mirror to reach approximately 275 °C and its silver coating to fail; recommended mitigations included forced-air cooling, baffling, and use of silicon mirror substrates (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 8-9).

**Bidare et al. (Heriot-Watt).** This compact, portable platform uses a 400 W, 1070 nm SPI single-mode fiber laser with a Raylase MS-II-14 scanner and 163 mm f-theta lens, achieving a ~50 µm spot (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5). The build area is approximately 30 × 30 mm with 10 mm depth—deliberately small to minimize powder consumption (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 7-9). A key lesson was that rigid tungsten-carbide knife and cylindrical spreaders jammed, so a 3 mm-diameter silicon cord was adopted as a compliant recoater at ~10 mm/s spreading speed with a 50 µm gap (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 5-7). The detachable Perspex chamber (350 × 230 × 220 mm) uses gas cross-flow with exhaust bubbled through a water bath to trap nanoparticles (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3). The system achieved >99% part density and demonstrated that slow recoating dominated build time rather than laser scanning (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 1-2, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 5-7).

**DTU Open Additive Manufacturing Initiative.** Andersen, Kjer, and Pedersen developed an open-source unified controller based on a Teensy 3.6 (180 MHz ARM Cortex-M4), using DMA and timer interrupts for deterministic scanner-laser synchronization (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12). The scanner interface is the XY2-100 protocol—a 20-bit, 2 MHz asynchronous digital protocol providing 16-bit (65,536-level) position resolution per axis (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12). They use a Sunny Galvo SG S9320D digital scanner with 20 mm aperture, <0.4 ms small-step response, <8 µrad repeatability, and >99.9% linearity (aagaard2023legacylaserpowder pages 3-3). The laser is a 250 W SPI fiber laser controlled via a 0–10 V analog signal (10-bit DAC, 0.24 W increment) with PWM modulation up to 100 kHz (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 3-7). The open-source firmware supports PID gas-flow control via VFD pumps, O₂ sensing, coaxial NIR photodiode monitoring, and three configurable sensor inputs (0–10 V or 4–20 mA) (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12, aagaard2023legacylaserpowder pages 2-3). The platform operates three LPBF/SLS systems and avoids proprietary lock-in (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 12-13).

**MIT/Hart Testbed.** Penny et al. built a bespoke platform around a 500 W, 1.075 µm SPI redPOWER qube single-mode fiber laser with a 250 mm collimator producing a 19.5 mm beam, scanned by a Thorlabs QS20XY galvanometer (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 3-4, penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 4-6). It features servo-actuated compliant-blade recoating, piston-fed powder supply, and only 60 mm clearance between the build plate and enclosure lid (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 3-4). Automation uses an NI cRIO-9039 on a 10 µs timebase; monitoring includes a high-speed MWIR camera (IRCameras IRC806HS, 640 × 512 InSb, 1–5 µm, ~50 µm resolution) through a CaF₂ protective window (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 4-6).

**Aconity Mini.** This commercial research platform uses a 190 W, 1064 nm Nd:YAG laser with adjustable focus over a cylindrical bed of Ø140 mm × 200 mm height; powder is delivered by a wiper rake from a manually filled reservoir, with argon backfill (maddison2022insitumonitoringand pages 61-66).

**DAMX (Sheffield).** This multi-wavelength platform supports interchangeable 450 nm, 915 nm, and 1064 nm laser heads (200 W SPI fiber + galvo with 70 µm spot), a Ø125 × 170 mm build envelope, layer thicknesses down to 10 µm, conductive preheating to 1200 °C, and a CFD-designed argon circulation maintaining <0.1% O₂ with optional vacuum below 10 mbar (aydin2025theuseof pages 114-120, aydin2025theuseof pages 106-110).

## 2. Powder Spreading: Recoater Design, Speed, Layer Thickness, and Spreadability

**Blade vs. Roller.** A direct comparison of single-layer packing densities found: non-rotating roller 45.39%, rotating roller 43.09%, chamfer recoater 42.21%, and straight blade 30.30% (sehhat2021powderspreadingin pages 12-14). The non-rotating roller produced the most uniform layer and fewest empty patches, while the straight blade suffered from point contact, particle dragging, roughness, and cavities (sehhat2021powderspreadingin pages 12-14). However, at very thin layers (e.g., 70 µm), rollers can produce the *lowest* density due to particle jamming (sehhat2021powderspreadingin pages 14-16, sehhat2021powderspreadingin pages 12-14). Counter-rotating rollers at high rates (>4.8π rad/s) harmed bed stability, while forward rotation improved packing density and surface morphology (jabri2024powderspreadingeffects pages 3-4). Among blade geometries, wide-nose (NB) blades outperformed sharp-edge (IB) blades because the rounded leading edge creates a compression zone (salehi2023optimisingspreadlayerquality pages 12-13).

**Compliant recoaters.** Bidare et al. found that rigid metal spreaders jammed; a 3 mm silicon cord successfully spread powder at 50 µm gap (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5). The MIT/Hart system also uses a servo-actuated compliant blade (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 3-4). Flexible polymer blades accommodate surface irregularities and improve robustness versus rigid blades (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 5-7).

**Recoater speed and layer thickness.** Typical LPBF layer thicknesses are 20–100 µm, with 20–40 µm common for high-resolution aerospace/biomedical applications (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 3-5). Reported recoater speeds range from 10 mm/s (Bidare) through 25–75 mm/s to 500 mm/s (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 5-7, jabri2024powderspreadingeffects pages 1-3, jabri2024powderspreadingeffects pages 3-4). An optimal speed of 250 mm/s was identified for PA12 within a 125–500 mm/s range (jabri2024powderspreadingeffects pages 3-4). Lower speeds generally improve packing density and uniformity but increase build time (sehhat2021powderspreadingin pages 14-16, astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 5-7).

**Particle size effects.** A critical particle radius of ~21.8 µm was reported: reducing from 84.7 to 61.6 µm improved flowability, but reducing from 17.4 to 12.2 µm worsened it due to van der Waals forces (sehhat2021powderspreadingin pages 14-16). Powder cohesion—especially below 100 µm—promotes clustering and reduces flowability (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 3-5). For the user's 15–63 µm range, particles near the fine end may cause flow issues.

**Minimizing powder consumption.** Bidare's ~30 × 30 mm build area consumed very small powder quantities (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 7-9). The NIST powder spreading testbed uses a 200 × 100 mm baseplate (reduced width justified by scaling arguments), with stencil-based or rotating-cylinder heap delivery rather than large piston reservoirs (whiting2023powderspreadingtestbed pages 15-18). The Aconity Mini's cylindrical Ø140 mm bed and manual reservoir also reduce waste (maddison2022insitumonitoringand pages 61-66). Piston-fed designs (MIT/Hart) offer precise dosing but require the reservoir volume to match or exceed the build volume (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 3-4).

## 3. Inert-Gas Environment for Aluminium Alloys

**O₂ requirements.** For LPBF of aluminium alloys, chambers are purged to below 0.1% O₂ (~1000 ppm); operating levels are typically a few hundred ppm (bosio2021laserpowderbed pages 47-49, aydin2025theuseof pages 151-156). Even at 0.1–0.2% O₂, thin oxide layers form on aluminium, disrupting melt-pool stability, causing balling, reducing wettability, and promoting lack-of-fusion porosity (bosio2021laserpowderbed pages 53-56). Residual O₂ below 500 ppm can still react with aluminium and titanium to form oxides (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 18-22). For reactive alloys like Al-Mg-Sc/Zr, targeting <200 ppm is prudent.

**Purge strategies.** The NIST AMMT uses evacuate-and-backfill with argon or nitrogen (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 2-4). The DAMX supports optional vacuum below 10 mbar before argon backfill (aydin2025theuseof pages 106-110). Vacuum-purge-backfill is more effective at removing moisture and achieving low initial O₂ than flow-through purging alone (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 18-22). Humidity must also be removed from powder and chamber, as laser interaction can split moisture into hydrogen (causing porosity in aluminium) and oxygen (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 38-41).

**Cross-flow velocity.** For effective spatter and plume removal, a minimum cross-flow velocity of approximately 2.0 m/s is needed; below this, spatters rise vertically and vapor plumes are poorly removed, causing a sharp decrease in melt-pool penetration (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 5-6). In a commercial EOS M290, maximum cross-flow reaches ~3.5 m/s near the inlet nozzle (25 mm above the platform) and ~2 m/s at mid-plate (weaver2021inertgasflow pages 12-15). At 4 m/s nominal inlet velocity, volumetric flow was ~300 L/min (~18 m³/h) (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 3-4). Flow is non-uniform across the plate—highest near the inlet, lowest near the outlet—and is affected by the recoater body (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 3-4).

**Nozzle design.** The EOS M290 uses dual inlets: an upper inlet near the chamber roof (preventing recirculation toward the laser window) and a lower nozzle near the build plate. The standard nozzle has many small circular holes leading to larger channels; the grid nozzle uses rectangular channels with a bottom row projecting gas downward (weaver2021inertgasflow pages 9-12). The NIST AMMT nozzle is an array of 3.2 mm-diameter circular channels with a length-to-diameter ratio of ~15.5 (weaver2021inertgasflow pages 9-12). Bidare's system uses simple side-inlet ports reversible for gases heavier or lighter than air, with exhaust through a water bath (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3).

**Gas consumption and filtration.** At 300 L/min recirculating flow with fresh-gas top-up when O₂ exceeds 0.2%, consumption depends on chamber leak rate and build duration (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 3-4). For a small desktop chamber (~30–50 L volume), initial purge requires 3–5 chamber volumes (~100–250 L) and steady-state consumption is modest if leak-tight. Bidare's water-bath exhaust captures nanoparticles (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3); commercial machines use HEPA-class filters in recirculating loops.

## 4. Beam Delivery: Galvanometer Scanner vs. Gantry

**Galvanometer + f-theta lens.** This is the standard LPBF beam-delivery architecture. Galvo mirrors change position on microsecond timescales (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 51-53). The Sunny Galvo SG S9320D has a 20 mm aperture, <0.4 ms small-step response, ±12.5° scan angle, >99.9% linearity, <8 µrad repeatability, <50 ppm/°C scale drift, and <15 µrad/°C zero drift (aagaard2023legacylaserpowder pages 3-3). The XY2-100 protocol provides 16-bit position space per axis at 2 MHz update rate (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12). Typical f-theta focal lengths are 163 mm (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5) to 250+ mm; voice-coil dynamic focusing is an alternative that avoids the cost and beam-distortion issues of large f-theta lenses (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 53-56). Spot sizes of 50–100 µm are standard; the NIST AMMT achieves ~60 × 48 µm (D4σ) (lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 4-6), while DAMX reports 70 µm (aydin2025theuseof pages 106-110).

**Gantry-based systems.** XY gantry motion is used in some DPM/DAM systems but is fundamentally slower than galvo scanning for LPBF. No quantitative gantry acceleration data for LPBF were found in the reviewed literature. Galvo scanning at speeds of 500–2100 mm/s is routine for aluminium (bosio2021laserpowderbed pages 36-40); gantry systems would be limited to <100 mm/s by inertia.

**Fiber laser for aluminium.** AlSi10Mg has very low absorptivity at 1070 nm—approximately 9% (bosio2021laserpowderbed pages 36-40)—and 5–10% at 1064 nm for powder (aydin2025theuseof pages 82-86). Near-full density was obtained using 300 W at 500 mm/s, while higher productivity was achieved at 900 W / 2100 mm/s (bosio2021laserpowderbed pages 36-40). For a desktop machine, 300–500 W at 1070 nm is the practical minimum for aluminium processing. Blue lasers (450 nm) offer ~15% absorptivity but are currently more expensive and lower power (aydin2025theuseof pages 82-86).

**Protective window contamination.** The upper gas inlet directs flow to prevent process byproducts from reaching the laser lens or protective window (weaver2021inertgasflow pages 12-15). The MIT/Hart system uses a CaF₂ protective window (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 4-6). Back-reflection from aluminium is a serious concern; the NIST AMMT experienced coating failure on optical elements due to back-reflected/scattered laser power (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 8-9).

## 5. Controls and Electronics

**Scanner protocols.** The XY2-100 is the most common industrial galvanometer protocol: a 20-bit, 2 MHz asynchronous digital interface using four differential pairs (clock, sync, X-data, Y-data), with each control word containing 3 mode bits, 16 position bits, and 1 parity bit (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12, aagaard2023legacylaserpowder pages 2-3). The NIST AMMT uses XYPT command files at 100 kHz (lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 6-8).

**Laser modulation.** The DTU system provides 0–10 V analog power control (10-bit DAC, 0.24 W increments for a 250 W laser) plus a 24 V PWM enable signal supporting CW or pulsed operation up to 100 kHz (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 3-7). The Forhad thesis describes a similar architecture with 0–10 V analog (representing 0–1000 W) and 8-bit digital laser interfaces (forhad2023developingcontrolsystem pages 32-39).

**Open-source control.** The DTU Unified Systems Controller firmware is open-source, runs on Teensy 3.6, and coordinates scanner, laser, gas flow, motors, beam expander, and sensor inputs (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12, andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 12-13). NIST uses SimpleAM (SAM) for scan-path generation (lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 6-8). PySLM is an open-source Python library for scan-path planning. The Reiff et al. group demonstrated learning feedforward control via an interface card to laser and galvo using XY2-100 (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12).

**In-situ monitoring.** Common sensors in custom LPBF machines include: coaxial melt-pool cameras (lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 1-4), layer-wise cameras (lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 1-4), MWIR cameras (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 4-6), coaxial NIR photodiodes (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12), pyrometers, and O₂/flow sensors. The DTU controller provides three configurable sensor inputs accepting 0–10 V or 4–20 mA signals (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12).

## 6. Safety

**Laser safety.** A custom LPBF machine with a ≥200 W Class 4 fiber laser must comply with IEC 60825-1 (classification and requirements) and IEC 60825-4 (laser guards) internationally, or ANSI Z136.1 in the United States. The system must be a fully enclosed Class 1 enclosure with safety interlocks on all access panels, a laser-safe viewing window (OD ≥ 6+ at 1070 nm), emergency stop, and key switch. The NIST AMMT and MIT/Hart systems both use sealed enclosures with protective windows (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 2-4, penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 4-6). Back-reflection from aluminium requires particular attention: an isolator on the laser output fiber and baffling/cooling of scanner optics are essential (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 8-9).

**Combustible metal powder handling.** NFPA 484 (Standard for Combustible Metals) and NFPA 654 (Prevention of Fire and Dust Explosions) are the governing U.S. standards (mathias2024metalpowderas pages 19-20). Aluminium powder is specifically identified as presenting fire and explosion hazards (abenante2020designandfabrication pages 18-20, mathias2024metalpowderas pages 19-19). Key controls include: inert-atmosphere processing, elimination of ignition sources, spark-resistant tooling, anti-static equipment, wet separators or passivated filters for exhaust (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3), full-face respirators and full-body PPE, segregated powder-handling rooms with signage and access controls, anti-static vacuum cleaners for spills, and "sticky rooms" for personnel decontamination (abenante2020designandfabrication pages 18-20).

**Nanoparticle hazards.** LPBF laser-material interaction generates respirable nanoparticles and agglomerates that are deposited on internal machine surfaces and can be carried into offices, break rooms, and other non-production areas on clothing and equipment (waltera2019safetyduringhandling pages 3-6, waltera2019safetyduringhandling pages 6-9). Correctly functioning exhaust-air or recirculating-air filtration (HEPA) is essential; faulty filtration was identified as a source of internal contamination (waltera2019safetyduringhandling pages 6-9). Personal exposures during machine opening and cleaning can exceed stationary measurements because workers lean into the build chamber (waltera2019safetyduringhandling pages 3-6).

**Moisture.** Humidity must be removed from the chamber and powder because laser interaction can split moisture into hydrogen (which dissolves in molten aluminium and produces gas porosity) and oxygen (which forms oxides) (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 38-41).

## 7. Ranked Design Recommendations for a Sub-$60k Custom Desktop LPBF for Aluminium Alloy Screening

Based on the literature reviewed, the following design choices are ranked by strength of evidence and suitability for the stated application (small batches of 15–63 µm ultrasonically atomized aluminium powder, good spreadability, safety, automation, minimal powder volume, high-purity inert gas):

1. **Small build area (~50 × 50 mm) with piston-fed or hopper-fed powder delivery.** The Bidare system demonstrated that a ~30 × 30 mm build area uses minimal powder and achieves >99% density (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 1-2). A 50 × 50 mm plate is a reasonable compromise between coupon variety and powder conservation. A small piston reservoir or gravity hopper above the recoater minimizes dead volume compared to large side-feed pistons.

2. **Compliant-blade or wide-nose-blade recoater at 50–150 mm/s.** Rigid metal blades jam with fine powders; compliant recoaters (silicon cord, rubber, or polymer blade) solved this problem in multiple testbeds (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5, penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 3-4). Wide-nose blades produce higher packing than sharp-edge blades (salehi2023optimisingspreadlayerquality pages 12-13). A speed of ~100 mm/s balances packing density and build time (jabri2024powderspreadingeffects pages 3-4, sehhat2021powderspreadingin pages 14-16). Layer thickness of 30–50 µm suits the 15–63 µm PSD.

3. **300–500 W single-mode fiber laser at 1070 nm.** AlSi10Mg absorptivity is only ~9% at this wavelength, requiring ≥300 W for near-full-density parts at standard scan speeds (bosio2021laserpowderbed pages 36-40). A 500 W SPI redPOWER qube (used at MIT and NIST) costs ~$15–25k and provides headroom. An optical isolator is mandatory to protect against aluminium back-reflection (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 8-9).

4. **Galvanometer scanner with XY2-100 digital protocol and f-theta lens.** The Sunny Galvo SG S9320D (~$3–5k) or Raylase MS-II-14 are proven choices with <0.4 ms step response, <8 µrad repeatability, and 20 mm aperture (aagaard2023legacylaserpowder pages 3-3, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5). A 163–254 mm f-theta lens provides 50–80 µm spots over the small build area. XY2-100 is the standard, non-proprietary scanner interface (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12).

5. **Vacuum-purge-backfill chamber with recirculating argon cross-flow ≥2 m/s.** For aluminium, target <500 ppm O₂ (operating) with a <0.1% (1000 ppm) hard interlock (bosio2021laserpowderbed pages 47-49, reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 5-6). Initial vacuum evacuation removes moisture and achieves low O₂ faster than flow-through purging (aydin2025theuseof pages 106-110, revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 18-22). A small chamber (~30 L) requires modest gas; recirculating flow at 2–3 m/s with a HEPA filter and O₂ sensor maintains atmosphere quality (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 5-6, weaver2021inertgasflow pages 12-15). A zirconia O₂ sensor (ppm-level) and dew-point sensor are recommended.

6. **Open-source embedded controller (Teensy 3.6 or equivalent ARM).** The DTU Unified Systems Controller provides deterministic XY2-100 galvo control, 0–10 V laser power, PWM modulation, PID gas-flow control, and sensor acquisition on a single low-cost microcontroller (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12, andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 3-7). Its open-source firmware is extensible and avoids vendor lock-in. Alternatively, an NI cRIO provides industrial-grade determinism but at higher cost (~$5–10k vs. ~$100 for Teensy) (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 4-6).

7. **Coaxial photodiode or camera for melt-pool monitoring.** A coaxial NIR photodiode (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12) is the lowest-cost in-situ sensor (~$200–500) and provides real-time melt-pool intensity feedback. A coaxial camera (Mikrotron or similar) adds spatial resolution but costs $5–15k (lane2020processmonitoringdataset pages 1-4).

8. **Full Class 1 laser enclosure with NFPA 484 powder controls.** The enclosure must have interlocked panels per IEC 60825-4, OD 6+ viewing window, emergency stop, and key switch. Aluminium powder handling requires inert-atmosphere glove-box or sealed transfer, wet separator or water-bath exhaust, HEPA filtration, anti-static tooling, full-face respirator and full-body PPE, and segregated handling area (abenante2020designandfabrication pages 18-20, mathias2024metalpowderas pages 19-20, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3).

9. **Gas-knife or cross-flow nozzle with dual-inlet design.** A lower nozzle array near the build plate (3–5 mm diameter channels, L/D ~15) provides laminar cross-flow for spatter removal, while an upper inlet prevents recirculation toward the laser window (weaver2021inertgasflow pages 9-12, weaver2021inertgasflow pages 12-15).

10. **Stepper/servo-actuated Z-axis and recoater carriage with ball-screw drives.** The NIST PST uses a Newport XPS controller with ball-screw linear slides (whiting2023powderspreadingtestbed pages 15-18). Industrial servo motors provide smooth motion and precise layer-height control; the Bidare system uses stepper motors with fine-pitch screws (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5).

**Estimated cost breakdown (approximate):** 500 W fiber laser $15–25k; galvo scanner + f-theta $3–8k; controller electronics $1–3k; linear stages and motors $2–5k; chamber fabrication $3–5k; gas system (valves, sensors, filter) $2–4k; enclosure and safety $3–5k; optics and protective window $1–3k; miscellaneous (wiring, software development) $2–5k. **Total: approximately $32–63k**, consistent with the sub-$60k target when component choices are optimized.
