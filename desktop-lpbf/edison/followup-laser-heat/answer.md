Question: We are designing a small research laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) machine for aluminium alloys with interchangeable heated build modules: Ø25, Ø50 and Ø75 mm plates on a 6061 heater block with 24 V cartridge heaters, a 200 °C set point, a Macor standoff thermal break, a 316L build cylinder (40 mm stroke) sealed with FKM O-rings under a 6061 chamber floor, and an NdFeB pot-magnet Z coupling at the rod end. Our axisymmetric finite-element model says each module loses only 30-97 W at 200 °C with the laser off, while a dense Al layout at 350 W (absorptance about 0.45, beam on 50-70 % of each layer cycle) deposits a time-averaged 80-110 W. The heater then switches off and the plate drifts to about 290 °C by the end of the stroke unless the module is cooled. Separately, the 500 W single-mode fibre laser we were quoted is forced-air cooled (1.7 kW drawn, 10-35 °C ambient) and would sit in an enclosed vertical-lift storage module.

Please review the peer-reviewed literature (and machine-builder documentation where it is the only source), with citations and numbers:
1. Measured build-plate, substrate or part temperature rise from laser heat input during LPBF, with and without platform heating: how far above the set point, over how many layers, and how it scales with scanned area fraction, interlayer time and plate size (small plates especially).
2. How commercial and research LPBF machines with platform heating (EOS, SLM/Nikon, Aconity, Trumpf, Renishaw, open-architecture rigs) take heat out of the build cylinder or platform: water-cooled cylinders or piston heads, interlayer dwell, closed-loop platform temperature control; reported overshoot or control performance.
3. What uncontrolled platform temperature drift (for example from 200 to 250-300 °C) does to Al alloys (AlSi10Mg, Al-Mg-Sc-Zr, 6xxx and 7xxx): in-situ ageing or overageing, Mg and Zn evaporation, cracking, density, residual stress and dimensional accuracy.
4. Temperature limits and design practice for parts near heated build platforms: FKM seals, NdFeB versus SmCo magnets, piston seal friction at temperature.
5. Air-cooled fibre lasers of a few hundred watts in enclosed cabinets or automated cells: reported derating, failures or ambient-temperature problems, and the trade-off against water-cooled units.

## Literature Review: Thermal Management Considerations for a Research LPBF Machine with Heated Interchangeable Build Modules for Aluminium Alloys

### 1. Measured Build-Plate and Part Temperature Rise from Laser Heat Input

The most systematic quantitative study of laser-induced heat accumulation in LPBF was conducted by Chaudry et al. (2022) on 316L stainless steel cuboidal specimens built in an SLM 280HL with a 100 °C platform preheat. Using mid-wave infrared thermography, they measured the temperature at the top of the freshly recoated powder surface at the end of each inter-layer time (ILT). The results demonstrated that ILT is the dominant variable controlling heat accumulation:

- **Short ILT (18 s):** The preheating temperature rose from the 100 °C set point to approximately **545 °C** at the top of the 70 mm build, with a steep initial gradient that decreased as convective and conductive losses grew (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 7-9, chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 9-10).
- **Medium ILT (65 s):** The maximum temperature reached approximately **230 °C** (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 9-10).
- **Long ILT (116 s):** The temperature stabilised near **170 °C**, indicating that an approximate equilibrium between energy input and dissipation had been reached (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 9-10).

Volumetric energy density (VED) also matters: at short ILT, increasing VED from 49 to 82 J/mm³ raised the peak temperature further, demonstrating that higher laser power and slower scan speeds compound the heat accumulation problem (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 9-10). The build platform acts as the principal heat sink during early layers, with an effective conductive heat-transfer coefficient on the order of 1000 W/(m²·°C) in modelling studies (lu2023thermomechanicalsimulationof pages 6-8). As build height increases, the growing thermal resistance between the scan plane and the cooled platform reduces its heat-sink effectiveness, so accumulation accelerates if ILT is too short (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 1-2).

For aluminium alloys on small platforms—the scenario of your machine—the thermal situation is qualitatively similar but potentially more severe because aluminium's high thermal conductivity draws laser heat rapidly into the platform, while the small module thermal mass limits its heat-storage capacity. Heugue et al. (2026) reported that large A357 funnel-shaped specimens built on a Nikon NXG12 SLM600 (12 lasers, 650 W each, 150 °C platform) experienced such severe heat accumulation at approximately mid-height that surface blistering occurred and the build had to be stopped (heugue2026insituageing pages 4-6). This demonstrates that even at a modest 150 °C set point, dense, poorly ventilated geometries on a heated platform can locally reach temperatures well above 230 °C (heugue2026insituageing pages 15-16). On your Ø25 mm module with only 30 W conductive loss at 200 °C and an estimated 80–110 W time-averaged laser input, the net surplus of 50–80 W into a very small thermal mass will produce measurable drift within tens of layers unless active cooling or longer ILTs are used.

### 2. How Commercial and Research LPBF Machines Manage Platform Heat

**Platform preheating capabilities.** Standard commercial LPBF systems commonly provide resistive baseplate preheating up to approximately 200–500 °C (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 10-11). The SLM 280 platform (SLM Solutions/Nikon) is available with up to 500 °C preheating, and this was used to substantially reduce cracking and distortion in carbon-enriched H11 tool steel (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 10-11). Aconity 3D has developed customised machines with platform preheating above 1000 °C, originally motivated by processing of oxide ceramics at ~1600 °C (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 10-11). The EOS M290-HT prototype combines a 900 °C resistively heated baseplate with a VCSEL-based upper-powder-layer heater capable of 1200 °C, controlled via infrared camera and dynamic feedback, with process-chamber cooling provided by purified inert gas (wartbichler2022laserpowderbed pages 2-4, wartbichler2022laserpowderbed pages 4-5).

**Heat removal approaches.** The peer-reviewed literature is notably sparse on the mechanical details of how heat is removed from the piston or build cylinder in commercial machines. The build platform is universally recognised as the primary heat-extraction path: conduction through the substrate, into the piston head, and ultimately into the machine frame (maddison2022insitumonitoringand pages 12-16, lu2023thermomechanicalsimulationof pages 6-8). The Maddison (2022) thesis on an Aconity system mentions a control thermocouple for substrate temperature, implying closed-loop baseplate heating control (maddison2022insitumonitoringand pages 170-176). In standard machines operating at modest temperatures (≤200 °C), natural conduction to the machine body and the inert-gas atmosphere appears to be the dominant cooling mechanism, supplemented by ILT-based thermal management. Where higher temperatures are used, the EOS-M290-HT explicitly incorporates process-chamber cooling via inert gas flow (wartbichler2022laserpowderbed pages 2-4). Water-cooled pistons, build cylinders, and cold plates are described in manufacturer technical documentation and patent literature but are rarely detailed in peer-reviewed journal papers. For your design, the 30–97 W steady-state loss predicted by your FE model at 200 °C is insufficient to reject the 80–110 W of time-averaged laser input; active cooling (water channels in the piston head or cylinder wall) or deliberate ILT extension will be essential.

**Interlayer dwell as a thermal management strategy.** The Chaudry et al. (2022) results demonstrate that increasing ILT from 18 s to 116 s can reduce peak temperature from 545 °C to 170 °C under identical power and VED conditions (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 9-10). Bugatti and Colosimo (2024) noted that a constant ~28–30 s recoating dwell was sufficient to prevent cross-layer accumulation in their builds on an EOS system (bugatti2024predictingtheroughness pages 2-3). Extending ILT is therefore the simplest lever for your machine, though it reduces productivity.

### 3. Effects of Uncontrolled Platform Temperature Drift on Aluminium Alloys

#### AlSi10Mg and A357 (Al-Si-Mg): In-situ Ageing and Overageing

The Al-Si-Mg alloy system is acutely sensitive to the prolonged thermal exposure inherent in a heated-platform LPBF process. Bosio et al. (2021) systematically studied AlSi10Mg on platforms at 45, 100, 150, and 200 °C. At 150 °C, peak-age hardness of ~138.6 HV was reached in approximately 2.5 h of holding; builds exceeding ~13 h began to overage (bosio2021productionstrategyfor pages 4-8, bosio2021productionstrategyfor pages 2-4). At 200 °C, samples were already overaged after roughly 10 h of printing, with as-built hardness of only 100.5 ± 3 HV; 100 h isothermal treatment at 200 °C reduced hardness further to ~91 HV (bosio2021productionstrategyfor pages 4-8). At 250 °C, hardness fell below 80 HV, and distortion was essentially eliminated—but at the cost of severely degraded strength (bosio2021productionstrategyfor pages 1-2).

Macías et al. (2020) compared AlSi10Mg fabricated at 35 °C and 200 °C platform temperatures on an EOS M290. The 200 °C material exhibited significantly lower strength (while residual stress was negligible at ~0 MPa versus ~50 MPa at 35 °C). The Si-rich eutectic network was coarser, less interconnected, and more prone to damage initiation at 200 °C (macias2020influenceonmicrostructure pages 1-5). The strength improvement of LPBF over cast AlSi10Mg was 167% at 35 °C but substantially less at 200 °C (macias2020influenceonmicrostructure pages 1-5).

For A357 (AlSi7Mg0.6), Heugue et al. (2026) demonstrated that large-scale components on a Nikon NXG12 with a 150 °C platform experienced up to a **30% reduction in mechanical properties** in regions where heat dissipation was insufficient (heugue2026insituageing pages 1-2). Local temperatures exceeding approximately 230 °C caused hardness below 90 HV, interpreted as severe overageing with dissolution/coarsening of β″-Mg₅Si₆ precipitates and spheroidisation of the eutectic Si network (heugue2026insituageing pages 15-16). Post-build cooling contributed 15–35% of total equivalent ageing time, further exacerbating the problem (heugue2026insituageing pages 12-15).

Lehmhus et al. (2022) showed that stress-relieving AlSi10Mg at 350 °C/2 h reduced UTS to ~280 MPa at room temperature (versus ~450 MPa as-built at 35 °C), though the characteristic cellular microstructure was not completely destroyed (lehmhus2022hightemperaturemechanicalproperties pages 22-23).

**Implication for your design:** A drift from 200 °C to 290 °C over a 40 mm stroke with a few-hour build represents a worst-case overageing scenario for Al-Si-Mg alloys, transforming the as-built microstructure toward an overaged condition with potentially 30–50% loss in hardness and strength. Closed-loop cooling to hold the platform within ±10 °C of the 200 °C set point is therefore metallurgically necessary.

#### 7xxx and High-Strength Al-Zn-Mg(-Cu) Alloys: Evaporation and Cracking

For EN AW 7075, Velikajne et al. (2026) measured consistent evaporation of approximately **1 wt.% Zn and 0.3 wt.% Mg** during LPBF, independent of baseplate preheating temperature from 25 to 400 °C (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 1-2, velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 4-6). This evaporation is driven by the extreme melt-pool temperatures (well above the ~907 °C boiling point of Zn and ~1090 °C for Mg) and is therefore essentially unaffected by substrate-level thermal management (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 8-11, li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 38-42). Moderate preheating at 100–200 °C showed no significant reduction in crack density, while 400 °C promoted columnar crack networks and reduced hardness through stress relaxation and altered grain orientation (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 1-2, velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 8-11). Stopyra et al. (2020) reported that AA7075 cracking was driven by microsegregation of Zn, Mg, Cu, and Si to grain boundaries and the low 338 °C eutectic temperature of the Al-Mg-Zn system (stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 27-31). Preheating to 300 °C altered thermal gradients but did not change the solidification-crack fracture mode (stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 27-31).

For high-solute Al-Zn-Mg alloys, Babu et al. (2020) reported vaporisation losses of ~2.21 wt.% Zn and ~0.2 wt.% Mg for Al-18Zn-4Mg, with fine vaporisation cracks observed (babu2020laserpowderbed pages 4-4). Zhou et al. (2020) showed that greater evaporation reduced the solidification range and cracking severity, while Sc-Zr additions eliminated cracks through grain refinement on Al₃(Sc,Zr) nucleants (zhou2020processdependentcompositionmicrostructure pages 1-2).

For 2xxx alloys, Schuster et al. (2022) measured Mg loss from 1.95 to 1.55 wt.% (~20.5% of initial content) in a Zr-modified 2618 alloy, attributing 10–25 µm gas pores to Mg evaporation, though relative density remained above 99.5% (schuster2022processabilitymicrostructureand pages 9-10).

#### 6xxx Alloys

The peer-reviewed literature specifically addressing 6061/6063 LPBF under heated-platform conditions with temperature drift is limited. However, the general sensitivity of the Mg₂Si precipitation system to temperatures in the 150–300 °C range is well-established, and the evaporation concerns for Mg apply equally. Zhou et al. (2020) reported that 500 °C preheating was used for AA6061 to eliminate cracking, though this introduces the same overageing trade-off (zhou2020processdependentcompositionmicrostructure pages 1-2).

### 4. Temperature Limits and Design Practice for Build-Module Components

#### FKM (Viton) O-Ring Seals

Standard FKM compounds have a maximum continuous operating temperature of **200 °C** (−20 to +200 °C), with short-term excursions permissible to approximately **230 °C** (solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 31-33, solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 43-45). Heat-ageing qualification testing is conducted at 250 °C for 72 h, with acceptance criteria of ≤10 Shore A hardness change and ≤25% loss in tensile strength and elongation (solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 43-45). Above 200 °C, FKM undergoes accelerated compression set, hardening, and loss of elasticity, with the specific degradation rate depending strongly on compound formulation, sealed medium, and pressure (solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 45-47, solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 29-31). For your 316L build cylinder sealed with FKM O-rings at a 200 °C set point, the seals are operating at their rated limit. A temperature drift to 290 °C would be catastrophic for seal life and would likely cause leakage of powder and/or inert gas. FEP/PFA-encapsulated O-rings with FKM cores extend the range slightly to ~205 °C but do not solve the problem if the cylinder wall reaches 250+ °C (solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 110-111). Alternative elastomers for higher temperatures include FFKM (perfluoroelastomer, rated to ~325 °C) and PTFE-based piston seals.

#### NdFeB versus SmCo Magnets

The Nd₂Fe₁₄B compound has a Curie temperature of approximately **312 °C**, but the practical maximum operating temperature is far lower—typically **80–180 °C** depending on grade and magnetic circuit (liu2019sm–cohightemperaturepermanent pages 1-3, liu2019sm–cohightemperaturepermanent pages 18-19). Standard bonded NdFeB grades become unstable near ~100 °C (an2019magneticpropertiesof pages 1-3). The coercivity temperature coefficient of NdFeB is approximately **−0.51 to −0.14 %/°C** (depending on SmCo blending), meaning that a 90 °C temperature rise from ambient reduces coercivity by 25–45% (an2019magneticpropertiesof pages 1-3). Dy- and Tb-enhanced sintered grades can extend the operating range to approximately 180 °C, but heavy rare-earth additions are costly and grades become unsuitable for dynamic applications above this threshold (liu2019sm–cohightemperaturepermanent pages 1-3).

SmCo magnets offer dramatically better thermal stability. SmCo₅ and Sm₂Co₁₇ have Curie temperatures of approximately **727 °C and 920 °C**, respectively (liu2019sm–cohightemperaturepermanent pages 5-7). Conventional commercial SmCo grades are rated to **~300 °C**, while advanced Sm₂(Co,Fe,Cu,Zr)₁₇ compositions have demonstrated linear demagnetisation curves up to **550 °C**, with coercivity of ~1 MA/m at 400 °C and 0.72 MA/m at 500 °C (liu2019sm–cohightemperaturepermanent pages 10-11, liu2019sm–cohightemperaturepermanent pages 8-10). The near-room-temperature coercivity temperature coefficient for SmCo is approximately **−0.3 %/°C**, compared with **−0.9 %/°C** for Nd₂Fe₁₄B (liu2019sm–cohightemperaturepermanent pages 10-11).

**Implication for your Z-coupling design:** An NdFeB pot magnet at the piston rod end, with the Macor thermal break providing some insulation, may be acceptable if the magnet temperature can be maintained below ~120 °C. However, if the platform drifts to 290 °C and the Macor break is insufficient, partial demagnetisation is a serious risk. Switching to an SmCo pot magnet (rated to ≥300 °C with substantial margin) would eliminate this concern at the cost of a ~30–40% reduction in room-temperature holding force for similar volume.

#### Piston Seal Friction at Temperature

Dynamic elastomeric seal friction in reciprocating applications depends on surface roughness, sliding speed, squeeze, sealed medium, and temperature (usmani2024elastomericsealassessment pages 30-34). Nikas et al. found that friction, leakage, and wear in elastomeric seals have a complex multivariable dependency; a highly polished shaft does not necessarily minimise friction, and minimum leakage occurs at low pressures and low speeds (~100 mm/s) at the cost of higher friction (usmani2024elastomericsealassessment pages 30-34). At elevated temperatures, FKM hardness increases and elasticity decreases, which can increase breakaway friction and reduce the effectiveness of the hydrodynamic lubricating film. No peer-reviewed quantitative temperature-friction curves for FKM O-rings in the specific 200–300 °C range were identified, but the general trend is that above the rated limit, compression set accelerates, sealing force degrades, and stick-slip risk increases. For your 40 mm stroke at the slow speeds typical of LPBF Z-axis motion, friction forces are likely modest (single-digit Newtons for small-bore O-rings), but seal life will be sharply curtailed if the cylinder wall exceeds 200 °C.

### 5. Air-Cooled Fibre Lasers in Enclosed Cabinets

#### Heat Generation and Wall-Plug Efficiency

Ytterbium-doped single-mode fibre lasers (the dominant LPBF source at ~1070 nm) have a quantum defect of approximately **5–8%** (the energy difference between the 976 nm pump and ~1070 nm signal wavelengths) (ballato2024prospectsandchallenges pages 1-3). Additional losses from diode-pump coupling, spontaneous emission, non-radiative relaxation, and power-supply conversion bring the overall wall-plug efficiency to typically **>30%** for industrial fibre lasers, with reported lifetimes approaching 100,000 hours (zervas2014highpowerfiber pages 14-15). Your quoted 500 W source draws 1.7 kW, implying a wall-plug efficiency of ~29%, consistent with this range. The remaining **~1.2 kW** must be dissipated as heat from the diode pump modules, fibre spool, and power electronics.

#### Air versus Water Cooling

The fundamental contrast is in convective heat-transfer coefficients: approximately **100 W/(m²·K) for flowing air** versus **>1000 W/(m²·K) for flowing water** (ballato2024prospectsandchallenges pages 4-6). Ballato et al. (2024) calculated that for a 10 kW fibre laser generating 1 kW of waste heat distributed over 12 m of fibre in still air, the core temperature would reach ~835 K (562 °C), which is unacceptably high; forced water cooling reduced the temperature rise by approximately a factor of **5.5**, to ~100 K above ambient (ballato2024prospectsandchallenges pages 3-4). The same physics applies at smaller scales: a 500 W source rejecting 1.2 kW into an enclosed cabinet will raise the internal air temperature by an amount determined by the cabinet volume, insulation, and exhaust flow. With the quoted ambient specification of 10–35 °C, the cabinet air can easily exceed the diode-pump operating limit (typically 40–50 °C for telecom-grade pump diodes) if airflow is restricted.

#### Practical Concerns for the Proposed Vertical-Lift Storage Module

No peer-reviewed papers were found that specifically document derating, failures, or ambient-temperature problems of LPBF-class (~200–1000 W) air-cooled single-mode fibre lasers in enclosed automated-storage environments. However, the physics is clear: 1.2 kW of waste heat in a closed volume will produce a steady-state temperature rise ΔT = Q/(h·A), where A is the available convective surface area and h ≈ 100 W/(m²·K) for forced air. Even with a 1 m² internal heat-exchange surface, ΔT ≈ 12 °C per kW of waste heat; at 35 °C ambient, internal air could reach ~47 °C, potentially exceeding diode limits. Forced exhaust to the room or facility HVAC is effectively mandatory. Water-cooled fibre lasers eliminate this concern by piping the heat to a remote chiller, but add plumbing complexity, leak risk, and potential vibration from pumps and flow (ballato2024prospectsandchallenges pages 4-6). The vibration concern is non-trivial: active cooling introduces mechanical movement that can affect laser phase through the photoelastic effect, though at LPBF powers this is generally not a process-quality issue (ballato2024prospectsandchallenges pages 4-6).

Heat generation in fibre lasers can also cause transverse mode instability (TMI) and thermal lensing at high powers, though these effects are more significant above ~1 kW and are unlikely to be limiting at 500 W (nemova2024briefreviewof pages 2-4, zervas2014highpowerfiber pages 14-15). Photodarkening—structural colour-centre formation that increases background absorption—is another thermal concern in Yb-doped fibres, producing excess heating that degrades performance over time (nemova2024briefreviewof pages 2-4).

### Key Recommendations from the Literature

1. **Active platform cooling is essential.** Your FE model predicts net heat input of 50–80 W above the 30–97 W conductive loss, which will drive the platform well above 200 °C within the first hour of a dense Al build unless active cooling (e.g., water channels in the piston head or cylinder wall) can reject the surplus.

2. **Interlayer dwell time is the simplest thermal lever.** Chaudry et al. showed that extending ILT from 18 s to 116 s reduced peak accumulation from 545 °C to 170 °C on a 100 °C platform (chaudry2022experimentalandnumerical pages 9-10). For your small modules, even modest ILT extension may suffice, but it must be balanced against productivity and total thermal exposure (which drives overageing).

3. **The 200 °C set point is at the limit of multiple components.** FKM O-rings are rated to exactly 200 °C continuous; NdFeB magnets lose significant coercivity above ~120 °C; and AlSi10Mg overages in ~10 h at 200 °C. Any uncontrolled drift above set point simultaneously threatens seal integrity, magnet coupling, and part quality.

4. **SmCo is the safer magnet choice.** With a 300+ °C rating, conventional SmCo provides margin against platform drift, whereas NdFeB pot magnets require assured thermal isolation below ~120 °C (liu2019sm–cohightemperaturepermanent pages 1-3, liu2019sm–cohightemperaturepermanent pages 5-7).

5. **For 7xxx alloys, the platform temperature has minimal effect on the dominant defects.** Zn and Mg evaporation (~1 wt.% Zn, ~0.3 wt.% Mg for 7075) is driven by melt-pool temperatures and is independent of platform heating from 25 to 400 °C (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 1-2). Hot cracking is also not suppressed by preheating alone (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 8-11).

6. **The air-cooled laser must exhaust to the room.** At 1.2 kW waste heat in an enclosed vertical-lift module, internal air temperature will exceed diode-pump limits unless the cabinet is actively ventilated. A water-cooled unit eliminates this risk but adds plumbing and leak-management complexity (ballato2024prospectsandchallenges pages 3-4, ballato2024prospectsandchallenges pages 4-6).
