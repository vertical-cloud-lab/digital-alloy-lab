### 1.1 Protective Window — Spatter and Condensate

Metal condensate particles (<1 µm) and larger spatter (10–100+ µm) deposit on the process window and any sacrificial cover slide during LPBF processing. Even modest contamination is consequential: at 400 W with only 0.5% additional absorptivity from deposited condensate on a fused-silica window, simulations predicted a focal shift of approximately −21.6 mm, while experiments on progressively contaminated windows measured shifts of −22 to −30 mm and beam-waist growth from 35 µm to 53 µm (bonhoff2017experimentalandtheoretical pages 6-10, bonhoff2017experimentalandtheoretical pages 4-6). Direct transmission loss remained below an estimated 1%, but the focal shift and beam-quality degradation were far more detrimental to process consistency (bonhoff2017experimentalandtheoretical pages 6-10). Continued operation without cleaning can cause catastrophic thermal damage to the window (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 22-25). Gas knives, downdraft arrangements, cooling, and post-build cleaning are standard controls (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 51-53).

### 1.2 Gas Flow — Cross-Flow Non-Uniformity and Recirculation

Shielding gas flow must remove vapor plume and spatter while maintaining inert atmosphere. Porosity remained below 0.1% at cross-flow velocities of 2.5–4.0 m/s, but decreased sharply below approximately 2.0 m/s, with unstable melt pools, balling, necking, and increased porosity (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 5-6, reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 4-5). Commercial machine measurements show substantial spatial variation: approximately 4 m/s near the inlet decaying to approximately 1 m/s near the outlet (shen2020influenceofgas pages 1-2). In small chambers, the vertical and lateral margins above the build plate are reduced, making recirculation near the window much more likely; recirculated soot can cross the laser path, deposit on the window, or contaminate the powder bed (griggs2021atestbedfor pages 3-7). Baffles and guide vanes merely shifted recirculation zones in one compact-chamber study; only a secondary upper nozzle successfully suppressed window-side recirculation (griggs2021atestbedfor pages 3-7). Excessive flow velocity can entrain loose powder from the bed, so an upper limit must also be established (shen2020influenceofgas pages 1-2, abeyta2024anexperimentalapproach pages 26-31).

### 1.3 Recoater — Blade Wear, Collisions, and Streaking

Rigid blades can mechanically interact with protruding spatter, consolidated features, distorted thin walls, or upward-curled edges, causing layer disturbance, blade damage, and build termination (skacelik2026experimentalandcomputational pages 1-4, skacelik2026experimentalandcomputational pages 21-26, astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 5-7). Collision experiments across 20–200 mm/s and 5–20 µm layers showed that rigid ceramic blades produced collision and rebound marks, while compliant rubber blades imposed negligible mechanical stress even when clearance was fully closed (skacelik2026experimentalandcomputational pages 1-4, skacelik2026experimentalandcomputational pages 21-26). A nicked blade edge creates a repeatable longitudinal streak defect. Extending the cooling/stabilization interval before recoating prevented collisions in one study (skacelik2026experimentalandcomputational pages 21-26). Higher recoater velocity generally reduces packing density and coverage, increases roughness and segregation, and can cause particle splash (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 5-7, sehhat2021powderspreadingin pages 6-8). Reported blade speeds for PBF extend to approximately 150 mm/s; lower speeds generally improve rearrangement quality (oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 3-5).

### 1.4 Powder Dosing and Spreading — Short Feed, Segregation

Insufficient powder delivery causes short feed and incomplete layer coverage. During spreading, larger particles tend to remain near the front of the heap while smaller particles migrate into voids and may deposit earlier, producing layer-to-layer size segregation (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 5-7). Humidity creates capillary bridges between particles, increasing cohesion and reducing mobility, with agglomeration, density fluctuations, poor coverage, and spreading instability (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 5-7). Chamber gas flows can redistribute loose powder and create local powder-bed morphology variations (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 5-7).

### 1.5 Electrostatic Charging of Powder

Triboelectric charging occurs during powder–powder, powder–hopper, and powder–recoater contact. AlSi10Mg powder acquired negative charge of approximately −2.80 to −5.70 nC/m² after contact with stainless steel, dependent on flow rate (ramirez2026influenceofpowder pages 109-116). Electrostatic forces add to cohesion and friction; if comparable to or greater than gravity, they impede flow, promote adhesion and agglomeration, and alter segregation (alchikhsulaiman2020powderspreadingand pages 149-152, alchikhsulaiman2020powderspreadingand pages 129-133). Smaller particles tend to charge oppositely to larger particles of the same material, enabling charge-driven size segregation (alchikhsulaiman2020powderspreadingand pages 149-152). Lower humidity increases persistent charge (alchikhsulaiman2020powderspreadingand pages 43-48). Aluminium sits toward the positive end of the triboelectric series relative to steel but toward the negative end relative to borosilicate glass (alchikhsulaiman2020powderspreadingand pages 149-152, alchikhsulaiman2020powderspreadingand pages 129-133).

### 1.6 Oxygen and Moisture Ingress

Oxygen can enter through chamber leaks, filter changes, and low-pressure regions near gas recirculation pumps, producing oxygen spikes (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 22-25). Moisture desorbs from powder, chamber walls, porous insulation, and seals after backfill and heating. Under laser exposure, water can decompose into hydrogen and oxygen; released oxygen oxidises powder and alters build properties, while hydrogen causes gas porosity in molten aluminium and can embrittle high-strength Al alloys (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 38-41). One commercial system automatically repurged when oxygen exceeded 0.2% (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 3-4), but aluminium alloys likely require tighter limits.

### 1.7 Thermal Drift of Optics

Thermal lensing arises when laser energy absorbed in transmissive optical components and their coatings creates refractive-index gradients and mechanical deformation. This shifts the focal position and alters the minimum achievable spot size (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 56-59, revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 48-51). Open-loop Z-focus control can produce dynamic errors, while closed-loop systems may exhibit underdamped or overcompensating responses (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 51-53).

### 1.8 Z-Axis Positioning and Layer-Thickness Error

Z-axis error directly changes effective layer thickness and recoater clearance. Backlash, compliance, seal drag, stick–slip, thermal expansion, platform rocking, magnetic-coupling slip, and insufficient settling all contribute (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 18-22, revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 51-53). One modular research testbed specified 5 µm minimum vertical stage increments, but this is not equivalent to absolute accuracy or repeatability (oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 1-3, oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 3-5). The proposed magnetic Z coupling and bayonet/drawbar plate retention introduce additional compliance and potential slip that must be characterized under load.

### 1.9 Build Piston Seals and Powder Leakage

Peer-reviewed LPBF literature provides remarkably little quantitative data on piston-seal leakage rates or service life. The general mechanism is well understood: powder migrates into annular gaps, embeds at the sliding interface, raises friction, causes stick–slip and scoring, and loads the Z drive with hysteresis. Because commanded layers are only 30–50 µm, micron-scale stick–slip or plate rocking is significant to process quality (oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 1-3).

### Key Concerns Elaborated

**Fused-silica window material.** Among the three window materials evaluated by Bonhoff et al., fused silica exhibited the largest thermo-optical sensitivity due to its large thermo-optic coefficient. At 400 W and 0.5% added absorption, fused silica produced a simulated focus shift of −21.6 mm and approximately 30% beam-waist growth; N-FK51A (an athermalized glass) shifted only about −1.3 mm under identical modeled conditions (bonhoff2017experimentalandtheoretical pages 6-10, bonhoff2017experimentalandtheoretical pages 4-6). The Griggs et al. high-pressure testbed selected sapphire for the structural pressure window due to its high fracture toughness and NIR transmission, with a sacrificial fused-quartz microscope slide suspended between the build area and the sapphire window (griggs2021atestbedfor pages 3-7). For the proposed machine, the 3 mm fused-silica cover slide will accumulate contamination rapidly; replacing it with an athermalized glass or using sapphire for the main window and a thin, inexpensive sacrificial borosilicate slide would reduce thermally induced process drift.

**Gas flow velocity and chamber design.** The proposed 2 m/s cross-flow through 120 × 8 mm slots sits precisely at the reported critical threshold below which process quality degrades sharply (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 5-6, reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 4-5). In a 6.4 L chamber with only 120 mm of build-plate clearance, recirculation is a substantial risk. Griggs et al. found that in a small chamber, baffles and guide vanes were ineffective; only a secondary nozzle near the window suppressed recirculation (griggs2021atestbedfor pages 3-7). The EOS M290 study by Medinilla et al. documented a recirculation region below the lower vent that was corrected only by raising the build platform to eliminate the backward-facing step between the inlet and the build surface (wu2025improvingshieldinggas pages 2-4, wu2025improvingshieldinggas pages 7-9).

**Preheating limitation.** The 200 °C maximum is adequate for AlSi10Mg, where platform preheating at 200 °C modestly affects microstructure and residual stress (jatti2024investigatingtheeffect pages 3-4). However, for EN AW 7075, preheating at 100–200 °C showed no significant reduction in crack density; at 400 °C a transition to columnar crack networks occurred with reduced hardness (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 1-2, velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 8-11, velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 6-8). For Zr-modified 6182, crack-free parts with >99.0% density required 500 °C preheating (riener2022processabilityofhighstrength pages 1-2). If the machine is intended to screen 2xxx/6xxx/7xxx alloys, 200 °C is insufficient.

## 3. Dose-from-Above Powder Delivery Systems

The proposed system uses swappable stainless wedge hoppers with 8 mm outlet slots feeding a fluted-roller metering base. Published research on dose-from-above systems for LPBF is limited compared to supply-piston designs.

**Vibrating hopper for cohesive powders.** Oropeza et al. developed a modular powder spreading testbed with an interchangeable vibrating hopper for dispensing fine, cohesive powders (oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 1-3, oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 3-5). The hopper used a turbine vibrator at pressures of 20–60 psi and hold times of 0.5–20 s to dispense <22 µm stainless steel powder. Dispensed mass increased with both duration and vibration pressure, and the dispensing rate was characterized empirically by linear fitting at each pressure condition (oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 8-10). The authors explicitly stated that dose calculations from these calibrations were only first-order estimates. The dispensing slot was wider than the build piston width to account for powder that falls outside the build area.

**Bridging and ratholing risks.** Fine aluminium powder (15–45 µm for LPBF) is cohesive due to van der Waals, capillary, and electrostatic forces. Powders below approximately 10 µm may be dominated by van der Waals forces (hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67). These interparticle forces can promote arching (bridging) across narrow outlets and ratholing within hoppers, particularly at low fill levels or with rough wall surfaces (hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67). Irregular particle shape increases surface area, friction, and mechanical interlocking. The literature recommends preventing stagnant zones by reducing material level, using internal mechanical agitators or external vibrators, enlarging the outlet, using steep smooth mass-flow walls, avoiding electrostatic construction materials, maintaining smooth internal surfaces, and controlling moisture and humidity (hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67).

**Segregation.** During spreading, heap segregation occurs as larger particles remain near the front while smaller particles migrate into voids (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 5-7). Reservoir segregation and unsuitable powder feed can cause inconsistent deposition, including incomplete coverage or increased size variation across the build plate (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 5-7). Triboelectric charging can further segregate particles by size, as smaller particles tend to charge oppositely to larger ones (alchikhsulaiman2020powderspreadingand pages 149-152).

**8 mm outlet slot.** No published study directly validates reliable gravity flow of 15–45 µm aluminium powder through an 8 mm slot without active agitation. The standard guidance for hoppers handling cohesive fine powders is that outlet dimensions should be many times the maximum stable arch span, which depends on powder cohesion, wall friction, and hopper half-angle. For the proposed design, vibration or agitation at the outlet, load-cell dose verification, and a short-feed detection interlock are strongly recommended.

## 4. Very Small Build Plates (25–75 mm Diameter)

### 4.1 Wall Effects on Packing Density

Container walls disturb particle packing by producing vacant regions near the boundary where spheres cannot occupy positions that would overlap the wall. This wall effect becomes stronger as the container height—or powder-layer thickness—decreases relative to particle size (jacob2016measurementofpowder pages 3-4). In LPBF, typical layer thicknesses (30–50 µm) are only 1–2 times the mean particle diameter, so wall-induced packing effects can be significant in thin powder layers near the cylinder edge (jacob2016measurementofpowder pages 3-4). For bulk random packing in cylinders, the classical rule of thumb is that wall effects extend approximately 4–5 particle diameters inward from the wall; for a 25 mm diameter plate with 30 µm mean particles, the D/d ratio is approximately 833, so the bulk wall-effect zone is a small fraction of the total area. However, in thin spread layers the wall-proximity zone can be proportionally more significant because the reduced dimensionality amplifies the geometric constraint.

### 4.2 Heat Accumulation

On small build plates, the total thermal mass is low and heat cannot dissipate laterally as effectively as on larger plates. For AlSi10Mg, platform preheating at 200 °C combined with laser energy input can produce significant in-process temperature rise, particularly for small parts requiring short print times (jatti2024investigatingtheeffect pages 3-4). The 25 mm plate with a 24 V cartridge heater in a 6061 block will reach steady state quickly, but temperature uniformity across the small plate must be verified; the Macor thermal break and aramid-felt insulation may create temperature gradients between the heated block and the piston head.

### 4.3 Gas-Flow Effects

On a 25 mm plate within a 280 mm wide chamber, the build area occupies less than 10% of the chamber cross-section. Cross-flow designed for the full 120 mm slot width will be largely unobstructed by the small plate, but the boundary layer and wake from the cylinder rim and surrounding powder surfaces may create local flow disturbances directly over the build area. The small plate dimension relative to the inlet nozzle width means that flow uniformity across the plate is likely better than on larger plates, but recirculation at the edges of the sunken cylinder bore can trap condensate or redirect spatter onto the build.

### 4.4 Usable Area

The usable area depends on the edge-exclusion zone, which typically accounts for the wall-packing disturbance, the recoater overshoot, and the gas-flow boundary effects. No published study provides a definitive usable-area fraction for 25–75 mm LPBF plates. Based on the packing-density wall effect (~150 µm inward exclusion for 30 µm particles) and practical engineering margins (1–2 mm for recoater alignment and seal geometry), the effective usable diameter is reduced by approximately 2–4 mm on each side. For a 25 mm plate, this means roughly 17–21 mm usable diameter (approximately 46–71% of plate area); for a 75 mm plate, approximately 67–71 mm (approximately 80–90% of plate area). These are engineering estimates informed by the literature rather than directly measured published values.

## 5. Highest-Priority Design Changes Before Build

Based on the evidence reviewed, the following changes are recommended in order of priority:

**1. Replace fused-silica main window or add athermalized protection.** Fused silica is the most thermally sensitive window material studied, producing approximately −22 to −30 mm focal shift when contaminated at 400 W (bonhoff2017experimentalandtheoretical pages 6-10). An N-FK51A window would reduce the modeled shift to approximately −1.3 mm (bonhoff2017experimentalandtheoretical pages 6-10). Alternatively, use sapphire for the structural window (as in the Griggs testbed) with a thin, cheap sacrificial slide swept by the gas knife (griggs2021atestbedfor pages 3-7). Include a transmitted-power sensor and focal verification procedure.

**2. Redesign gas flow for ≥2.5 m/s local velocity everywhere and suppress recirculation.** The proposed 2 m/s is at the critical lower threshold (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 5-6, reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 4-5). Add a secondary upper nozzle near the window to prevent recirculation (griggs2021atestbedfor pages 3-7). Align the inlet with the build surface to avoid backward-facing-step flow (wu2025improvingshieldinggas pages 2-4). Validate with CFD and in-chamber velocity measurement before first builds.

**3. Implement atmosphere quality monitoring.** Install a low-range O₂ sensor (target <0.1% for aluminium, ideally <100 ppm) and a dew-point sensor near the gas return. Interlock laser operation to validated limits. Use multiple vacuum-backfill cycles rather than a single evacuation to 1 mbar, and flow dry argon through the system during warm-up to desorb moisture from powder, insulation, and seals (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 38-41).

**4. Add vibration or agitation to the hopper outlet and dose verification.** The 8 mm outlet with cohesive fine Al powder is a bridging risk. Add a controllable vibrator, make the outlet adjustable, and install a load cell or optical short-feed sensor for dose verification (hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67, oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 8-10). Calibrate delivered mass empirically for each alloy, fill level, and humidity condition.

**5. Provide interchangeable compliant recoater blade option.** A rigid HSS blade maximizes collision risk during early process development. A compliant rubber or silicone blade accommodates minor overheight features without terminating the build (skacelik2026experimentalandcomputational pages 1-4, skacelik2026experimentalandcomputational pages 21-26). Start development at 50–100 mm/s rather than the proposed 150 mm/s maximum, and add an accelerometer or force sensor for automatic collision detection and abort (whiting2023powderspreadingtestbed pages 15-18).

**6. Address preheating limitations for crack-prone alloys.** If the machine is to screen 2xxx/6xxx/7xxx alloys, the 200 °C heater limit is inadequate. For 6182, crack-free parts required 500 °C (riener2022processabilityofhighstrength pages 1-2); for 7075, even 400 °C was insufficient to suppress cracking (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 1-2, velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 8-11). Either redesign the heated module for higher temperatures (requiring different insulation and seal materials) or restrict the machine's initial scope to AlSi10Mg and Al-Mg-Sc-Zr alloys.

**7. Harden powder-contact surfaces against cross-contamination.** When screening multiple alloy compositions, bare 6061 aluminium chamber surfaces and powder-contact parts will retain powder and condensate. Use removable electropolished stainless-steel liners, trays, and ducts for all powder-contact surfaces, with swab-based cleanliness verification between alloy campaigns.

**8. Qualify Z-axis positioning under realistic conditions.** Characterize the magnetic coupling, bayonet/drawbar plate retention, and piston-seal drag for bidirectional positioning accuracy, repeatability, lost motion, tilt, and settling at the operating temperature. The 30–50 µm layer thickness demands sub-5 µm repeatability, which must be verified with the full seal stack and thermal load, not just the bare stage (oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 1-3, oropeza2021amodulartestbed pages 3-5).

### Limitations of This Review

Several topics received limited coverage in the peer-reviewed literature: (a) quantitative piston-seal leakage and wear rates in research LPBF machines are essentially unreported; (b) direct dose-repeatability measurements for hopper-fed systems dispensing fine aluminium powder through narrow slots were not found; (c) usable-area fractions for 25–75 mm build plates are engineering estimates rather than published measurements; (d) chamber-material compatibility (bare 6061 vs. stainless steel for aluminium powder processing) is addressed by engineering judgment rather than controlled published studies. These gaps reflect the nascent state of open-architecture LPBF machine design literature and suggest areas where the proposed machine itself could contribute valuable data.
