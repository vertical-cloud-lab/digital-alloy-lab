| Protective window | Metal condensate, soot, and spatter deposit on the process window or sacrificial slide; contamination is spatially nonuniform and can become a local absorber. | For contaminated fused silica at **400 W**, about **0.5% additional absorptivity** produced a simulated **−21.6 mm focal shift**; experiments measured approximately **−22 to −30 mm**, while beam-waist radius increased from **35 to 53 µm**. Direct transmission loss was below an estimated **1%**, but focal shift and beam degradation were more consequential. Continued heating can damage or burn the window. Gas-knife or down-draft protection, cooling, sacrificial glass, and inspection after each build are reported controls. | Bonhoff et al.; Griggs et al.; Revanur (pqac-00000016, pqac-00000017, pqac-00000048, pqac-00000054) |
| Gas flow | Insufficient local velocity, inlet-to-outlet velocity decay, stagnation, reverse flow, and recirculation; recoater position and chamber geometry alter the flow field. Small chambers have little vertical or lateral margin, making window contamination by recirculated soot more likely. | In one LPBF study, porosity stayed below **0.1% at 2.5–4.0 m/s**, whereas **≤2.0 m/s** caused sharply increased porosity, shallower and unstable melt pools, balling, necking, and swelling. Commercial measurements ranged from about **4 m/s near the inlet to 1 m/s near the outlet**. A compact testbed used a secondary upper nozzle to suppress window-side recirculation and obtained **>1.4–1.5 m/s** over the full print area at **12–15 NL/min**. Excessive velocity can entrain loose powder. | Reijonen et al.; Shen et al.; Griggs et al.; Medinilla et al. (pqac-00000008, pqac-00000009, pqac-00000010, pqac-00000011, pqac-00000052, pqac-00000054) |
| Recoater | Rigid-blade contact with adhered spatter, consolidated protrusions, distorted thin walls, or upward-curled edges; particle jamming where clearance approaches the largest particle diameter; damaged or nicked edges generate repeatable streaks. Literature directly quantifying HSS wear rate is sparse. | Contact can lift and excite the blade, permanently deflect thin parts, detach layers, disturb powder across the blade width, jam the mechanism, or terminate the build. Collision experiments covered **20–200 mm/s** and **5–20 µm** layers. Rigid ceramic blades produced collision and rebound marks; compliant rubber blades imposed little mechanical stress even with the gap closed. Longer cooling or stabilization prevented collisions in one experiment. | Sehhat and Mahdianikhotbesara; Whiting et al.; Oropeza et al.; Skácelík et al. (pqac-00000023, pqac-00000026, pqac-00000058, pqac-00000059, pqac-00000060, pqac-00000062) |
| Powder spreading and dosing | Short feed, incomplete coverage, heap segregation, agglomeration, bridging or ratholing in fine cohesive powder, and layer-density variation. Smaller particles migrate into voids and may deposit earlier, while larger particles remain near the heap front. | Higher traverse speed generally lowers packing density and coverage and increases roughness, segregation, splash, accumulation, and bare regions. Reported blade speeds extend to **150 mm/s**, while roller translation spans **5–130 mm/s**; lower speed generally improves rearrangement. Fine powders below roughly **10 µm** may be dominated by van der Waals forces. A vibrating-hopper testbed dispensed **<22 µm** steel powder, but dose depended on vibration pressure and duration and required empirical calibration. | Oropeza et al.; Sehhat and Mahdianikhotbesara; Astarita; Hou (pqac-00000020, pqac-00000023, pqac-00000034, pqac-00000037, pqac-00000056, pqac-00000057, pqac-00000058) |
| Powder spreading—electrostatics | Triboelectric charging during powder–powder, powder–hopper, and powder–recoater contact; charge polarity and magnitude depend on oxide chemistry, counterface, flow rate, humidity, roughness, and particle size. Dry conditions increase persistent charge. | Electrostatic forces add to cohesion and friction; if comparable to or greater than gravity they impede flow, promote adhesion and agglomeration, and alter segregation and layer uniformity. AlSi10Mg acquired negative charge after stainless-steel contact, with reported values around **−2.80 to −5.70 nC/m²**, dependent on flow rate. Small particles can charge oppositely to large particles, enabling charge-assisted size segregation. | Alchikh-Sulaiman; Galindo et al.; Ramirez (pqac-00000027, pqac-00000028, pqac-00000029, pqac-00000030, pqac-00000031) |
| Build piston and heated plate | Powder enters annular gaps and migrates past scraper or wiper seals; thermal expansion changes radial clearance and seal preload. Powder embedded at the sliding interface raises friction, stick–slip, scoring, and wear. Seal drag loads the Z drive and can produce motion hysteresis. | Peer-reviewed LPBF literature provides little quantitative service-life or leakage data for piston seals; this is mainly a machine-design and tribology risk rather than a well-quantified process result. Because commanded layers are only **30–50 µm**, micron-scale stick–slip or plate rocking is significant. Published research testbeds commonly use precision guided stages; one modular system specified **5 µm** minimum vertical increments. | Oropeza et al. (pqac-00000056, pqac-00000057) |
| Atmosphere | Air leakage through doors, seals, feedthroughs, and low-pressure recirculation-loop regions; oxygen spikes during filter service; desorption of water from chamber surfaces and powder; humid powder introduces oxygen and hydrogen during melting. | Oxygen promotes oxide growth and changes fusion and mechanical properties. Moisture can yield oxygen and hydrogen; hydrogen causes gas porosity in molten aluminium and can embrittle high-strength Al alloys. One recirculating commercial system automatically repurged when oxygen exceeded **0.2%**, but that was a stainless-steel experiment and is not an aluminium best-practice target. Continuous low-range O₂ plus dew-point monitoring is required rather than relying on cylinder grade or chamber pressure alone. | Reijonen et al.; Revanur (pqac-00000002, pqac-00000004, pqac-00000011, pqac-00000015, pqac-00000065) |
| Optics | Absorption in contaminated transmissive optics and AR coatings generates refractive-index gradients and surface bulging; scan head, lens, mounting structure, and chamber-window temperature changes cause focal and geometric drift. | Thermal lensing changes focus, waist size, beam quality, and delivered irradiance even when transmitted-power loss is small. At **400 W** and **0.5% added absorption**, fused silica shifted focus by **−21.6 mm** and increased waist radius by about **30%**; N-FK51A shifted only about **−1.3 mm** under the modeled conditions. Temperature-controlled optics, purge or cooling, focus verification, witness coupons, and power measurement after all windows are needed. | Bonhoff et al.; Revanur (pqac-00000005, pqac-00000017, pqac-00000046, pqac-00000047, pqac-00000048) |
| Z axis and layer thickness | Backlash, compliance, seal drag, stick–slip, thermal expansion, platform rocking, magnetic-coupling slip, datum disturbance after recoater contact, and insufficient settling. Open-loop focus or Z control can add dynamic error. | Z error directly changes effective layer thickness and recoater clearance; accumulated bias changes part height and energy per unit powder volume. Recoater collision can alter the datum and produce inconsistent subsequent layers. Research platforms report **5 µm** minimum stage increments, but increment size is not equivalent to absolute accuracy or repeatability. Qualification should measure bidirectional positioning, loaded repeatability, lost motion, tilt, and settling at operating temperature. | Oropeza et al.; Revanur (pqac-00000000, pqac-00000001, pqac-00000051, pqac-00000056, pqac-00000057) |
