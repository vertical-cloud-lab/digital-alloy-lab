# Critique of the Beam Delivery System for a Lab-Built 500 W Aluminium LPBF Machine

## Overview

The proposed optical train—IPG YLR-500-SM → QBH collimator → SCANLAB basiCube 20 galvo → fused-silica f-theta lens (f = 160 or 254 mm) → AR-coated fused-silica chamber window → sacrificial cover slide → argon atmosphere—is architecturally conventional for a small LPBF platform. However, aluminium alloys with volatile elements (Mg, Zn) and high reflectivity introduce specific beam-delivery hazards that require careful engineering beyond catalogue component selection. The following critique addresses each of the seven topics with quantitative data where available.

## 1. Thermal Focal Shift of Fused-Silica Optics

Fused silica has a high temperature coefficient of optical path length (TCOP, G ≈ 9.03 × 10⁻⁶/K), and its relatively low thermal conductivity (≈1.31 W/m·K) produces steep radial temperature gradients under absorbed laser power, resulting in strong negative focal shift (laskin2021ontheselection pages 22-25, laskin2022ontheselection pages 6-8). Experimentally, a contaminated (fingerprint) fused-silica focusing lens showed a focal shift of −0.32 mm at 2.5 kW and −0.47 mm at 4.5 kW, in reasonable agreement with calculated values of −0.31 and −0.40 mm respectively (laskin2022ontheselection pages 9-12). At 500 W, the absolute shift scales approximately linearly with absorbed power, but the absorbed fraction depends critically on coating quality and surface cleanliness. Faas et al. measured an intensity-specific thermal refractive power D* of 0.55 × 10⁻⁶ mm/W for a new fused-silica lens with a high-quality wavelength-specific coating, versus 1.16–1.27 × 10⁻⁶ mm/W for used, uncoated fused-silica lenses (faas2018determinationofthe pages 5-7). Broadband coatings introduce substantially greater thermal power than the substrate alone (faas2018determinationofthe pages 3-5).

**Contamination effect.** Even a fingerprint or thin dust layer on a fused-silica window in a welding shop or SLM chamber creates a temperature gradient that shifts the focus and distorts the spot profile (laskin2021ontheselection pages 22-25). This is especially relevant for aluminium LPBF where Mg and Zn condensate continuously deposits on optics.

**Athermal alternatives.** Crystalline quartz (SiO₂, extraordinary orientation) has near-zero TCOP and thermo-optical ratio (TOR ≈ −0.04 × 10⁻⁶ W/m vs. 6.89 × 10⁻⁶ W/m for fused silica), fulfilling the self-compensation condition almost exactly. Sapphire achieves a low TOR (0.38–0.39 × 10⁻⁶ W/m) through extremely high thermal conductivity despite a high TCOP. The specialty glass aThermoXX (TCOP ≈ −0.31 × 10⁻⁶/K) offers near-zero thermal lensing and is used for SLM protective windows at power densities up to about 3 kW/cm² (laskin2021ontheselection pages 22-25, laskin2021ontheselection pages 25-26). Undoped YAG is another candidate for high-power windows, with good hardness and machining properties, though available sizes are typically limited to about 1 inch (laskin2021ontheselection pages 25-26).

**Detection and compensation.** LPBF builders should perform Z-scan burn tests (a staircase of single tracks at varying Z-offset) at cold start and after thermal soak to quantify the focal shift in situ. Trending delivered power and focal position before every build using an inline or removable focus monitor is essential. The relationship Δf/z_R ≈ −D* × P/(λ × M²) can be used for first-order estimation (faas2018determinationofthe pages 1-3).

## 2. F-Theta Lens Suitability at 500 W CW

A catalogue marking-grade f-theta lens (Thorlabs FTH series or similar) is typically specified with a pulsed laser-induced damage threshold (LIDT), which is not directly applicable to CW operation. At 500 W CW, the governing failure mode is not instantaneous optical damage but cumulative thermal degradation: coating absorption, thermal lensing, and thermally induced stress. The coating is the dominant thermal contributor—broadband coatings can produce roughly double the thermal refractive power of an uncoated substrate (faas2018determinationofthe pages 5-7, faas2018determinationofthe pages 3-5).

Fused-silica f-theta lenses have been successfully used in LPBF at 500 W with a 35 µm spot for copper processing, though this required careful attention to thermal effects (stoll2020processdevelopmentfor pages 9-13). Commercial LPBF machines (EOS, SLM Solutions, Renishaw) typically use industrial-grade fused-silica f-theta lenses from suppliers such as Sill Optics or Linos/Excelitas, which are specifically rated and warranty-qualified for CW multi-hundred-watt operation. These are generally non-telecentric designs for smaller machines, with telecentric lenses reserved for larger scan fields where constant spot size and normal incidence are critical.

**Recommendation.** Do not qualify a low-cost lens from its pulsed LIDT number alone. Require from the vendor: (a) total absorption specification at 1070 nm CW, (b) thermal focal shift data at the intended power and duty cycle, (c) clear aperture trace at all scan angles for the intended beam diameter, and (d) a warranty covering the CW operating conditions. If the vendor cannot provide this data, budget for an industrial LPBF-grade lens (Sill S4LFT or comparable).

## 3. Back-Reflection from Aluminium Melt Pools

Aluminium reflects approximately 90% of 1070 nm radiation from a polished bulk surface; copper can exceed 95% (naeem2013laserprocessingof pages 1-2, canacoo2026laseradditivemanufacturing pages 6-8). However, the fraction actually recoupled into the delivery fibre in LPBF is much smaller because: the melt pool is rough and displaced from the beam waist; the surface is not normal to the beam at most scan positions; and the collection solid angle of the return path through the f-theta and galvo is limited (rivera2018nextgenerationfiberlasers pages 3-5). Nevertheless, even a brief specular return during initial melting on a smooth build plate can deliver dangerous power spikes.

**Damage mechanisms.** Back-reflected light travelling through the delivery optics can overheat polymer fibre components, damage optical coatings on the galvo mirrors, and destabilise or damage the laser source (naeem2013laserprocessingof pages 3-3, canacoo2026laseradditivemanufacturing pages 6-8). Copper LPBF studies have documented optical mirror coating damage during high-power processing (stoll2020processdevelopmentfor pages 9-13).

**Protection strategies.** Several approaches are used in practice:

- **Laser internal protection:** Modern IPG YLR-series lasers include an internal back-reflection monitor and automatic power shutdown when reflected power exceeds a threshold. The YLR-500-SM likely tolerates some reflected power, but the exact specification should be obtained from IPG for the specific serial number configuration (naeem2013laserprocessingof pages 3-3).
- **Free-space isolators:** Faraday isolators provide high isolation (>30 dB) but introduce insertion loss, thermal load, and potential beam degradation; at 500 W single-mode they are expensive and require careful thermal management (naeem2013laserprocessingof pages 3-3).
- **Beam dump and monitoring:** JK Lasers/IPG developed systems that redirect back-reflected light into a beam dump and continuously monitor reflection levels, automatically shutting down the laser when limits are exceeded (naeem2013laserprocessingof pages 3-3).
- **Tilting the processing head/window:** Tilting diverts specular returns away from the fibre but produces an elliptical, larger spot with lower power density and potentially increased heat input (naeem2013laserprocessingof pages 3-3).
- **Operational protocols:** Avoid stationary exposure at the exact field centre, never fire at full power on a bare polished build plate, use a roughened or pre-coated plate surface, and begin scanning immediately upon laser turn-on (stanisz2026angulardependenceof pages 9-11).

## 4. Window and Cover-Slide Contamination

**Contamination sources.** Aluminium alloys containing Mg and Zn produce significant metal vapour during LPBF. This vapour condenses as fine nanoparticulate (condensate) that, together with ejected melt-pool spatter, deposits on the chamber window and sacrificial cover slide. The condensate attenuates the laser beam through both absorption and scattering, and creates local hot spots that can nucleate thermal damage on the optics (yakubov2022laserpowderbed pages 103-108, revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 36-38).

**Effect on delivered power.** Reducing shielding gas cross-flow from 4 m/s to 2 m/s in one study decreased melt-pool cross-sectional area by 30–50%, primarily attributed to laser attenuation by the uncleared vapour plume (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 5-6). Most vaporised condensate particles concentrate below approximately 1 mm above the keyhole, so attenuation is strongest near the interaction zone (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 5-6).

**Gas flow requirements.** A cross-flow velocity of approximately 2.5–4.0 m/s above the powder bed effectively clears the vapour plume and bends spatter trajectories downstream (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 4-5). Below 2.0 m/s, porosity increases exponentially and process stability degrades (reijonen2020ontheeffect pages 5-6). A modified two-row nozzle arrangement increased spatter removal efficiency from 69% to 93% (sing2020laserpowderbed pages 13-19). The proposed 2.5–3 m/s cross-flow is at the lower end of the effective range.

**Window protection design.** Patents for LPBF machines describe a dual gas-inlet architecture: a horizontal cross-flow across the bed for plume/spatter removal, plus a separate downward-flowing "gas knife" adjacent to the transmission element (window) that creates a curtain of clean gas preventing rising condensate from reaching the optics (EP3147047B1 pages 3-5, EP3321003B1 pages 4-5). Porous sintered-metal or fibre-web flow straighteners with 20–90% porosity and 1–10 mm pore sizes produce laminar flow and uniform velocity profiles (EP3321003B1 pages 4-5). Commercial practice is to clean the optical window after every build (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 51-53).

**Window tilting.** A slight wedge or tilt on the chamber window (1–2°) prevents ghost reflections from the window surfaces from propagating back along the beam path into the galvo and fibre. This is standard practice on many LPBF machines and highly recommended.

**Recommendation for volatile alloys.** For Mg/Zn-bearing aluminium alloys, budget for sacrificial cover slides changed every 1–3 builds (more frequent for high-Mg alloys), supplement the bed cross-flow with a dedicated window gas knife, and monitor transmitted power with a photodiode on the return path of a dichroic beamsplitter.

## 5. Galvo Aperture Clipping, Mirror Heating, and Edge Effects

**Beam-to-aperture margin.** The SCANLAB basiCube 20 has a 20 mm entrance aperture. With a 7.5 mm collimated beam (1/e² diameter), the beam-to-aperture ratio is 20/7.5 ≈ 2.67, which provides adequate margin against clipping on the entry mirror. With a 5 mm beam, this ratio increases to 4.0, which is safer for clipping but produces a larger diffraction-limited spot (scaling inversely with beam diameter on the lens). As a conservative design rule, the worst-case clear semi-aperture at any scan angle should be ≥1.5× the 1/e² beam radius.

**Mirror heating.** The basiCube mirrors are rated for approximately 1 kW at 1064 nm without active cooling. At 500 W CW with high-quality dielectric coatings (absorption <0.1%), thermal loading on each mirror is approximately 0.5 W, which is manageable without cooling for typical scan patterns. However, long dwell times on a single mirror position (e.g., during hatch scanning near one field edge) increase local heating and may introduce transient pointing drift.

**Angle of incidence and spot ellipticity.** For a 75 mm diameter scan field with f = 160 mm, the maximum half-field scan angle is arctan(37.5/160) ≈ 13.2°. With f = 254 mm, this reduces to arctan(37.5/254) ≈ 8.4°. The geometric spot elongation from oblique incidence on the powder bed is 1/cos θ, giving 1.027× at 13.2° and 1.011× at 8.4°—modest in themselves. However, combined scanner and lens aberrations produce measurable spot elongation at field edges (hernandez2023designandimplementation pages 74-79). The beam is approximately circular only at the build-plane centre and becomes increasingly elliptical with radial distance (hernandez2023designandimplementation pages 74-79, hernandez2023designandimplementation pages 79-84). This elongation causes position-dependent changes in effective power density and melt-pool depth: perpendicular elongation reduces penetration, while parallel elongation can partially compensate through increased interaction time (hernandez2023designandimplementation pages 79-84). Edge-of-field positions show increased porosity and surface roughness (hernandez2023designandimplementation pages 74-79).

**Recommendation.** Use f = 254 mm for a 75 mm field (8.4° max angle) rather than f = 160 mm (13.2°), accepting a larger nominal spot. Measure caustic, power, ellipticity, and centroid at centre, mid-axes, and corners after thermal stabilisation to build a field-correction map.

## 6. Commissioning Instruments and Melt-Pool Monitoring

**Beam characterisation.** NIST's open-architecture AMMT/TEMPS LPBF platform uses a 500 W fibre laser with a shared optical train supporting both high-power delivery and broadband measurement, including filtered detectors and a high-speed imager with FPGA-based real-time processing (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 2-4, grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 1-2). Commercial LPBF beam-characterisation tools reported in the literature include the Focus Beam Profiler (FBP), BeamWatch AM (BWAM), FocusMonitor+ (FM+), and 3-D Beam Mapper; the latter is used to map beam characteristics across the entire working envelope (hernandez2023designandimplementation pages 15-21).

**Minimum commissioning kit:**
- **Power meter:** Calibrated thermopile or water-cooled calorimeter rated ≥600 W (e.g., Ophir 500A-BB-34 or Coherent LM-500).
- **Beam profiler / focus finder:** A system capable of measuring ≈75 µm caustics (e.g., Primes FocusMonitor, Ophir BeamWatch, or Spiricon pyroelectric array with appropriate attenuation).
- **IR viewer / detector card:** 1070 nm sensitive cards or phosphor-coated viewers for alignment.
- **Visible alignment laser:** A co-aligned HeNe or red diode for coarse optical path verification.
- **Scan-field calibration:** Engraved or anodised stainless-steel calibration plates marked at known grid positions, plus a metrology microscope or optical CMM for affine and higher-order field correction.
- **Thermal camera:** For monitoring optic temperatures during power ramp.

**Melt-pool monitoring.** Coaxial monitoring in LPBF uses a dichroic beamsplitter inserted into the laser path before the galvo scanner head: the ≈1070 nm processing beam passes through, while shorter-wavelength thermal radiation from the melt pool is reflected to sensors (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 4-6, fox2024inprocessmonitoringand pages 44-47). Commercial implementations include the Renishaw AM250's dual-camera system using two Photron FASTCAM SA5 high-speed cameras with wavelength-specific bandpass filters for two-colour thermography, plus a Thorlabs PDA10C photodiode for laser power monitoring (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 4-6). Two-wavelength pyrometers using InGaAs photodiodes enable emissivity-independent temperature estimation (sun2019computationalfluiddynamics pages 53-56, lafirenza2025developmentofinsitu pages 35-37).

For a basiCube-class head, the simplest fit is a fast coaxial Si or InGaAs photodiode (sensitive to thermal emission in the 900–1700 nm range) fed via a dichroic beamsplitter and laser-line notch filter, providing a scalar melt-pool intensity signal correlated with temperature and pool size. An off-axis global-view camera (visible or SWIR) adds layer-by-layer imaging capability. Full two-band coaxial pyrometry requires calibrated optics and careful field-of-view registration.

## 7. Laser Safety Optics

**Viewing windows for a Class 1 enclosure.** For a 1070 nm fibre laser at 500 W, viewing windows in the enclosure must attenuate any conceivable direct or scattered beam to below the Class 1 accessible emission limit (AEL). An optical density of OD 7 at 1070 nm attenuates 500 W to 50 nW, which is well below the CW Class 1 AEL at this wavelength (stanisz2026angulardependenceof pages 9-11). The required OD must be calculated per IEC 60825-1 from the specific viewing geometry, wavelength band, and exposure duration. Certified laser-safety filter glass (e.g., Laservision, Kentek, or NOIR) rated for the 1000–1100 nm band with OD ≥7 is recommended. Polycarbonate with appropriate absorptive filter dyes is another option, but glass filter windows are more resistant to surface damage and degradation.

**Guard and enclosure burn-through.** IEC 60825-4 (and ANSI Z136.1) require that protective housings withstand the maximum foreseeable laser exposure for a specified guard time. Burn-through time for 1.6 mm aluminium sheet exposed to a 500 W CW beam at 1070 nm is not a fixed number—it depends strongly on spot size (focused vs. defocused), beam intensity profile, alloy thermal conductivity, surface reflectivity, and cooling (ozgoren2011highpowerallfiber pages 65-70). Aluminium's high reflectivity at 1 µm (>90%) initially reflects most incident power, dramatically slowing penetration compared to steel; once absorption increases at elevated temperature, thermal conductivity rapidly conducts heat away from the spot (ozgoren2011highpowerallfiber pages 65-70). For a defocused beam (as would typically strike a guard panel), the irradiance may be well below the absorption-coupled melting threshold, potentially preventing penetration entirely.

**Practical guidance:** Prefer steel guards (1.5–3 mm mild steel) over aluminium for the beam enclosure, as steel has lower reflectivity and conductivity at 1 µm, providing more predictable guard behaviour. Use double-walled construction with an air gap. Install beam traps at foreseeable direct-beam impingement points. Interlock all doors, removable panels, window shutters, and monitor loss of coolant/flow and scanner faults. Commission the entire enclosure through a formal laser safety assessment per IEC 60825-1/IEC 60825-4, including a hazard analysis and, ideally, an empirical guard penetration test at maximum power with the worst-case focused beam geometry. Do not rely solely on a calculated burn-through time (ozgoren2011highpowerallfiber pages 65-70).

## Summary of Key Concerns

The proposed beam path is feasible but demands attention to several aluminium-specific risks: (1) thermal focal shift in fused-silica optics can exceed the Rayleigh range even at 500 W if surfaces become contaminated with Mg/Zn condensate—crystalline quartz or aThermoXX alternatives should be considered at minimum for the chamber window (laskin2021ontheselection pages 22-25, laskin2021ontheselection pages 25-26); (2) catalogue marking-grade f-theta lenses lack CW thermal qualification; (3) back-reflection from aluminium mandates verified laser protection and operational protocols (naeem2013laserprocessingof pages 3-3); (4) volatile-element condensate requires a dedicated window gas knife in addition to the bed cross-flow (EP3321003B1 pages 4-5, EP3147047B1 pages 3-5); (5) a 7.5 mm beam with f = 254 mm is preferred over f = 160 mm for edge-spot quality over a 75 mm field (hernandez2023designandimplementation pages 74-79, hernandez2023designandimplementation pages 79-84); (6) a minimum set of beam-characterisation and monitoring tools must be budgeted from the outset (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 2-4, hernandez2023designandimplementation pages 15-21); and (7) laser-safety design should use steel guards, OD ≥7 viewing filters, and a formal IEC 60825 hazard analysis rather than relying on aluminium burn-through estimates.
