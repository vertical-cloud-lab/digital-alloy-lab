**Structure-borne acoustic emission (AE)** is the most accessible laboratory method. Seleznev et al. coupled a broadband high-temperature D9215 piezoelectric sensor to the aluminium baseplate beneath LPBF-built Al₉₂Mn₆Ce₂ specimens using high-temperature grease, with 40 dB preamplification, a 50–600 kHz band-pass filter, and 2 MHz / 18-bit sampling (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 2-3). Crack-related waveforms were triangular bursts of 1–3 ms duration with spectra peaking near 100 kHz and extending to approximately 500 kHz (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 3-5). Validation against Zeiss Xradia Versa 510 micro-CT at 2.85 µm voxel size showed all four CT-identified crack locations in one build were associated with AE activity, but a triple-crack site produced only one detected event, possibly because the cracks occurred simultaneously or fell below the 90–100 dB threshold (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 3-5, seleznev2022insitudetection pages 5-7). Crucially, a substantial proportion of crack signals occurred during the first five minutes after build initiation and also during post-build cooling, with crack formation potentially delayed by up to an hour after the responsible layer was built (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 7-7). No formal detection rate or false-alarm rate was reported, and the inert-gas circulation pump was a significant noise source requiring waveform-based discrimination (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 5-7).

**Regarding AE through a heated plate with thermal breaks:** The Seleznev study coupled the sensor directly to the aluminium baseplate through high-temperature grease, with the sensor described as "high-temperature" but with no stated operating temperature limit (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 2-3). For the described machine's ceramic thermal break and bolted/foil interfaces, one should expect significant ultrasonic attenuation and mode conversion at each interface. Practical approaches include: (a) coupling a waveguide rod directly to the build plate below the thermal break, bringing the signal to a sensor at a cooler location; (b) using a high-temperature sensor rated to ≥200–300 °C (the MISTRAS D9215 is rated to approximately 500 °C Curie temperature); and (c) establishing a pencil-lead-break transfer function for every plate/gasket/bolt configuration to verify that crack-frequency signals propagate with detectable amplitude.

**Operando synchrotron X-ray radiography** synchronized with AE on CM247LC confirmed that micro-cracking occurs during solidification, primarily at high-angle grain boundaries near the terminal solidification stage, and that both Hf and nano-Y₂O₃ additions mitigate cracking through distinct mechanisms (fardan2026unveilingcrackmitigation pages 1-2). This approach provides definitive physical validation but requires beamline access and thin-specimen geometry unrepresentative of bulk builds.

**Inline coherent imaging (ICI)** detected surface crack openings as small as 7 µm in humped CM247LC DED tracks, with an off-axis head at 24° and <10 µm lateral registration accuracy. ICI surface-topology measurements correlated >0.93 with synchrotron radiography (fleming2023insitucorrelative pages 1-2, fleming2023insitucorrelative pages 2-3). Although demonstrated in DED rather than LPBF, ICI can in principle be integrated coaxially in LPBF systems.

**Airborne acoustic sensing** has captured 12–16 kHz audible signals from microcracking in SiC-reinforced 316L DED composites (nobari2026mechanismssensorsand pages 9-11), but this evidence is from DED thermal-stress cracking rather than rigorously proven LPBF solidification cracking.

**Thermal sensing** does not detect the crack itself but monitors the causal thermal environment—gradients, cooling rates, and heat accumulation—and can serve as a cracking-susceptibility covariate (lafirenza2025developmentofinsitu pages 35-37, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 4-6).

**Crack timing relative to the melt pool:** Solidification cracking occurs near the terminal stage of solidification when tensile stresses separate solute-rich interdendritic liquid (fardan2026unveilingcrackmitigation pages 1-2). In the AE data, some cracks formed during the build (within seconds of lasing the responsible layer) while others were delayed by minutes to an hour during post-build cooling (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 7-7). The melt-pool tail in Ti6Al4V extends approximately 400–1000 µm behind the laser and solidifies in 200–600+ µs (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 15-19), suggesting that solidification cracks open within millimetres of the melt pool. Surface visibility depends on crack opening; ICI resolved 7 µm openings (fleming2023insitucorrelative pages 1-2), but typical LPBF hot cracks in aluminium alloys may remain smaller and subsurface.

## 2. Coaxial Melt-Pool Sensing Through a Galvo Scanner

### Photodiodes vs. two-colour pyrometers vs. cameras

The planned single-channel silicon photodiode (520–985 nm, ≥100 kHz) is a proven baseline. Photodiodes offer fast response, high sensitivity and straightforward integration, with demonstrated sampling rates of 10–100+ kHz (sun2019computationalfluiddynamics pages 53-56, grasso2017processdefectsand pages 18-20). However, a single broad-band channel conflates temperature, melt-pool area, emissivity and plume effects into one scalar signal.

**Two-colour (ratio) pyrometry** adds a second spectral channel and uses the intensity ratio to reduce emissivity dependence. A system by Hooper (2018) used two Photron FASTCAM SA5 cameras with ~700 nm and ~950 nm bandpass filters at up to 200,000 fps and 128×128 pixels on a Renishaw AM250, measuring Ti6Al4V melt-pool temperatures from approximately 1000 to 4000 K with cooling rates of 1–40 K/µs and thermal gradients of 5–20 K/µm (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 22-24, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 1-4). Calibration used a stabilised tungsten-halogen source (Thorlabs SLS201L) illuminating an integrating sphere inside the build chamber, with radiance verified by a calibrated spectrometer; validation against a type-C thermocouple showed agreement within 1% while the laser was on and 5% at ~1900 K after shutoff (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 9-12, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 6-9). A simpler two-InGaAs-photodiode pyrometer has been demonstrated at 1260 nm centre wavelength, 100 nm bandwidth, 1200–2900 K range, and 560 µm spot (grasso2017processdefectsand pages 18-20).

**For aluminium alloys**, two-colour pyrometry faces serious challenges. Aluminium has very low emissivity (~0.1–0.2 for liquid) that changes rapidly with oxide state, temperature and phase. A dual-pyrometer study on AlSi10Mg at 200 kHz detected melting modes but produced counterintuitive lower temperatures over shallow overhangs because the signal collapses melt-pool size, shape and temperature-profile information (sloane2025investigatingthevalue pages 118-122, sloane2025investigatingthevaluea pages 118-122). The low emissivity means weak thermal emission in the NIR, compounding signal-to-noise challenges.

### Effects of 1064 nm galvo mirror coatings and f-theta chromatic aberration

The basiCube 20 mirrors are coated for 1064 nm. Kozjek et al. (2026) note that galvanometer mirror coatings optimised for the working laser wavelength can strongly suppress other bands: a spectrometer test found that wavelengths in the 750–875 nm range were "almost completely blocked" by one scanner's mirror coating (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 12-13). Therefore, **before purchasing any coaxial sensor, the end-to-end spectral transmission through the actual basiCube mirrors and f-theta lens must be measured**—ideally with a broadband source and fibre spectrometer at multiple scan positions.

The f-theta lens is designed for 1064 nm; at other wavelengths, chromatic aberration produces wavelength-dependent focal shift and lateral image offset. Hooper noted that imaging at ~700 and ~950 nm through a 1064-nm-optimised scan head required independent camera-position adjustment for the two channels, and that galvanometer-dependent image offsets had to be mapped and corrected as a function of build-plate position and wavelength (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 6-9, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 4-6). The focal distance also varies with scan position in the build plane (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 10-12). A pre-focusing unit can partially compensate (grasso2017processdefectsand pages 18-20), but coaxial camera imaging through a basiCube without a camera port will likely be limited to low-resolution intensity collection rather than sharp spatial imaging of the melt pool.

**Practical recommendation for this machine:** Since the basiCube 20 has no camera port and the mirrors may severely attenuate visible/NIR, the most robust coaxial upgrade is a second photodiode channel at a wavelength that transmits adequately, creating a custom two-channel ratio monitor. Measure the actual mirror transmission first, then select two narrow bands that both pass, avoid 1070 nm, and fall within InGaAs or Si detector response (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 12-13, kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 10-12).

## 3. Off-Axis High-Speed Imaging of Melt Pool and Spatter

Research LPBF testbeds have demonstrated a wide range of off-axis high-speed imaging configurations:

- **Ultrahigh-speed (1 Mfps):** Sakai et al. used a Shimadzu HPV-X2 at 1,000,000 fps with 200 ns shutter, 400×250 pixels, and a 20× Mitutoyo M-PLAN NIR infinity-corrected objective. Illumination was a Cavitar CAVILUX HF at 640 nm with a 60 ns pulse and four-direction beam splitting; a 645±10 nm bandpass filter was used. The illumination source was 23 cm from the sample (sakai2025insitucharacterizationof pages 4-5, sakai2025insitucharacterizationof pages 2-4, sakai2025insitucharacterizationof pages 1-2).

- **LLNL testbed:** Calta et al. used an 810 nm pulsed laser (500 W peak, Cavitar) for coaxial illumination and high-speed X-ray + optical imaging (sun2019computationalfluiddynamics pages 95-99).

- **Off-axis thermal/visible cameras at moderate speed:** Thanki used a Mikrotron Eosens 3CL at 20,000 fps, 120×120 pixels, 100 µm/pixel, 30 µs exposure, 25° from vertical, with a 975 nm short-pass filter (thanki2024correlationbetweeninprocess pages 132-138). Mitchell et al. used two cameras at 6500 fps, 65×80 pixels, 21 µm/pixel, with 750 nm and 900 nm filters (sloane2025investigatingthevalue pages 109-114, sloane2025investigatingthevaluea pages 109-114). Lough et al. used off-axis IR at 2500 fps with a 1450 nm filter and 130 µm/pixel (sloane2025investigatingthevalue pages 109-114).

- **DED illuminated imaging:** An 808 nm illumination laser with 10 µs pulse and 810±10 nm bandpass filter was used at 100 fps/150 µs exposure, with the camera 320 mm from the nozzle at 30° (sun2019computationalfluiddynamics pages 95-99).

**Window protection from condensate:** The 2-inch fused-silica window with a replaceable cover slide is essential. Metal condensate from aluminium alloys is aggressive; the cover slide should be replaced frequently. A gas curtain or laminar-flow screen across the viewing window helps. Steep viewing angles reduce condensate deposition.

**Cheapest useful setup:** An entry-level high-speed camera (Kron Chronos 2.1-HD at ~$5,000–7,000, capable of ~20 kfps at reduced resolution) paired with a used 808 or 850 nm CW diode illumination module (~$300–1,000) and a matched 10 nm bandpass filter (~$200), plus a long-working-distance zoom lens (Navitar 12× or similar, ~$1,500–3,000), can resolve melt-pool shape and gross spatter dynamics at 10–20 µm/pixel over a few-millimetre field. This would cost approximately $7,000–12,000 total. A Cavitar CAVILUX HF system (~$30,000–50,000+) is superior for pulsed illumination and freeze-frame quality but exceeds the stated budget.

## 4. Powder Spreading and Layer Quality

### Image resolution and lighting for defect detection

Scime et al. demonstrated that side-lighting and low camera angles produce high contrast, with 60–99% anomaly detection depending on defect type and machine; pre-recoat images target spatter and porosity, post-recoat images target swelling, incomplete spreading and recoater streaking (sloane2025investigatingthevalue pages 104-109, sloane2025investigatingthevaluea pages 104-109). Lu et al. used a Nikon DSLR with white LED illumination at 10–13 µm/pixel, achieving Pearson correlation up to 0.78 between pixel intensity and XCT-detected pores (sloane2025investigatingthevalue pages 104-109). An inline powder scanner at ~5 µm resolution detected powder unevenness, ultra-high edges and grooves (hou2022onlinemonitoringtechnology pages 3-5).

The planned 12 MP camera at ~26 µm/pixel is adequate for streaks, short feed, raised edges and spatter deposits. It will not resolve individual 30–60 µm particles reliably. Adding two independently strobed raking-light directions and one diffuse top light, plus HDR/flat-field calibration, will maximise contrast.

### In-situ height mapping

**Fringe projection profilometry (FPP)** has been integrated into LPBF chambers. Zhang et al. (2016) achieved approximately 6.8 µm lateral sampling and 0.47 µm single-point height repeatability on a 28×15 mm field, resolving 28 µm peak-to-valley recoater waviness and detecting fused material protruding above the powder level (zhang2016insitusurface pages 8-11). Practical in-machine vertical accuracy is more commonly ~10 µm; IN-718 builds measured part edges to within 100 µm laterally and surfaces to within 10 µm vertically (sloane2025investigatingthevalue pages 104-109). Multi-phase-shift FPP requires several images per measurement, limiting real-time capability but feasible for inter-layer inspection (suresh2025recentadvancesinin pages 22-23).

**Inline coherent imaging (ICI)** provides 7 µm axial resolution, 25–35 µm lateral resolution, and measurement rates from 50 kHz to 312 kHz (fleming2020trackingandcontrolling pages 10-13, fleming2023synchrotronvalidationof pages 2-3). Single-point height repeatability is 0.30 µm over 100 seconds (fleming2020trackingandcontrolling pages 10-13). ICI scans coaxially through the processing optics and has been used to detect raised regions, depressions, recoater-blade damage (striations in powder layers) and to perform closed-loop correction of surface roughness with a 54% reduction in maximum roughness (fleming2020trackingandcontrolling pages 6-10). However, ICI integration requires coupling into the laser beam path, which is complex with the basiCube 20.

**Low-coherence scanning interferometry (LCSI)** at LLNL produced powder-bed height maps at 100 µm lateral spacing and 25 µm vertical resolution, sampling at 100 kHz (sloane2025investigatingthevalue pages 104-109).

**Recoater-mounted laser-line profilometry** (e.g., Keyence LJ-V7060) provides micron-level surface mapping over a 15 mm swath, detecting gouges, ridges and super-elevated features, though it did not report numerical accuracy (barrett2019highspeedstereovision pages 48-52).

**Imaging the spreading process itself** at high speed inside a chamber has not been convincingly demonstrated in the reviewed literature. Eschner et al. tracked individual powder particles with a two-camera stereoscopic system (suresh2025recentadvancesinin pages 19-20), but this assessed particle motion rather than the powder heap ahead of the blade.

## 5. Thermal Imaging in a Small Chamber

### Camera types and spectral ranges

Three spectral ranges are relevant, with distinct trade-offs:

- **NIR (Si detector, ~400–1000 nm):** Silicon sensors are limited to temperatures above ~700 K (chechik2022processmonitoringand pages 46-49, chechik2022processmonitoringanda pages 46-49). They offer high resolution and frame rates (up to 200,000 fps for scientific cameras) and use standard fused-silica or B270 glass windows, making them compatible with the existing chamber window. However, they cannot see the cooling track below red heat.

- **SWIR (InGaAs, ~0.9–1.7 µm):** More sensitive to cooler material, allowing observation of the cooling track tail and heat accumulation patterns. SWIR cameras are more compact and affordable than MWIR and are recommended as the practical choice for integration into small chambers (altenburg2020insitumonitoringof pages 16-18). The existing fused-silica window transmits adequately to ~2 µm. Frame rates of 500+ fps with cropped ROI are typical. The melt pool may saturate; ND filters are needed (chechik2022processmonitoringanda pages 46-49).

- **MWIR (3–5 µm):** Best sensitivity to near-ambient and moderate temperatures and can capture full-layer thermal maps. However, fused silica is opaque at MWIR wavelengths—a sapphire, CaF₂ or ZnSe window would be required, adding cost and complexity. MWIR cameras are also more expensive. Altenburg et al. concluded that SWIR and MWIR provide more useful temporal and spatial temperature information than VIS cameras (altenburg2020insitumonitoringof pages 16-18).

### Aluminium emissivity challenges

Emissivity of aluminium varies strongly with oxide state, temperature, wavelength, phase (powder vs. liquid vs. solid), and surface condition (chechik2022processmonitoringand pages 46-49, chechik2022processmonitoringanda pages 46-49). Liquid aluminium has very low emissivity (~0.1–0.2), producing weak thermal emission relative to steel or titanium at the same temperature. The result is that any single-wavelength thermal measurement is essentially a radiance measurement, not a temperature measurement, unless emissivity is independently known or controlled. Two-colour pyrometry reduces but does not eliminate this problem, particularly when the emissivity ratio varies with temperature (non-grey behaviour) (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 22-24). For aluminium LPBF, thermography should be treated as a tool for relative comparisons (track-to-track, layer-to-layer, region-to-region) and thermal trending, not for absolute temperature measurement.

### What thermography reveals

Thermal imaging has shown heat accumulation in overhangs and thin walls (slower cooling, longer melt-pool tails), geometry-dependent cooling rates, and thermal-gradient variation with scan strategy (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 4-6, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 22-24, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 15-19). It can identify locations likely susceptible to cracking by virtue of anomalous thermal history, but no reviewed study directly imaged crack formation thermally. In Ti6Al4V, Hooper measured cooling rates of 1–40 K/µs and thermal gradients of 5–20 K/µm, with overhang scans showing greatly reduced cooling and prolonged solidification (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 19-22, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 22-24).

## 6. Data and Synchronisation

### Signals to record

The NIST AMMT architecture provides a reference model. It records: commanded XY position and power (XYPT format, 10 µs between points), DAQ-measured galvanometer X/Y encoder feedback, laser modulation (gate), analog laser power (sampled at 25 kHz), coaxial melt-pool camera images with hardware triggers, layerwise images under multiple LED positions, oxygen sensor, and thermocouple readings (yang2025afullyregistered pages 8-13, hocker2023powderbedfusion pages 11-25, hocker2023powderbedfusion pages 1-11, yang2025afullyregistered pages 5-8). For the described machine, the full signal list should include: scan-controller XY commands and laser gate from the SCANLAB RTC interface, actual galvo encoder positions, commanded and measured laser power, coaxial photodiode(s), acoustic emission (raw waveform and/or parameterised hits), off-axis cameras (layer and high-speed), plate thermocouples, chamber O₂, dew point, pressure, and gas-flow proxy (anemometer or differential pressure).

### Synchronisation

A 500 µs digital synchronisation pulse triggered sensors in the NASA/NIST testbed, with camera exposure timing combined with the trigger to determine scan position (hocker2023powderbedfusion pages 11-25). Exact temporal synchronisation between galvo-scanner commands and monitoring measurements is essential for high-resolution spatially resolved data; the SCANLAB RTC controller can output a position-synchronised trigger signal. At 100 kHz photodiode sampling and 1.1 m/s scan speed, samples are separated by ~11 µm along the scan path—commensurate with the melt-pool scale. Use hardware timestamps rather than PC arrival times.

### Data rates and storage

Kozjek et al. estimated that a typical PBF-LB/M build using one photodiode plus laser and galvo position data at 100 kHz generates tens of gigabytes (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 13-13). Adding raw AE waveforms at 2 MS/s and high-speed camera frames will increase this substantially; a small build might produce 50–200 GB, while extensive instrumentation on larger builds can exceed 1 TB.

### Standards and misleading data

The NIST AMMT datasets are publicly available from data.nist.gov and represent a de facto reference format using registered CSV files with per-layer, per-part structure (yang2025afullyregistered pages 8-13, yang2025afullyregistered pages 1-5). ASTM F3490 (AM data package) and ISO/ASTM 52942 (qualification) address data requirements at a higher level but do not prescribe signal-level schemas. Documented cases of misleading data include: (a) coaxial pyrometry on AlSi10Mg overhangs producing counterintuitive lower temperatures for hotter, narrower, deeper melt pools because the signal conflates geometry and temperature (sloane2025investigatingthevalue pages 118-122, sloane2025investigatingthevaluea pages 118-122); (b) emissivity-related errors in absolute temperature measurements (chechik2022processmonitoringand pages 46-49, lafirenza2025developmentofinsitu pages 35-37); and (c) coaxial-only monitoring missing process signatures outside the melt pool (lafirenza2025developmentofinsitu pages 35-37).

### Rationale for the ranking

**Acoustic emission ranks first** because it is the only affordable sensor that has been demonstrated to detect crack initiation in-situ during LPBF of a crack-prone aluminium alloy, including delayed cracking during post-build cooling—information that no other sensor provides at any price (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 2-3, seleznev2022insitudetection pages 7-7). It is also inexpensive and minimally invasive.

**The second coaxial channel ranks second** because it upgrades the already-planned photodiode from an intensity monitor (sensitive to every confound) to a ratio measurement with reduced emissivity dependence, at modest cost (sloane2025investigatingthevalue pages 118-122, sloane2025investigatingthevaluea pages 118-122). However, the basiCube mirror coatings may limit wavelength choices and require careful spectral characterisation before purchase (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 12-13).

**Off-axis high-speed imaging ranks third** because it provides direct visual evidence of melt-pool shape, spatter, plume, denudation and potentially surface cracks—information essential for understanding aluminium LPBF phenomenology and correlating with the other scalar sensors (sakai2025insitucharacterizationof pages 4-5, thanki2024correlationbetweeninprocess pages 132-138). An entry-level system is achievable within the budget.

**The DAQ/synchronisation backbone ranks fourth** because without it, the other sensors produce interesting but unregistered data that cannot be correlated spatially or temporally with specific tracks, layers and defects (hocker2023powderbedfusion pages 11-25, kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 13-13, yang2025afullyregistered pages 8-13).

A complete first-tier instrumentation package (AE system + second photodiode channel + entry-level high-speed camera + DAQ backbone + airborne microphone + layer camera lighting upgrades) can plausibly fit within $15,000–25,000, leaving headroom for a SWIR camera or fringe projection as a second-tier addition.

**Within a $20,000–40,000 total budget**, items 1–5 and 8 (the incremental layer-camera upgrade) can be achieved, with either item 6 (SWIR camera) or item 7 (fringe projection) depending on whether thermal mapping or height mapping is prioritised. For crack-prone aluminium alloy screening specifically, the SWIR camera is arguably more informative than fringe projection because it reveals cooling-rate and heat-accumulation anomalies that drive cracking susceptibility.
