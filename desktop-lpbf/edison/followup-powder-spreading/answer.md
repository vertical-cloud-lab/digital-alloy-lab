**Young's modulus and scaling.** The real Young's modulus of AlSi10Mg is approximately 75 GPa (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10). Girnth et al. (2024) systematically evaluated Young's modulus reduction from 100% to 0.01% of the real value (75 × 10⁹ down to 75 × 10⁵ Pa) and found that the angle of repose remained stable within approximately 5% error down to about E_sim/E_real = 10⁻³ (i.e., ~75 MPa) for AlSi10Mg, below which numerical artifacts emerged (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11). Ye et al. (2026) similarly reduced E by three orders of magnitude (~75 MPa) for computational efficiency (ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 6-7). For stainless steel powders, scaled moduli of 33–828 MPa have been reported to maintain maximum particle overlaps of 0.1–0.5% of the largest particle radius (haapa2023simulationofmetal pages 33-36). The time step must be validated concurrently, as it scales with √(m/k) and cohesive force magnitudes can depend on T_sim (ajabshir2024effectoftemperaturea pages 121-126).

**Poisson ratio.** Values of 0.30–0.33 are consistently used for aluminium alloys (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10). This parameter is relatively insensitive in low-stress spreading contexts but enters the effective stiffness calculation.

**Coefficient of restitution.** Girnth et al. measured 0.54 ± 0.006 for an aluminium sphere dropped onto an AlSi10Mg substrate at 100 mm drop height (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 7-9). Ye et al. used 0.64 for AlSi10Mg (ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10). Literature for broader metal powders spans approximately 0.2–0.8, with DEM spreading studies commonly adopting 0.2–0.5 (haapa2023simulationofmetal pages 36-38, haapa2023simulationofmetal pages 33-36).

**Sliding friction.** Girnth et al. measured AlSi10Mg–AlSi10Mg sliding friction as 0.20 ± 0.018 using powder-coated inclined-plane tests on substrates with Rz = 3.4 µm, while AlSi10Mg on rougher substrates (Rz = 17.4 µm) yielded 0.45 ± 0.15 (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 7-9). Ye et al. measured 0.67 for AlSi10Mg–AlSi10Mg using a similar inclined-plane method (ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10). The large spread illustrates that effective friction depends strongly on surface preparation and roughness. For IN718, parametric ranges of 0–0.9 have been explored (marchais2021a3ddem pages 4-6).

**Rolling friction.** Girnth et al. explored 0.02–0.12 for AlSi10Mg, with this parameter serving as a proxy for nonspherical particle shape, satellite particles, and surface roughness that mono-spheres cannot resolve (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11). Ye et al. calibrated 0.09 for AlSi10Mg (ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10).

**JKR surface energy density.** Girnth et al. used 0.5–3.5 mJ/m² for AlSi10Mg (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11). Shaheen et al. explored 0, 0.1, and 0.4 mJ/m² for a generic metal powder (shaheen2020theinfluenceof pages 17-22). It is critical to confirm whether the software expects single-surface energy γ or work of adhesion Γ = 2γ, as the LIGGGHTS simplified JKR model uses the cohesion energy density parameter differently from the full JKR formulation (lupo2021calibrationofdem pages 5-7).

**Calibration methods.** The most common calibration approach is matching the static angle of repose between DEM and experiment (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 5-7, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 6-7, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10). Girnth et al. additionally used bulk density as a second target and measured sliding friction and restitution independently (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 7-9). Ye et al. validated against in-situ dynamic repose angle with <2% relative error (ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10). Other calibration targets reported in the literature include Hall flow time, rotating drum/Revolution powder analyser dynamic repose angle and cohesive index, shear cell unconfined yield strength, and Anton Paar powder cell torque (haapa2023simulationofmetal pages 36-38, ajabshir2024effectoftemperaturea pages 121-126, lupo2021calibrationofdem pages 2-3). Lupo et al. (2021) demonstrated that a bulk calibration approach using angle of repose alone with Hertz–Mindlin JKR could not simultaneously satisfy both angle of repose and unconfined yield strength, recommending direct measurement of interparticle surface energy when possible (lupo2021calibrationofdem pages 2-3, lupo2021calibrationofdem pages 5-7). Using at least two independent observables—ideally including one shear-dominated test resembling blade spreading—is recommended (haapa2023simulationofmetal pages 36-38).

### 2. Spreading Study Results

**Packing fraction vs. gap/D90.** Salehi et al. (2023) reported that continuous powder layers require a blade gap of approximately 3–4 × D90 for cohesive powders and about 2 × D90 for free-flowing powders (salehi2023optimisingspreadlayerquality pages 2-3). Increasing gap thickness from 0.01 to 0.03 mm raised packing fraction from roughly 15% to 33% (salehi2023optimisingspreadlayerquality pages 2-3). Burgess (2024) reviewed SS316L spreading at gap settings of 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 × D90 (45–135 µm for D90 = 45 µm); smaller gaps produced more voids and porosity, with quality deteriorating significantly below about 1.5 × D90 (burgess2024discreteelementmethod pages 88-94). Wang (2021) tested Hastelloy X (D90 = 52.7 µm) at gaps of 70 and 90 µm (~1.3–1.7 × D90) (wang2021particlescalemodelling pages 121-125). For AlSi10Mg with D90 ~ 60 µm, a 30–50 µm layer thickness gives gap/D90 ratios of only 0.5–0.8, which places the process in the regime where jamming and coverage degradation are significant concerns.

**Blade speed effects.** Packing fraction generally decreases with increasing spreading speed. One DEM simulation found a decline from 47% to 25% as speed rose from 10 to 100 mm/s (salehi2023optimisingspreadlayerquality pages 2-3). Brocksieper et al. (2026) found that AlSi10Mg powder layers showed notably higher surface roughness at 250 mm/s compared to 50 and 150 mm/s, particularly with blade and hopper recoaters; roller recoaters were less speed-sensitive for 316L and IN718 but still showed increased roughness for AlSi10Mg at the highest speed (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 7-9). The literature converges on the finding that lower spreading speeds (typically 20–50 mm/s) produce more uniform, denser powder beds with fewer voids and lower surface roughness (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 46-48, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 51-54, burgess2024discreteelementmethod pages 88-94).

**Blade type comparisons.** Roller spreaders generally produce denser, smoother layers than rigid blades due to their larger contact area and gradual compaction effect (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 46-48, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 51-54, salehi2023optimisingspreadlayerquality pages 2-3). Brocksieper et al. confirmed that the roller recoater consistently produced the lowest arithmetic mean roughness Sa across all three tested powders, while the hopper recoater produced the highest Sa with characteristic grooves (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 7-9). Among blade geometries, wide-nose or flat-bottomed blades outperformed sharp-edged blades, as the rounded leading edge applies downward force and the flat trailing surface promotes particle settling (salehi2023optimisingspreadlayerquality pages 12-13). Steel blades produced better coverage than silicone or ceramic blades (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 48-51).

**Critical gap and jamming.** Below approximately 1–1.5 × D90, particle jamming, mechanical arching, streaks, and empty patches become frequent (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 46-48, burgess2024discreteelementmethod pages 88-94, burgess2024discreteelementmethod pages 85-88). A critical blade height exists below which mass flow is zero because particles jam or their diameters exceed the gap (burgess2024discreteelementmethod pages 85-88). For AlSi10Mg with D90 ~ 60 µm, spreading at 30 µm nominal layer thickness (gap/D90 ~ 0.5) is well into the jamming-prone regime.

**Effective layer thickness after melt shrinkage.** Lu et al. (2026) developed a mathematical model showing that the steady-state effective powder stacking height approaches H_s = H/δ, where H is the nominal layer thickness (platform drop) and δ is the effective packing/densification ratio (~0.6 for the studied powder), yielding H_s ≈ 1.667H (lu2026exploringtheactual pages 8-12, lu2026exploringtheactual pages 12-16, lu2026exploringtheactual pages 16-19). For a nominal 30 µm layer, the steady-state powder bed height stabilizes at approximately 50 µm after about 25 layers (lu2026exploringtheactual pages 12-16). The total volumetric shrinkage was reported as approximately 40%, comprising 31.5% from liquid-metal infiltration into powder-bed voids and 8.5% from solidification contraction (lu2026exploringtheactual pages 16-19, lu2026exploringtheactual pages 6-8). Elton et al. (2023) similarly found that melting randomly packed powder (~50–60% solid fraction) produces a consolidated layer of approximately 50–60% of the nominal powder-layer thickness (elton2023electrostaticpowderspreading pages 14-19). The relationship is layer_effective = packing_density × nominal_powder_height; the steady-state consolidated thickness equals the nominal platform drop H once the powder bed reaches equilibrium (bogdanova2024mesoscalesimulationof pages 10-13).

**Granular Bond number.** Spierings (2018) defined the cohesive granular Bond number as BN = F_interparticle/F_gravity, noting that increasing fine-particle content raises BN and reduces flowability (spierings2018powderspreadabilityand pages 115-119). However, explicit numerical Bond number values for 20–63 µm AlSi10Mg were not found in the retrieved literature. For the particle sizes of interest (D50 ~ 35–40 µm, density ~2700 kg/m³), van der Waals adhesion forces become comparable to gravitational forces, placing these powders in the transitional regime (BN ~ 0.1–1) between free-flowing and cohesive behavior.

**Hausner ratio.** Gas-atomised AlSi10Mg showed a Hausner ratio of 1.06, with apparent density 1.40 g/cm³ and tapped density 1.50 g/cm³ (kraner2020characterizationofnonferrous pages 2-3). This value indicates "good" to "excellent" flowability on the Carr classification scale. However, Hausner ratio alone may not predict spreadability, as Spierings noted poor correlation between Hausner ratio and observed powder-layer quality in several cases (spierings2018powderspreadabilityand pages 115-119).

**Angle of repose and avalanche angle.** Values for AlSi10Mg vary dramatically with test geometry: Kraner measured 45° using a standard ASTM method (kraner2020characterizationofnonferrous pages 2-3), while Girnth et al. measured 60.73° ± 4.62° using a miniaturized setup with a 500 µm orifice and 5 mm table (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 7-9). For AlSi7Mg powders, static angles ranged from 26° to 39° depending on PSD and morphology (munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 8-11). Untreated fine aluminium powder gave a 60° angle of repose, reduced to 24–32° by silane surface treatment (chen2010characterizationofparticle pages 6-8). These large variations underscore the importance of matching test geometry to the DEM calibration setup.

**Humidity, oxide, and drying effects.** Aluminium powders are particularly sensitive to environmental conditions due to their native Al₂O₃ oxide layer, which increases surface roughness and interparticle friction (rezaei2026powderdegradationand pages 24-25). Fine aluminium particles (<20 µm) are most susceptible to moisture uptake, which promotes adhesion, agglomeration, poor spreading, and non-uniform layers (rezaei2026powderdegradationand pages 24-25). The fine AlSi7Mg powder (D50 = 31 µm) with higher surface area showed substantially greater moisture/vapor sorption than coarser fractions, increasing surface energy heterogeneity and work of cohesion (munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 8-11). Surface modification—particularly silane treatment—reduced surface free energy from 123 mJ/m² to 19.9–30 mJ/m² for fine aluminium, with corresponding reductions in angle of repose from 60° to 24–32° (chen2010characterizationofparticle pages 6-8). Drying at ~200°C under vacuum for 2 hours is a standard conditioning step for AM aluminium powders; sieving out fines, inert-atmosphere handling, and dry storage are also recommended to maintain flowability (rezaei2026powderdegradationand pages 24-25, munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 5-8).

### 4. Dose Factor Recommendations and Hopper Flow

**Dose factor and effective layer thickness.** The dose factor (ratio of powder volume dispensed to the nominal layer volume on the build area) must compensate for powder-bed porosity and spreading losses. The Lu et al. (2026) model predicts that the steady-state effective powder-bed height is H/δ ≈ 1.67H for δ = 0.6 (lu2026exploringtheactual pages 8-12, lu2026exploringtheactual pages 12-16). This implies that a minimum dose factor of approximately 1/δ ≈ 1.67 is required to avoid "short feed" (insufficient powder reaching the far end of the build plate). In practice, dose factors of 1.5–2.0 as planned are consistent with this requirement, providing a margin for spreading losses, edge effects, and powder-pile depletion across the build width. When the dose is insufficient ("short feed"), the powder pile is exhausted before the blade traverses the full build area, resulting in bare or thin regions on the far side of the build plate, reduced packing density, and voids that lead to lack-of-fusion defects.

**Hopper flow of fine Al powder.** Fine aluminium powders (20–63 µm) present significant hopper flow challenges. Jenike analysis uses cohesive strength from a flow-function test and wall friction to calculate the minimum outlet size for arching prevention and the hopper angle for mass flow (maynard2013tenstepsto pages 2-4, maynard2013tenstepsto pages 4-5). For mass flow, the hopper walls must be sufficiently steep with low wall friction; a commonly cited 60° conical hopper angle from horizontal is generally insufficient to guarantee mass flow (maynard2013tenstepsto pages 2-4). Mass flow requires that the hopper slope be steep enough that material flows along the walls whenever solids are withdrawn, preventing stagnant zones that lead to ratholing (maynard2013tenstepsto pages 2-4). For funnel-flow self-cleaning, the hopper slope should be 15–20° steeper than the wall-friction angle (maynard2013tenstepsto pages 4-5).

**Bridging and ratholing in narrow outlets.** For a slot outlet of approximately 8 mm width, fine cohesive aluminium powder is at significant risk of arching/bridging, as the critical arching dimension for cohesive powders in this size range can exceed the outlet width. The minimum outlet width depends on the material's unconfined yield strength at the consolidation stress in the hopper, requiring direct shear cell measurement. For fine metal powders, permeability-based analysis is essential because standard coarse-powder discharge equations do not account for resistance to airflow through the fine powder bed (maynard2013tenstepsto pages 4-5). Stainless-steel hoppers provide relatively low wall friction angles (typically 15–25° for polished stainless against metal powders), which is favorable for mass flow, but the wall angle must still be verified against measured wall friction for the specific aluminium powder (maynard2013tenstepsto pages 2-4).

### 5. Powder Migration into Gaps and Seals

Published literature on powder migration into radial clearances around build plates and pistons in LPBF machines is limited, as this is primarily proprietary machine design knowledge. The build platform typically moves within a close-fitting build sleeve, and the dosing piston moves within a dosing sleeve (EP3363566B1 pages 5-6). For fine metal powders with particles as small as 20 µm, radial clearances of 0.1–0.4 mm represent gaps that are several particle diameters wide, allowing significant powder migration under the combined effects of gravity, vibration from the recoater, and gas flow.

**Seal types.** Deridov (2024) described the use of acrylic felt as a piston powder seal for a prototype SLS machine where elevated temperatures were not a concern, and specified wool felt for a functional high-temperature machine to prevent burning (deridov2024analysisofpowder pages 31-36). The patent by McMurtry (EP3363566B1) describes build platform and dosing piston arrangements within sleeves but does not detail specific sealing elements in the retrieved sections (EP3363566B1 pages 5-6). In practice, commercial LPBF machines employ various sealing strategies including felt strip seals (compressed between the piston periphery and the sleeve), brush seals (bristle rows that conform to the gap while allowing piston motion), labyrinth grooves machined into the piston or sleeve that trap powder in successive channels, and close-tolerance machined fits that rely on the powder's own bridging behavior in narrow gaps (<0.1 mm). O-rings and elastomeric seals are less common due to thermal constraints, powder abrasion, and the need for very low friction during precise vertical positioning.

**Design considerations.** The minimum clearance is constrained by thermal expansion (the build plate and sleeve heat differentially during processing), mechanical tolerances, and the need to avoid binding. With aluminium alloy builds, the lower processing temperatures compared to steel or nickel alloys reduce differential thermal expansion, potentially allowing tighter clearances. However, the light density and fine particle size of Al powders make them particularly prone to migration compared to denser materials. Pressurized inert gas flow in the build chamber can either aid or hinder powder migration depending on flow direction relative to the gap. Periodic cleaning and maintenance protocols are essential, as accumulated powder in the piston mechanism can cause binding, positional errors, and contamination of subsequent builds.

### Summary of Recommended DEM Input Parameters for LIGGGHTS Simulations

For the user's planned LIGGGHTS simulations of AlSi10Mg spreading with Hertz–Mindlin and simplified JKR cohesion, the following starting parameters are suggested based on the literature synthesis:

- **Young's modulus:** Scale to ~75 MPa (10⁻³ × E_real), verifying that maximum overlaps remain <0.5% of particle radius (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 6-7)
- **Poisson ratio:** 0.33 (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11)
- **Density:** 2670–2680 kg/m³ (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10)
- **Coefficient of restitution:** 0.54–0.64 (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 7-9, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10)
- **Sliding friction:** 0.2–0.67 (measured values vary with surface condition; start with ~0.3–0.4 and calibrate) (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 7-9, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10)
- **Rolling friction:** 0.05–0.12 (calibrate against angle of repose and/or bulk density) (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10)
- **JKR cohesion energy density:** 0.5–3.5 mJ/m² (start with ~0.8–1.5 mJ/m² and calibrate against angle of repose and spreading tests) (girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 9-11, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10)
- **Calibration targets:** Static angle of repose plus at least one additional test (bulk density, Hall flow, shear cell, or in-situ dynamic repose angle) (haapa2023simulationofmetal pages 36-38, girnth2024dimensionlessquantitiesin pages 5-7, ye2026digitaltwindrivenmanufacturing pages 7-10, lupo2021calibrationofdem pages 2-3)
