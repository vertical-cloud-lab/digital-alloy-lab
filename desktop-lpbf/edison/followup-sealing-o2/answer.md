## Atmosphere Control and Seal Design for a Vacuum-Purged LPBF Chamber Processing Aluminium Alloys: A Literature Review

### 1. Residual O₂ and H₂O Levels, Purging Strategies, and Published Machine Data

**Vacuum purging versus flow purging.** Two principal methods exist for establishing the inert atmosphere in an LPBF build chamber. Flow (displacement) purging uses elevated inert-gas flow to dilute and displace the initial air until the residual oxygen falls below a machine-defined threshold; it is the most commonly adopted commercial approach (pauzon2019theprocessatmosphere pages 15-16). Vacuum purging evacuates most of the air before back-filling with inert gas, optionally repeating the cycle; it has not been broadly adopted by equipment manufacturers despite its potential for faster and more gas-efficient atmosphere preparation (pauzon2021tailoredprocessgases pages 26-29, pauzon2021tailoredprocessgases pages 23-26). For an ideal well-mixed chamber evacuated to 1 mbar and backfilled with Ar 5.0 (≤10 ppm total impurities), a single cycle reduces atmospheric O₂ from approximately 210,000 ppm to roughly 210 ppm; two cycles bring it below approximately 0.2 ppm from the residual air contribution alone, with leaks, outgassing, and gas-supply impurities dominating thereafter.

**Published machine data.** Approximately 1,000 ppm O₂ (0.1%) is the typical process-initiation threshold for most commercial LPBF systems (pauzon2021tailoredprocessgases pages 26-29, pauzon2021controlofresidual pages 1-3). The EOS M290, a widely studied platform, uses approximately 40 L/min of inert gas during chamber purging for about 20 minutes (approximately 800 L total), followed by a holding flow of a few L/min during printing to counteract leaks (pauzon2021tailoredprocessgases pages 23-26). The TRUMPF TruPrint1000 was operated in one study with an Argon 5.0 supply and a target of 100 ppm O₂, which the external ADDvance O₂ precision electrochemical system achieved and maintained stably (pauzon2021controlofresidual pages 3-6). The Renishaw AM250, a system that includes evacuation capability, was studied with both evacuation-plus-argon-purge and purge-only preparation; the study primarily monitored chamber relative humidity rather than reporting a specific O₂ endpoint (das2019contributionofcellulosic pages 5-6, das2019contributionofcellulosic pages 2-3). The NIST Additive Manufacturing Metrology Testbed (AMMT) uses a medium-vacuum chamber capable of reaching approximately 0.1 Pa (10⁻³ torr), enabling vacuum purging prior to backfilling with nitrogen or argon, which the designers note "provides faster means to reduce oxygen levels compared to simply purging with inert gas" (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 3-6). The AMMT optics tower is mechanically decoupled from the vacuum chamber to avoid deflection-induced alignment errors (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 6-8).

**Leak rates and rate-of-rise.** Quantitative leak-rate specifications (mbar·L/s) for commercial LPBF machines are not routinely published in the open literature. The Karlsson study on a pressurized build chamber enclosure noted that the maximum allowable leak rate was defined by the available inert gas supply—283 L/min in that case—illustrating that large-format machine enclosures are typically designed for positive-pressure containment rather than vacuum tightness (karlssonUnknownyearfrommachineenclosure pages 24-29). For a 6–10 L chamber with O-ring face seals and KF-25 flanges designed for evacuation to 1 mbar, well-assembled FKM seals on clean, properly finished flanges should readily achieve integral leak rates below 10⁻⁴ mbar·L/s, which is orders of magnitude tighter than what is achievable with panel-based enclosures. A rate-of-rise test—isolating the pumped-down chamber and monitoring pressure increase over time—is the standard acceptance method; the resulting rate directly gives the total leak-plus-outgassing load in mbar·L/s.

### 2. O₂ and Moisture Thresholds for Processing Aluminium Alloys

**Oxygen thresholds.** Aluminium alloys are particularly susceptible to oxidation due to the thermodynamic stability of Al₂O₃. Many LPBF studies processing Al alloys (AlSi10Mg, 2xxx, Al-Mg-Sc-Zr) report maintaining chamber O₂ below 100 ppm (pauzon2021controlofresidual pages 3-6). Baroutaji et al. (2023) systematically compared AlSi10Mg fabricated at 100 ppm and 1,000 ppm O₂ on an EOS M290 and found relatively modest macroscopic effects: elongation improved by approximately 5.7% at lower oxygen, while ultimate tensile strength decreased by approximately 3% and yield stress by approximately 1% (baroutaji2023theinfluenceof pages 11-12, baroutaji2023theinfluenceof pages 1-2). For TPMS lattice structures, compressive property variations were generally below 2–8% depending on geometry (baroutaji2023theinfluenceof pages 8-11). The authors characterized their findings as preliminary and recommended investigating a broader range including 20–2,000 ppm (baroutaji2023theinfluenceof pages 11-12).

**Oxide inclusions and fatigue.** Tang (2017) demonstrated that in LPBF AlSi10Mg, oxide-driven pores were the dominant defect controlling fatigue resistance (tang2017inclusionsporosityand pages 6-8, tang2017inclusionsporosityand pages 1-6). Larger oxide particles associated with crack initiation were believed to form through oxidation of metal vapor during manufacture. While oxide inclusions were observed to be smaller than the dominant pores, they were found on fracture surfaces at fatigue-failure initiation sites. Lack-of-fusion pores with sharp edges created the strongest stress concentrations and were the most harmful to fatigue life (tang2017inclusionsporosityand pages 46-50, tang2017inclusionsporosityand pages 42-46).

**Hydrogen porosity.** The mechanism linking moisture to hydrogen porosity is well established: ambient moisture reacts with liquid aluminium at melt-pool temperatures to form Al₂O₃ and atomic hydrogen, which dissolves readily in the melt but has dramatically lower solubility in the solid, leading to pore nucleation during solidification (kramer2026investigationofthe pages 2-4). Van Cauwenbergh (2019) reported that LPBF AlSi7Mg0.6 printed at approximately 3 ppm O₂ with process gas containing ≤5 ppm H₂O produced parts with 13 ± 4 ppm hydrogen versus 45 ± 11 ppm in the starting powder, indicating significant hydrogen loss during the vacuum pre-cycle and printing (cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenporesb pages 4-6). Elevated oxygen and moisture in the atmosphere promote oxidation and balling: reducing oxygen from 2% to 0.1% was shown to prevent balling in 316L through reduced melt-pool oxidation and improved wettability (pauzon2019theprocessatmosphere pages 22-25).

**Spatter.** Higher oxygen concentrations contribute to oxide-film stabilization on the melt pool, which can alter surface-tension-driven flow, increase spatter generation, and change spatter morphology. The recirculation of spatter-contaminated powder, which carries higher oxygen, can progressively degrade powder quality and increase porosity in subsequent layers (pauzon2019theprocessatmosphere pages 22-25).

### 3. Seal Design for Vacuum Plus Low Positive Pressure

**O-ring permeation: FKM versus other elastomers.** Sturm et al. (2004) measured permeation coefficients for polymer O-rings used in gas-sampling flasks. For Viton (FKM), the O₂ permeation coefficient was approximately 1.0–1.1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m²·s⁻¹·hPa⁻¹, while NBR ranged from 0.7 to 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m²·s⁻¹·hPa⁻¹ in the same units (sturm2004permeationofatmospheric pages 3-4). Silicone was substantially more permeable, with values in the range of 76–460 × 10⁻¹⁵ m²·s⁻¹·hPa⁻¹. For water vapor, FKM had a permeation coefficient of approximately 220 × 10⁻¹⁵ m²·s⁻¹·hPa⁻¹, which is much higher than its O₂ permeation, confirming that water-vapor permeation through elastomeric seals is a significant contributor to chamber moisture even when O₂ leakage is well controlled (sturm2004permeationofatmospheric pages 4-6). The authors noted that polymer formulation and manufacturer differences can produce order-of-magnitude variation in permeation coefficients (sturm2004permeationofatmospheric pages 3-4).

**Implications for a 2.3 m seal length.** For 2.3 m of FKM O-ring seal (e.g., 3.53 mm cross-section compressed to approximately 2.5 mm exposed width), a crude estimate of the O₂ permeation flux at atmospheric ΔpO₂ of approximately 210 hPa across a compressed thickness of approximately 2 mm gives an O₂ ingress rate on the order of 10⁻⁴ mbar·L/s, which would add roughly 0.5–1 ppm/hour to a 10 L chamber at 1013 mbar—generally acceptable. Water vapor, with a permeation coefficient approximately 200× higher, would dominate the seal-permeation contribution to chamber humidity.

**O-ring gland design.** For vacuum-plus-low-positive-pressure service, standard AS568/ISO 3601 gland dimensions with 15–25% squeeze and gland fill of 70–85% are appropriate. The surface finish for vacuum-grade O-ring grooves is typically Ra ≤ 0.4 µm on the sealing surfaces. KF-25 flanges with centering rings and FKM O-rings are well-suited for this pressure regime.

**Outgassing.** FKM has lower outgassing rates than NBR, EPDM, or silicone, which is an important consideration for vacuum chambers. Aluminium walls outgas primarily water vapor; baking at modest temperatures (100–150°C) dramatically reduces outgassing rates, but the 200°C build plate in an LPBF chamber will warm nearby surfaces and can drive desorption of adsorbed water from internal chamber walls, increasing the moisture load (pauzon2019theprocessatmosphere pages 22-25, das2020moisturesourcesin pages 25-66).

**Dynamic seals for the build piston and recoater.** The NIST AMMT uses a "spring/felt seal assembly" around the build plate, which is noted in descriptions of the build bed, powder feed, and recoater alignment system, although specific seal-geometry details are not provided (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 6-8). Felt and brush seals provide powder containment while accommodating the sliding motion of the build piston; they do not provide high-integrity gas sealing and are typically complemented by an O-ring or PTFE lip seal on the piston rod for atmosphere containment. Edge-welded metal bellows can provide zero-leak dynamic sealing for limited strokes but are expensive and must be protected from powder ingress. Magnetic-fluid (ferrofluidic) feedthroughs are well-established for rotary shaft sealing in vacuum applications and provide leak rates below 10⁻⁹ mbar·L/s, making them attractive for a recoater shaft feedthrough where rotary motion must pass through the chamber wall without compromising atmosphere quality. Standard PTFE lip seals on a rotary shaft offer leak rates in the 10⁻³ to 10⁻² mbar·L/s range, which may be adequate for a positive-pressure chamber but would dominate the leak budget of a vacuum-purged system.

**Key finding from LPBF-specific comparison.** Pauzon et al. (2021) directly compared a zirconia-based lambda probe (the standard sensor in the TRUMPF TruPrint1000) with an electrochemical cell (ADDvance O₂ precision) during LPBF processing of Ti-6Al-4V. When the lambda probe recorded a 50 ppm O₂ increase, the electrochemical cell simultaneously measured a 700 ppm increase—a 14:1 discrepancy attributed to the lambda probe's cross-sensitivity to hydrogen and other reducing species (H₂, CO) that oxidize on the hot (approximately 700°C) sensor head, consuming oxygen and making the reading appear artificially low (pauzon2021controlofresidual pages 8-12). The electrochemical cell does not exhibit this cross-sensitivity. Over seven hours of continuous monitoring, the electrochemical sensor measured an oxygen drift of approximately 1.7 ppm O₂/min, providing a direct measure of the total leak-plus-outgassing-plus-permeation rate of the system (pauzon2021controlofresidual pages 8-12).

**Response time.** Electrochemical and semiconductor metal oxide sensors exhibit response times ranging from approximately 3–4 seconds to 60–70 seconds depending on electrolyte composition, electrode design, temperature, and sampling geometry (bulowski2024thinfilmsemiconductor pages 7-8). Heated planar zirconia sensors can reach operating temperature in under 10 seconds, but installed sampling-line dead volume typically dominates system response (halley2024mixedpotentialsensor pages 26-30).

**Poisoning.** Zirconia sensors are susceptible to poisoning by sulfur oxides at concentrations as low as a few ppm, and by lead and phosphorus compounds that can coat electrode surfaces (bulowski2024thinfilmsemiconductor pages 7-8). In an LPBF environment, metal condensate, soot, and silicone-derived contamination (from greases, sealants, or silicone-based thermal interface materials) represent analogous poisoning risks; locating the sensor in a filtered sample bypass is essential (bulowski2024thinfilmsemiconductor pages 7-8). Electrochemical cells are sensitive to membrane fouling and electrolyte ageing and require periodic replacement.

**Dew-point sensors.** Chamber humidity in LPBF has been monitored using capacitive probe hygrometers measuring relative humidity, as demonstrated on the Renishaw AM250 (das2019contributionofcellulosic pages 2-3). Chilled-mirror dew-point sensors provide a primary measurement of water content and are suitable for monitoring the LPBF chamber gas, though their use in LPBF has not been widely reported in the literature.

### 5. Moisture Carried by Aluminium Alloy Powders, Drying Practice, and Effects

**Powder moisture content.** Lubkowitz et al. (2024) measured new AlSi10Mg powder at 0.015 wt% (approximately 150 ppm by mass) moisture using Karl Fischer titration; this condition yielded the highest achievable component density of 2.65 ± 0.002 g/cm³ (lubkowitz2024comparisonofinprocess pages 5-6). Storage in unsealed containers or exposure to humid air increased moisture and reduced achievable density. Van Cauwenbergh (2019) reported that AlSi7Mg0.6 powder contained 45 ± 11 ppm hydrogen, while printed parts contained 13 ± 4 ppm, with the reduction attributed partly to moisture extraction during the vacuum pre-cycle and to low-moisture process gas (H₂O ≤ 5 ppm) (cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenporesb pages 4-6). Cordova et al. (2017) found that aluminium powders (Scalmalloy and AlSi10Mg) were the most moisture-sensitive among the alloys tested (also including Ti-6Al-4V, IN718, 316L), with AlSi10Mg showing the strongest visual and compositional response to humid aging at 50°C, 80% RH for 72 hours (cordova2017assessmentofmoisture pages 1-3, cordova2017assessmentofmoisture pages 3-5). Schulze et al. (2025) reported hydrogen contents ranging from 143 to 908 ppm in AlSi10Mg powder depending on condition (new, briefly aged, aged, or aged plus dried) (lubkowitz2024comparisonofinprocess pages 5-6).

**Drying practice.** Cordova et al. (2017) used vacuum drying at 85°C for 6 hours and air drying at 150°C for 20 minutes (cordova2017assessmentofmoisture pages 1-3). Lubkowitz et al. (2024) compared furnace drying at 80–200°C, vacuum drying (various pressure/temperature/time combinations), and in-process laser drying. Furnace drying had the weakest moisture-removal effect and can promote oxide layer growth, while vacuum-dried powder produced higher sample density than oven-dried powder, likely because its oxide layer was thinner (lubkowitz2024comparisonofinprocess pages 1-2, lubkowitz2024comparisonofinprocess pages 4-5). In-process laser drying was most effective, reducing hydrogen pore fraction from 28% in undried powder to 9% at a laser energy density of 3.9 J/mm³ and to 3% at 9.6 J/mm³, though excessive energy caused particle sintering and lack-of-fusion defects (lubkowitz2024comparisonofinprocess pages 4-5). None of the methods fully restored aged powder to the density performance of new powder, leading the authors to recommend an inert, closed powder-handling cycle (lubkowitz2024comparisonofinprocess pages 4-5). Pre-drying at 200°C for 16 hours has also been reported to reduce pore enlargement during post-build heat treatment (lubkowitz2024comparisonofinprocess pages 5-6).

**Cellulosic filter as a moisture source.** Das et al. (2019) identified cellulosic HEPA-type filters used in LPBF recirculation systems as a significant unrecognized moisture source. A Hengst E1900 L filter stored at ambient humidity could retain up to 51.28 g of moisture (at 80% RH), which was subsequently released into the build chamber during inert-gas recirculation (das2019contributionofcellulosic pages 3-5, das2019contributionofcellulosic pages 6-7). Greater moisture desorption occurred during inert-gas purging than during evacuation, because the flowing gas enhanced desorption from the filter media (das2019contributionofcellulosic pages 5-6). The recommended countermeasure is to dry the filter at 90°C for 5 hours before installation, which removed all measured moisture, or to store filters in a low-RH environment (das2019contributionofcellulosic pages 6-7). This finding is directly relevant to the described chamber design, which uses a recirculating blower and HEPA filter: the filter can release grams of water into the chamber and significantly elevate the dew point unless it is properly conditioned.

**Effect on chamber dew point and hydrogen porosity.** The moisture released from powder, chamber walls, and especially from cellulosic filters establishes the chamber dew point, which is typically not monitored in commercial LPBF systems (das2020moisturesourcesina pages 66-116, das2019contributionofcellulosic pages 1-2). At melt-pool temperatures, moisture dissociates to provide both oxygen (forming Al₂O₃) and hydrogen (dissolving into liquid aluminium). The sharp drop in hydrogen solubility during solidification nucleates hydrogen pores, which can grow further during post-build heat treatment, particularly at temperatures above 500°C where hydrogen diffusion becomes rapid (kramer2026investigationofthe pages 2-4). Controlling the chamber dew point through vacuum purging, dry gas supply, pre-dried filters, and dried powder is therefore essential for minimizing hydrogen porosity in LPBF aluminium alloys.

### Summary of Practical Implications for the Described 6–10 L Chamber

For a bare 6061-T6 aluminium chamber of 6–10 L with FKM O-ring face seals (2.3 m total), KF-25 flanges, and vacuum capability to approximately 1 mbar, the following design considerations emerge from this review:

1. **Vacuum purging** to 1 mbar followed by backfill with Ar 5.0 provides a theoretical single-cycle dilution of approximately 1000:1, reducing atmospheric O₂ from 210,000 ppm to approximately 210 ppm; a second cycle reaches sub-ppm residual air. This is significantly more efficient than flow purging, which requires approximately 800 L (or approximately 100 chamber volumes for a 10 L chamber) on an EOS M290 (pauzon2021tailoredprocessgases pages 23-26). The NIST AMMT adopted vacuum capability for this reason (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 3-6).

2. **FKM O-rings** provide the lowest gas permeation among common elastomers (O₂ permeation approximately 100× lower than silicone), but water-vapor permeation through FKM remains significant at approximately 220 × 10⁻¹⁵ m²·s⁻¹·hPa⁻¹ (sturm2004permeationofatmospheric pages 3-4, sturm2004permeationofatmospheric pages 4-6). An aluminium chamber will outgas primarily water vapor, and the 200°C build plate will accelerate wall desorption.

3. **Oxygen monitoring** should use an electrochemical cell rather than relying solely on a zirconia lambda probe, which can underestimate O₂ by an order of magnitude in the presence of hydrogen from moisture decomposition (pauzon2021controlofresidual pages 8-12). The measured drift rate of approximately 1.7 ppm O₂/min from the electrochemical sensor provides a direct measure of total atmospheric ingress.

4. **Powder and filter conditioning** are critical: new AlSi10Mg powder carries approximately 150 ppm moisture (lubkowitz2024comparisonofinprocess pages 5-6), and cellulosic filters can hold tens of grams of water (das2019contributionofcellulosic pages 6-7). Pre-drying the powder and filter before each build is essential for minimizing hydrogen porosity.

5. **Target atmosphere quality** for aluminium alloy processing should aim for ≤100 ppm O₂ and dew point below −40°C (approximately 120 ppm H₂O), based on the thresholds used in research studies achieving high-density, low-porosity parts (pauzon2021controlofresidual pages 3-6, cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenporesb pages 4-6).
