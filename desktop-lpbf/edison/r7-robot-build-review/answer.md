# Critical Review: Two Mobile Manipulators Building a Desktop LPBF Machine in a University Shop

## 1. Published Examples and Industrial Practice of Mobile Manipulators for CNC Machine Tending and Assembly

### 1.1 Maturity of Mobile-Manipulator Machine Tending

Autonomous mobile manipulators (AMMs) combining an AMR base with a cobot arm are recognized as a scalable solution for high-mix, low-volume CNC tending, but the published evidence base remains largely at the prototype or conceptual level (feiyu2024anintelligentframework pages 43-47, khishtan2026areviewof pages 2-4). A systematic review of robotic machine-tending literature found that AMM-based loading and unloading using 3D point-cloud object detection, marker recognition, and CNN-based material handling are classified as prototypes, with no fully autonomous system yet replacing a human for tasks such as cycle start, emergency handling, or screen-based machine interaction (feiyu2024anintelligentframework pages 43-47). One cited example uses a KUKA AMR with a gripper and vision on the end-effector to pick raw material from local storage and transport it to a machine for loading, but this remains a demonstration rather than a production deployment (feiyu2024anintelligentframework pages 43-47).

### 1.2 Achieved Positioning Accuracy After Docking and Re-Registration

Positioning accuracy is the principal bottleneck for mobile manipulation. SLAM-only AMR navigation produces positioning errors of approximately 50–100 mm (hifi2025flexibleandintelligent pages 74-80). Adding fiducial markers (ARTags) together with a wrist-mounted vision system reduces errors to 10 mm or less, representing an improvement of more than 80% (hifi2025flexibleandintelligent pages 74-80). Vision-based compensation in one study achieved an average offset of 0.19 mm (hifi2025flexibleandintelligent pages 74-80). In a robotic-chemist application where a touch-sensitive six-point station calibration was used, positioning accuracy improved to ±0.12 mm with ±0.005° orientation over 563 movements (hifi2025flexibleandintelligent pages 69-74). The KUKA KMR iiwa has a manufacturer-quoted positioning accuracy of up to ±5 mm, with experimental results at low speed generally remaining below 2 mm (hifi2025flexibleandintelligent pages 208-214, hifi2025flexibleandintelligent pages 63-69). Your proposed ±3 mm AMR docking plus AprilTag re-registration with a wrist camera is therefore realistic and consistent with published results, provided that station-specific datum probing or touch-off is added as a secondary verification. For CNC loading at ±0.1 mm repeatability, the cobot's own repeatability (±0.05 mm for UR10e/12e) must be used relative to a station-registered frame, not the AMR pose.

### 1.3 Uptime, Failure Rates, and Cycle-Time Penalties

No published study reports operational uptime, mean-time-between-failure, or quantified cycle-time penalties for a mobile manipulator tending CNC machines in production. NIST's survey of manufacturing robotics barriers identified organizational uptime targets of 16,000–80,000 hours and noted that uncertainty about robotic reliability is a major adoption barrier (messina2024researchopportunitiesfor pages 17-20). The docking overhead per station visit—including navigation, docking, registration verification, and tool change—is an inherent cycle-time penalty, estimated at 1–3 minutes per station visit based on AMR transit speeds and typical registration sequences, which is significant for short machining cycles but negligible for multi-hour CNC operations.

### 1.4 Commissioning Effort Per New Task

Cobot manufacturers claim that a simple screwdriving or pick-and-place task can be programmed in minutes, while conventional automation deployment commonly takes 8–17 weeks (brera2022collaborativescrewdrivingintegrating pages 35-38, brera2022collaborativescrewdrivingintegratinga pages 35-38). However, full cell commissioning—including safety assessment, sensing, grippers, recovery logic, and validation—is substantially longer and is not well-quantified in the literature. In one case study of cobot welding for an SME, only about 3% of technically suitable welding jobs were actually automated because programming time was burdensome for small batches, even though 95% of jobs were theoretically feasible (schumacher2022technologyadoptionof pages 5-7). NIST identifies long integration timelines, programming burden, and limited redeployability as persistent barriers to manufacturing robot adoption, particularly in high-mix production (messina2024researchopportunitiesfor pages 17-20, messina2024researchopportunitiesfor pages 34-37, messina2024researchopportunitiesfor pages 13-17, messina2024researchopportunitiesfor pages 20-25).

### 1.5 What Still Needs People

Published reviews consistently identify the following as requiring human involvement: emergency handling and machine-screen interaction for CNC cycle control (feiyu2024anintelligentframework pages 43-47); first-article verification, CAM programming, and tool setting; heavy-part handling beyond cobot payload; deformable-object manipulation (cables, hoses); exception handling and fault recovery; inspection requiring judgment; and process changes or new part introductions (messina2024researchopportunitiesfor pages 34-37, messina2024researchopportunitiesfor pages 28-30).

### 3.1 Detailed Discussion of Key Failure Modes

**Cross-threading and galling of stainless screws** are the highest-consequence assembly failures. Cross-threading occurs when external and internal threads engage on misaligned helices (pagliara2025improvingdependabilityin pages 2-3). Detection relies on torque–angle monitoring: each successful screw insertion produces a characteristic torque–angle signature, and deviations outside a taught window indicate faults. Torque-rate monitoring samples torque after each angular increment and compares the current deviation against a tolerance zone (xu2019areviewon pages 16-17). Published general fault-recognition rates reach approximately 90–97% using teach-based, ANN, or ML classifiers, but deployment-ready performance on actual assembly lines remains an ongoing challenge (brera2022collaborativescrewdrivingintegrating pages 31-35). Torque-only control can deviate by up to 50% due to friction, making it unsuitable for precise fault detection without supplementary angle data (brera2022collaborativescrewdrivingintegrating pages 31-35).

Galling is a specific risk for stainless steel that is **not addressed in the robotic assembly literature**. It requires process-level mitigation: compatible alloy grades, anti-seize lubricant, clean threads, slow driving speed, and conservative torque limits. The torque signature for galling—a sudden, irregular torque increase with stick-slip behavior—overlaps with other friction anomalies, making automated detection unreliable without validated process-specific envelopes.

**Chips and coolant contamination** of fixtures and datums is the most common CNC machine-tending failure mode. Swarf on a vise jaw or chuck datum creates a seating error that propagates through machining. Detection requires dedicated close-up vision of datum surfaces, air-pressure/flow confirmation, and force-displacement monitoring during part seating.

**Part pose errors** from gripper misalignment or incorrect orientation cause downstream failures in CNC loading and assembly. Force/torque data during insertion show distinctive profiles—sawtooth patterns during successful threading, and distinctive force spikes or stalls during failures—but relating force/torque data to part geometry is identified as a missing capability (watson2020autonomousindustrialassembly pages 16-18).

**O-ring twist and incomplete seating** are especially dangerous for half-dovetail and internal rod glands where the groove is occluded. Force alone does not prove correct circumferential seating, and visual inspection may be inadequate for internal grooves. Full-circumference machine vision, borescope inspection, or pressure-decay testing after closure are the most reliable detection methods.

## 4. Safety Standards and Risk Assessment Requirements

### 4.1 Applicable Standards

The safety framework for your proposed cell must combine multiple standards because no single standard covers integrated mobile manipulators (alexandra2019safetyofmobile pages 3-4, vitolo2022mobilerobotsand pages 5-6, alexandra2019safetyofmobile pages 2-3):

- **ISO 10218-1:2025** addresses robot design and construction, including functional safety, operating modes, emergency stops, and speed limiting (duplak2025applicationofsafety pages 2-4).
- **ISO 10218-2:2025** addresses robot system integration, covering cell layout, safeguarding, commissioning, programming, operation, and maintenance. The 2025 revision gives greater attention to collaborative applications (faria2020safetyrequirementsfor pages 4-6, valori2021validatingsafetyin pages 5-6).
- **ISO/TS 15066:2016** supplements ISO 10218 for collaborative operation and defines four modes: Safety-rated Monitored Stop (SRMS), Hand-Guiding (HG), Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM), and Power and Force Limiting (PFL) (faria2020safetyrequirementsfor pages 4-6, valori2021validatingsafetyin pages 5-6, duplak2025applicationofsafety pages 4-6). PFL requires that contact forces remain below body-region-specific thresholds. For a mobile manipulator where contact with the face cannot be excluded, the worst-case transient force limit is 65 N and the corresponding maximum interface speed is 0.12 m/s (vitolo2022mobilerobotsand pages 15-17). The body model contains 29 specific contact locations grouped into 12 body regions; quasi-static limits are half the transient limits (scibilia2021analysisofinterlaboratory pages 1-2, scibilia2021analysisofinterlaboratory pages 2-3).
- **ISO 3691-4** addresses safety requirements for driverless industrial trucks, including AMRs (alexandra2019safetyofmobile pages 2-3).
- **ANSI/RIA R15.08** was developed specifically for industrial mobile robots and mobile manipulators to address gaps among AGV, AMR, and mobile-manipulator standards. At the time of key publications, it was announced for publication in early 2019 as a draft (alexandra2019safetyofmobile pages 3-4). It is now published and addresses the combined hazards of mobility and manipulation.
- **ISO 12100** provides the overarching risk-assessment methodology (mariano2026roboticsafetyin pages 3-3, duplak2025applicationofsafety pages 2-4, duplak2025applicationofsafety pages 4-6).

### 4.2 Risk Assessment Requirements for This Cell

A documented, iterative risk assessment is required covering (mariano2026roboticsafetyin pages 3-3, faria2020safetyrequirementsfor pages 4-6, duplak2025applicationofsafety pages 2-4, alexandra2019safetyofmobile pages 3-4, alexandra2019safetyofmobile pages 2-3):

1. **System limits definition:** usage, spatial, temporal, and other limits, including the entire mobile platform, robot arm, end-effectors, surrounding roads, work areas, human interaction, and interfering factory traffic.
2. **Hazard identification:** mechanical (crush, impact, shear, entanglement), electrical, thermal, noise, chemical (coolant, cleaning solvents), and radiation (if laser testing is performed) hazards.
3. **Representative contact scenarios:** transient and quasi-static collisions between the mobile manipulator and personnel at every reachable position and speed, including during transit, docking, and manipulation.
4. **Combined-system hazards:** The mobile base, manipulator, payload, sensors, and actuators must be assessed as an integrated system, not as separate components (alexandra2019safetyofmobile pages 3-4, alexandra2019safetyofmobile pages 2-3).
5. **Risk evaluation and reduction:** inherently safe design, safety-rated functions (monitored stop, speed limiting, force limiting), protective measures (sensors, interlocks, barriers where needed), and residual-risk documentation.
6. **Validation:** stopping-performance tests, force/pressure measurements against ISO/TS 15066 limits, interlock verification, speed-and-separation tests, and documented acceptance (valori2021validatingsafetyin pages 5-6, scibilia2021analysisofinterlaboratory pages 1-2, scibilia2021analysisofinterlaboratory pages 2-3).

For a university shop where people move freely, the most practical collaborative mode is **PFL during manipulation** (UR12e's built-in force/torque sensing supports this) combined with **SSM during transit** (MiR250's laser scanners provide separation monitoring). However, PFL speed limits of 0.12 m/s worst-case would impose severe cycle-time penalties if people are routinely nearby; zoning the shop to separate transit corridors from active workstations is strongly recommended.

### 5.1 Key Findings

**For a single machine, the commissioning-to-build ratio is approximately 3:1 to 7:1 against automation.** The estimated total to produce one machine via the mobile-manipulator cell is 1,430–2,850 person-hours, versus 360–740 person-hours for a fully manual build. The dominant cost is commissioning: approximately 1,230–2,470 person-hours for end-effector design, assembly programming (~90 steps at 3–7 hours each), vision and sensor commissioning, safety assessment, process validation, and debugging. By comparison, cobots claim minute-scale programming for individual simple tasks, but complete cell integration is orders of magnitude larger (brera2022collaborativescrewdrivingintegrating pages 35-38).

**In calendar time,** assuming a team of two robotics-capable graduate students working approximately 30 productive hours per week, commissioning would take approximately 20–40 weeks (5–10 months). The manual build by an experienced instrument maker or machinist might take 10–20 weeks of elapsed time. Conventional automation deployment is reported at 8–17 weeks, but that assumes a production-ready integrator and a repeating task (brera2022collaborativescrewdrivingintegrating pages 35-38).

**The break-even point for the robotic cell** depends on reuse. If the same programs, grippers, and processes are used for 5–10 identical machines, the per-unit commissioning amortization drops to 120–500 person-hours, approaching parity with manual build. However, the question specifies a one-off machine, making the economic case clearly unfavorable unless the cell commissioning itself is a research objective.

### 5.2 Recommendation

For a one-off desktop LPBF machine in a university shop, **building manually with selective cobot assistance** is more practical than fully commissioning the mobile-manipulator cell. A pragmatic hybrid approach would:

1. **Build the first machine primarily by hand**, using the UR12e arms on their MiR250 bases as general-purpose material handlers and CNC tending assistants for the longest-running machining operations.
2. **Automate 2–3 high-value repetitive subtasks** (e.g., CNC part loading/unloading during long mill runs, kit transfer between stations, ultrasonic wash loading) where the programming effort is modest and the time saved during machining is substantial.
3. **Treat the remaining assembly as a development testbed**: program and validate individual skills (screw driving, dowel insertion, O-ring installation with custom tooling) on representative sub-assemblies, building toward a future production capability.
4. **Retain people for all tasks on the person-only list plus the additions identified in Section 2.1**, and do not attempt dual-mobile-base coordinated lifting.

This approach produces the machine in approximately 400–700 person-hours while building reusable robotic skills that could be deployed if a second or third machine is built, and avoids the 1,200–2,500 person-hour commissioning overhead for a cell that runs once.
