## Build-Plate Preheating in Laser Powder Bed Fusion: When Is 400–600 °C Needed, What Complications Arise, and How Should the Platform Be Designed?

This review synthesizes peer-reviewed literature on high-temperature build-plate preheating for LPBF, covering preheat requirements by alloy class, practical complications, commercial machine implementations, build-plate materials, and plate-to-bore sealing strategies. The findings are organized around the three questions posed, followed by the requested summary table and ranked design recommendations.

---

### 1. When Is 400–600 °C Preheat Actually Needed—and When Is ≤200 °C Sufficient?

#### 1.1 Crack-Prone Wrought Aluminium Alloys (2xxx, 6xxx, 7xxx)

The 2xxx, 6xxx, and 7xxx aluminium series are susceptible to solidification (hot) cracking because of their broad freezing ranges—approximately 132 K for Al 2024, 140 K for Al 6061, and 177 K for Al 7075 (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 34-38). Crack-free unmodified AA6061 has been demonstrated at 500 °C powder-bed preheating, yielding modest tensile properties (YS ≈ 60 MPa, UTS ≈ 130 MPa, elongation ≈ 15 %) (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 31-34). For AA7075, a 200 °C base-plate preheat did not eliminate cracking despite producing high relative density (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 31-34). For 2xxx alloys, substrate preheating from 200–400 °C reduced but did not eliminate thermal cracking (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 31-34).

**Grain-refiner alternatives are strongly effective.** Adding 1 wt% Zr nanoparticles to AA6061 and AA7075 powders formed nano-Al₃Zr nucleation sites, promoting equiaxed grain growth and producing crack-free, fully dense microstructures without requiring high preheating (bradfordvialva2021developmentofa pages 45-49). Combining 1 wt% Zr with just 100 °C platform heating significantly reduced porosity and cracking in AA6061 (dixit2022laseradditivemanufacturing pages 16-18). In situ TiAl₃ formation from Ti + B₄C additions produced partial grain refinement and prevented solidification cracks in A6061 (rosito2023processabilityofa6061 pages 1-2). For Al-Cu-Mg alloys, 1.5 wt% Ti reduced average grain size from 6.64 to 1.64 µm and prevented crack formation, while 0.5 wt% nano-CaB₆ reduced cracking in AA2024 (tumulu2025laserpowderbed pages 48-53). Si additions (3–4 wt%) improve Al 7075 processability by promoting eutectic phases and reducing the solidification range (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 34-38). Green-laser processing has also been demonstrated as an alternative, reducing energy density requirements and Mg/Zn evaporation while enabling crack-free 6061 and 7075 with Ti particle additions (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 31-34).

#### 1.2 Al-Mg-Sc/Zr (Scalmalloy®) and AlSi10Mg

Scalmalloy® (Al-4.5Mg-0.73Sc-0.27Zr-0.49Mn) does not require high preheat to prevent cracking; Sc and Zr intrinsically form Al₃(Sc,Zr) nucleants that promote fine equiaxed grains. A 200 °C build platform has been used for in-situ solute clustering and aging during the LPBF process, producing high strength directly in the as-built condition and avoiding a separate heat-treatment step (schimback2024advancementsinmetal pages 1-2). Conventional peak aging requires temperatures above 300 °C (schimback2024advancementsinmetal pages 1-2).

AlSi10Mg is the most widely printed aluminium alloy and normally requires no preheat above ambient to 200 °C for crack-free processing. The near-eutectic Si content provides a narrow solidification range, good fluidity, and inherent grain refinement (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 31-34, wang2024areviewon pages 7-9).

#### 1.3 Tool Steels (H13, M2, M50, High-Speed Steels)

For AISI H13 hot-work tool steel (≈0.3–0.4 wt% C), preheating at 200 °C is reported as processible, though results are powder- and parameter-dependent (wu2021hotworktool pages 3-4). Preheating up to 200 °C was associated with compressive stresses, while 300–400 °C produced tensile stresses because the temperature exceeded the martensite-start temperature (platl2021defectsina pages 1-4). For Thermodour 2344 (essentially H13), preheating at 200, 300, and 500 °C was investigated, but cracking was not completely prevented even at 500 °C (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 13-14). The relationship between carbon content, martensite transformation temperature, and cracking is complex—carbon content alone is not a reliable predictor, and thermal conductivity differences between powders can dominate (wu2021hotworktool pages 25-25).

For M2 high-speed steel, cracking occurred at 100 and 180 °C, while 200 °C produced crack-free material in one study (wu2021hotworktool pages 3-4). However, other work found cold cracks persisted across 200–400 °C (bergmueller2022crackfreeinsitu pages 1-2). For M50, substrate preheating up to 500 °C improved processability, with the microstructure changing from epitaxial grain growth at ≈200 °C to columnar dendritic bainitic structure with precipitated carbides at 500 °C (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 12-13). For HS6-5-3-8, approximately 350 °C minimized initial crack formation, but cracks could not be completely avoided even with high-temperature platform preheating (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 12-13). A related grade (HS6-5-8-3) was reported crack-free at 500 °C (wu2021hotworktool pages 3-4). An even higher-carbon steel (1.64 wt% C) required whole powder-bed preheating at approximately 800 °C for crack-free samples (platl2021defectsina pages 1-4).

Crack risk is strongly related to carbon content, with risk initially decreasing over 0.2–0.5% C then increasing. Preheating above the martensite-start temperature suppresses transformation during building but can produce very high residual stress during final cooling (wu2021hotworktool pages 25-25).

#### 1.4 Non-Weldable Nickel Superalloys (CM247LC, IN738LC, IN713LC)

These γ′-strengthened superalloys represent the most demanding case for preheating. For CM247LC and IN713LC, preheating up to approximately 900 °C did not eliminate hot cracking; cracks remained associated with γ′ precipitation and segregation of carbide-forming elements. The study indicated that preheating above the γ′-solvus temperature—exceeding 1200 °C—would be needed to fully suppress cracks (abdelwahed2023crackingmechanismsand pages 1-2). For IN738LC, high-temperature preheating (500–1200 °C) is identified as a key strategy, with crack-free EB-PBF forming demonstrated at 1000 °C using multiple preheating processes (abdelwahed2023crackingmechanismsand pages 1-2). For IN939, 400 °C preheating increased residual stress by 16.2% compared to room temperature, attributed to γ′ precipitation during fabrication (mariana2025innovationsinin939 pages 16-19).

Weldable IN718, by contrast, does not normally require high preheat. Builds at 80, 300, and 500 °C have been investigated, with the main effects being on defect occurrence and stress distribution rather than preventing fundamental cracking (calliariUnknownyearinfluenceofprocess pages 18-22, fassinatoUnknownyearinfluenceofprocess pages 35-40).

#### 1.5 Ti-6Al-4V, TiAl, NiTi, Cu, W, Mo, WC-Co

**Ti-6Al-4V** does not generally require high preheat for crack-free bulk parts; 200–400 °C is used primarily for residual-stress reduction in distortion-sensitive or large builds. The Aeroswift platform targeted 600 °C for large Ti-6Al-4V builds (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 1-3).

**γ-TiAl intermetallics** require whole-build-volume induction heating at approximately 800 °C for crack-free processing (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 3-5).

**Pure tungsten** requires approximately 1000 °C preheating for crack-free processing, though porosity remains an issue (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 13-14).

**WC-17Co** was processed at 900 °C powder-bed preheating on a laboratory-scale Aconity MIDI system, reducing porosity to approximately 2.0 %, though cobalt evaporation, brittle phases (W₂C, η-phase), and delamination remain concerns (fries2020laser‐basedadditivemanufacturing pages 1-2, fries2020laser‐basedadditivemanufacturing pages 3-3).

**Nickel aluminium bronze** (Cu alloy) was investigated from 25–550 °C on an EOS M290-HT, with platform temperature strongly influencing distortion, cracking, and phase formation (saremi2026designingnickelaluminum pages 4-6).

**Molybdenum** printed on AISI 304 stainless steel delaminated after approximately 10 layers due to brittle Sigma phase formation at the diluted interface (≈40–50 wt% Mo). A Cu-OFE plate provided good compatibility (rebesan2021characterizationofmolybdenum pages 70-75).

#### 1.6 Commercial Machines Offering ≥400 °C

**SLM 280**: A commercially available SLM 280 equipped with a preheating system capable of 500 °C has been used for tool steel processing (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 10-11).

**Aconity 3D (MIDI/MINI)**: Known for customized machines with preheating systems above 1000 °C (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 10-11). The AconityMIDI provides a maximum platform temperature of 500 °C, with a build space of approximately 170 mm diameter × 180 mm height, residual oxygen below 100 ppm, and a 200 W single-mode fiber laser (fassinatoUnknownyearinfluenceofprocess pages 35-40). WC-Co was processed at 900 °C on an Aconity MIDI (fries2020laser‐basedadditivemanufacturing pages 1-2). High-speed steel HS2-2-2 was processed on the same platform type at room temperature, 200, 350, and 500 °C under argon with O₂ below 200 ppm (lucke2023influenceoflaser pages 2-5).

**EOS M290-HT**: A modified M290 with a 12.5 cm diameter base plate reaching up to 900 °C. It incorporates VCSEL (vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser) modules for upper powder-bed heating up to 1200 °C, with an infrared camera measuring surface temperature every second for dynamic control. Each newly recoated layer can be uniformly heated in less than half a second without separate pre- or post-heating steps. A gas-purification system maintains oxygen below 200 ppm (saremi2026designingnickelaluminum pages 4-6, saremi2026designingnickelaluminum pages 3-4).

**Aeroswift (large-format)**: Uses 3 kW aluminum-nitride ceramic resistive heaters, targeting 600 °C for Ti-6Al-4V. FEA predicted only 395 °C at the heater and 374 °C at the substrate due to multiple conductive heat-loss paths and 44% insulation efficiency. Redesigned concepts predicted 650 °C without additional heater power (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 1-3, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 5-7).

**Induction-heated platforms** have been used for WC-Co at 900 °C and γ-TiAl at 800 °C, and laser-diode in-situ annealing for 316L at 625 °C (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 3-5).

### 2. Complications at 400–600 °C

#### 2.1 Powder Flowability, Sintering, and Agglomeration

Particles near the melt pool or exposed to sustained heating can form solid necks or fuse into larger clusters, shifting the particle-size distribution toward coarser sizes and reducing free-flowing, reusable material (rezaei2026powderdegradationand pages 7-9). These agglomerates can obstruct recoaters or delivery systems, produce streaks or local powder-bed irregularities, and require more energy to melt completely, potentially creating lack-of-fusion defects (rezaei2026powderdegradationand pages 7-9). Studies on GH3536 Ni-based powder showed that at 100–200 °C the powder remained highly cohesive and non-flowable by Hall flow testing; at 300 °C cohesion decreased enough for some flowability; and at 400 °C the best spreading was achieved (repose angle decreasing from ≈43° at 100 °C to ≈27° at 400 °C) (wang2024effectofparticle pages 7-9, wang2024effectofparticle pages 6-7). However, heating also increased oxygen content to 478 ppm at 400 °C (wang2024effectofparticle pages 7-9). For aluminium powders specifically, the literature identifies moisture, oxide coatings, condensate, and fine particles as the dominant factors controlling cohesion and spreadability, but controlled temperature-dependent flowability data at 400–600 °C for Al LPBF powder is sparse (rezaei2026powderdegradationand pages 29-30, rezaei2026powderdegradationand pages 41-42, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 1-2).

#### 2.2 Oxidation, Mg/Zn Evaporation, and Atmosphere Control

Aluminium powder is hydrophilic and readily forms alumina; humid powder aging increases alumina and hydroxide inclusions and raises oxygen and hydrogen content, destabilizing the melt pool (hussain2026metallurgicalaspectsof pages 11-12). Over five production cycles, AlSi10Mg powder oxygen content increased by 120 ppm when affected by laser-induced heating, compared with only 10 ppm for powder recycled without heat exposure (sargioti2025physicochemicalandtoxicological pages 2-4). Mg (boiling point ≈1091 °C) and Zn (≈907 °C) are selectively lost from LPBF melt pools at high energy densities; Zn is lost preferentially, with the Zn:Mg ratio decreasing by as much as 33% compared to starting powder at high volumetric energy densities (fields2024structuralandcompositional pages 58-63). These losses reduce the concentrations of strengthening-phase formers (Mg₂Si in 6061, MgZn₂ in 7075) (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 38-42). Metal vapor condenses into nanoscale fume particles (≈10–150 nm) that scatter the laser beam, reduce energy delivery, destabilize melting, increase porosity, and contaminate optics (qin2021influenceoflaser pages 10-12). Excessive gas flow can become turbulent and disturb powder or melt-pool behavior, while insufficient flow allows fume accumulation (qin2021influenceoflaser pages 10-12, qin2021influenceoflaser pages 1-2).

Operating oxygen levels in reported high-temperature systems are generally below 200 ppm (EOS M290-HT) (saremi2026designingnickelaluminum pages 4-6) to below 100 ppm (Aconity MIDI) (fassinatoUnknownyearinfluenceofprocess pages 35-40, lucke2023influenceoflaser pages 2-5). The literature uniformly emphasizes the need for high-purity argon atmosphere and moisture control, but quantitative dew-point specifications for aluminium LPBF are rarely stated explicitly.

#### 2.3 Thermal Management, Distortion, and Part Removal

Preheating can paradoxically increase residual stress in some alloys: for IN939, 400 °C preheating increased distortion by 16.2% (maximum 0.028 mm) compared to room temperature, attributed to γ′ precipitation (mariana2025innovationsinin939 pages 16-19). Very high melt-pool temperatures at elevated preheat can lower melt viscosity, negatively affecting part contour resolution (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 10-11). The Aeroswift study demonstrated that multiple metal-to-metal contacts and inadequate insulation can prevent the substrate from reaching its target temperature—a ceramic heater design predicted 395 °C at the heater but only 374 °C at the substrate (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 1-3, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 5-7).

Diffusion bonding of parts to the build plate is enhanced at higher temperatures due to reduced thermal gradients and improved metallurgical bonding, making part removal more difficult. Dissimilar material interfaces can form brittle intermetallic phases; Mo on stainless steel delaminated after ≈10 layers due to Sigma phase formation (rebesan2021characterizationofmolybdenum pages 70-75).

### 3. Build-Plate Materials, Thermal Expansion, and Plate-to-Bore Fit

#### 3.1 Thermal Expansion Mismatch

For a Ø75 mm 6061 plate at 500 °C in a 316L bore at ≈55 °C, using representative mean CTEs of ≈24–25 µm/m/K for 6061 and ≈16–17 µm/m/K for 316L, the differential diametral growth is approximately 0.36–0.41 mm—substantially more than the ≈0.14 mm stated for the 200 °C tier. This necessitates cold diametral clearances of approximately 0.50–0.65 mm before tolerances, cylindricity, bow, deposits, and seizure margin.

Lower-CTE aluminium plate materials such as 4032 (CTE ≈19 µm/m/K), A390, or other hypereutectic Al-Si alloys would reduce clearance closure. Al-SiC composites offer even lower CTE (≈12–17 µm/m/K depending on SiC fraction) but introduce concerns about reduced ductility, heterogeneous remelting at the first-layer interface, thermal fatigue, and machining difficulty. Spray-formed hypereutectic Al-Si controlled-expansion alloys are commercially available and represent an intermediate option. However, alloy-specific LPBF substrate qualification data for these materials is essentially absent from the peer-reviewed literature.

#### 3.2 Printing Al on Dissimilar Substrates

Dissimilar LPBF interfaces can form brittle intermetallic compounds, crack, or delaminate due to incompatible melting points, thermal conductivities, and CTEs (rebesan2021characterizationofmolybdenum pages 70-75, dubey2024negativethermalexpansion pages 9-11). The formation of brittle intermetallic phases is a primary concern in multi-material LPBF. For aluminium on steel, Fe-Al intermetallics (e.g., FeAl₃, Fe₂Al₅) form at the interface and are notoriously brittle. Aluminium on titanium would similarly form TiAl₃ intermetallics. Aluminium on copper forms Al₂Cu and other phases. These interfaces can compromise adhesion and part removal.

The practical solution is to use alloy-matched sacrificial build plates: aluminium for aluminium builds, steel for steel, Ni-alloy for Ni superalloys, Ti for Ti alloys. Where matching is impractical, a removable interlayer or sheet can be used—for example, a Mo sheet on a stainless platform solved delamination in Mo printing (rebesan2021characterizationofmolybdenum pages 70-75).

#### 3.3 Sealing Between Hot Plate and Cooler Cylinder

Published details of commercial seal designs are extremely limited. The Aeroswift platform used 5 mm Cermex BK2100SW insulation mats between the heater and mechanical components, with Ti-6Al-4V build plates mounted on a mild-steel baseplate inside a stainless-steel 17-4PH powder pool (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 5-7). Elastomeric seals are unsuitable above ≈300 °C.

For 500 °C operation, the literature and engineering practice point to: (1) replaceable compliant graphite felt/foil rings or aluminosilicate ceramic-fibre wipers outside the precision guiding land; (2) stepped labyrinth geometries that limit direct powder migration without requiring a zero-clearance sliding fit; and (3) decoupled guidance and sealing—guide the piston on a cool, long lower land while letting the upper hot plate float radially within its calculated clearance with a compliant upper seal that excludes powder but carries no lateral load (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 1-3, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 19-20, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 5-7).

### (b) Ranked Design Recommendations for the 500 °C Tier and Plate Material/Fit

| Rank | Recommendation | Rationale / evidence |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | **Do not make 500 °C the default for aluminium screening.** Use Tier A (≤200 °C) for AlSi10Mg and Al-Mg-Sc/Zr, and reserve Tier B for unmodified 6xxx experiments, in-situ heat treatment, or exceptionally stress-sensitive geometries. | AlSi10Mg normally prints without high preheat; Scalmalloy uses about 200 °C for in-situ clustering rather than crack prevention. Unmodified 6061 has been made crack-free at 500 °C, but inoculated alloys can achieve comparable crack control at much lower temperature (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 31-34, dixit2022laseradditivemanufacturing pages 16-18, schimback2024advancementsinmetal pages 1-2). |
| 2 | **Prefer grain-refining chemistry over a 500 °C plate for 2xxx, 6xxx, and 7xxx development.** Screen Zr/Sc, Ti/TiB₂, TiC, CaB₆, and eutectic-forming additions first. | Zr nanoparticles produced crack-free, fully dense 6061/7075; 1 wt% Zr with 100 °C reduced 6061 cracking; TiAl₃ prevented 6061 cracking; 1.5 wt% Ti prevented cracking in Al-Cu-Mg. By contrast, 200 °C failed for 7075 and 200–400 °C failed to eliminate 2xxx cracking (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 31-34, dixit2022laseradditivemanufacturing pages 16-18, rosito2023processabilityofa6061 pages 1-2, bradfordvialva2021developmentofa pages 45-49, tumulu2025laserpowderbed pages 48-53). |
| 3 | **Replace the 6061 heater block with a mechanically retained steel, precipitation-resistant stainless steel, Ni alloy, or ceramic heater carrier.** Do not structurally load 6061 at 500 °C. | High-temperature platforms require all supporting and moving components to tolerate sustained heat. A ceramic resistive heater can reach the required temperature, but conductive losses through surrounding steel hardware can prevent the plate from reaching its target (rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 10-11, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 1-3, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 5-7). |
| 4 | **Thermally isolate the heater assembly from the piston and cylinder.** Use thick low-conductivity ceramic board/fibre insulation, minimized metallic contact area, slender thermal breaks, and radiation shields. | In the Aeroswift study, multiple metal contacts and only 5 mm insulation limited a nominal 600 °C system to much lower plate temperatures; the insulation was only 44% effective. Improved isolation was predicted to permit approximately 650 °C without more heater power (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 1-3, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 5-7). |
| 5 | **Use a separately water-cooled piston head, seal carrier, actuator interface, and cylinder rim—but interlock cooling flow with heater power.** | Cooling protects bearings, seals, encoder/actuator components, and the honed bore while allowing the removable plate to remain hot. The strong resulting gradient makes clearance and plate bowing critical; commercial high-temperature systems likewise incorporate chamber cooling (saremi2026designingnickelaluminum pages 3-4). |
| 6 | **Size the cold diametral clearance from the worst differential expansion, not the Tier-A value.** For a Ø75 mm 6061 plate at 500 °C in a 316L bore near 55 °C, differential diametral growth is approximately 0.36–0.41 mm; specify at least about 0.50–0.65 mm cold diametral clearance before adding tolerances, cylindricity, bow, deposits, and seizure margin. | Using representative mean CTEs of approximately 24–25 µm·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ for 6061 and 16–17 µm·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ for 316L gives about 0.18–0.21 mm **radial** closure—substantially more than the stated 0.14 mm Tier-A closure. The final fit must be validated thermally because a cooled bore and hot plate are non-isothermal. |
| 7 | **Do not use an elastomer as the hot-edge powder seal.** Use a replaceable compliant graphite felt/foil ring or aluminosilicate/other ceramic-fibre wiper outside the precision guiding land; add a stepped labyrinth and powder-catching annulus. | Elastomers are unsuitable around 500 °C, while fibrous high-temperature insulation and graphite tolerate the thermal environment. A labyrinth limits direct powder migration without requiring a zero-clearance sliding fit. Published commercial clearances and proprietary seal geometries are generally unavailable, so qualification testing is required (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 1-3, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 19-20, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 5-7). |
| 8 | **Decouple guidance from sealing.** Guide the piston on a cool, long lower land or separate bearing; let the upper hot plate float radially within its calculated clearance. | A hot plate tightly guided by the cooler honed bore risks seizure, galling, bore scoring, and plate buckling. The compliant upper seal should exclude powder, not carry lateral load. |
| 9 | **For aluminium builds, retain an aluminium-family sacrificial build plate whenever possible.** Start with 6061/5083 at ≤200 °C; evaluate 4032 or another qualified high-Si wrought/cast plate for Tier B. | Like-on-like aluminium gives predictable remelting, wetting, and removal. Higher-Si aluminium offers lower CTE and better elevated-temperature dimensional stability than 6061, but its LPBF interface, brittleness, flatness, and machinability must be tested. Evidence for specific 4032/A390 LPBF substrate qualification is sparse, so treat this as an engineering trial rather than a literature-proven substitution. |
| 10 | **Use hypereutectic Al-Si or Al-SiC only after coupon-level interface qualification; do not make ceramic-rich Al-SiC the first Tier-B plate.** | Controlled-expansion Al-Si and Al-SiC can reduce clearance closure, but reduced ductility, heterogeneous remelting, ceramic exposure, thermal fatigue, machining difficulty, and uncertain first-layer bonding can outweigh the CTE benefit. Compositionally compatible metallic Al-Si is the lower-risk first trial. |
| 11 | **Avoid direct aluminium deposition on steel or Invar unless a deliberately engineered interlayer/interface is acceptable.** Use a removable aluminium insert clamped to a steel heater carrier instead. | Dissimilar LPBF interfaces can form brittle intermetallics, crack, or delaminate because of incompatible melting points, conductivities, and CTEs. Mo on stainless steel, for example, delaminated after roughly ten layers where a brittle diluted phase formed, demonstrating that substrate compatibility can dominate the build (rebesan2021characterizationofmolybdenum pages 70-75, dubey2024negativethermalexpansion pages 9-11). |
| 12 | **Use alloy-matched substrates by material family:** aluminium plate for Al, steel plate for steels, Ni-alloy plate for Ni superalloys, Ti plate for Ti alloys, and compatible Cu/Cu-alloy plate for copper. | Matched plates minimize brittle dilution zones and differential contraction. Where matching is impractical, use a replaceable sheet/interlayer: a retained Mo sheet or Cu-OFE substrate solved an otherwise unsuccessful Mo/stainless interface in published work (rebesan2021characterizationofmolybdenum pages 70-75). |
| 13 | **Control oxygen to below 200 ppm at minimum and preferably below 100 ppm before heating; target still lower values for Ti, Mg-rich Al, and long hot soaks. Measure moisture/dew point independently.** | Reported high-temperature systems operate below 200 ppm; Aconity work reports below 100 ppm. Aluminium powder exposed to laser heating gained about 120 ppm oxygen over five cycles versus 10 ppm without heat exposure, and hot spatter is especially oxidation-prone (saremi2026designingnickelaluminum pages 4-6, sargioti2025physicochemicalandtoxicological pages 2-4, fassinatoUnknownyearinfluenceofprocess pages 35-40, lucke2023influenceoflaser pages 2-5). |
| 14 | **Bake/purge the empty chamber and hot-zone hardware before admitting production powder, and use only high-temperature-compatible seals, wiring, insulation, and lubricants.** | Heating releases adsorbed water from chamber walls, oxide/hydroxide-bearing powder, insulation, and seals. Moisture thickens aluminium oxide/hydroxide films, impairs spreading and bonding, and supplies hydrogen that can create porosity (hussain2026metallurgicalaspectsof pages 11-12, rezaei2026powderdegradationand pages 29-30, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 1-2). |

| 16 | **Thermally shield and, where needed, actively cool the laser window, O₂ sensor sampling line, recoater drive, hopper throat, camera, and chamber lid.** Locate the O₂ sensor in a conditioned bypass rather than directly above the hot bed. | A 500 °C plate radiates strongly into a 6 L chamber; commercial high-temperature systems incorporate chamber cooling and active monitoring. Fume and condensate additionally threaten the window and sensor, while temperature shifts can bias sensors and expand recoater hardware (saremi2026designingnickelaluminum pages 3-4, qin2021influenceoflaser pages 10-12). |
| 17 | **Use a thermally robust recoater and qualify hot spreading with the actual powder.** Prefer a replaceable metal/ceramic blade, force monitoring, and powder with tightly controlled fines; minimize particles below roughly 15 µm for Tier-B trials. | Fines, moisture, oxide coatings, agglomerates, and partial sintering increase cohesion and produce streaks or low-density layers. | 18 | **Limit and log each powder lot’s cumulative hot exposure.** Initially restrict 500 °C trials to short, shallow builds; segregate hot-zone powder, sieve it, and test oxygen, PSD, flow/spread density, and Mg/Zn composition before reuse. | Thermal exposure produces necking, satellites, agglomeration and oxide/hydroxide condensate. ### Key Conclusions

1. **For the primary aluminium alloy screening mission, 200 °C (Tier A) is sufficient for AlSi10Mg, Al-Mg-Sc/Zr, and inoculated 6xxx/7xxx/2xxx alloys.** The 500 °C tier is needed primarily for: unmodified 6061 crack elimination, in-situ heat treatment experiments, residual-stress-sensitive thin-wall geometries, and later expansion to tool steels or other crack-prone materials (li2024additivemanufacturingof pages 31-34, dixit2022laseradditivemanufacturing pages 16-18, schimback2024advancementsinmetal pages 1-2).

2. **Grain-refining additions (Zr, Sc, Ti, TiB₂, TiC, CaB₆) are more effective and far less mechanically complex than a 500 °C platform for eliminating solidification cracking in wrought Al alloys** (bradfordvialva2021developmentofa pages 45-49, tumulu2025laserpowderbed pages 48-53, wang2024areviewon pages 7-9).

3. **The 500 °C tier introduces substantial engineering challenges:** thermal expansion management (≈0.4 mm differential growth for Ø75 mm plate), powder sintering and degradation, accelerated oxidation, Mg/Zn evaporation, insulation inefficiency, component heating, and enhanced part-to-plate bonding.

4. **For tool steels, 200 °C handles H13 and M2 in many process windows; 500 °C extends the range to M50 and some high-speed steels.** Non-weldable Ni superalloys and refractory metals require temperatures far exceeding 500 °C (>900–1200 °C), which are beyond the scope of the proposed Tier B (abdelwahed2023crackingmechanismsand pages 1-2, wu2021hotworktool pages 3-4, rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 13-14).

5. **The EOS M290-HT (VCSEL-based, up to 900 °C base plate, 1200 °C powder-bed top-layer heating) and Aconity MIDI (up to 500 °C standard, >1000 °C customized) represent the current commercial state of the art** for high-temperature LPBF preheating (saremi2026designingnickelaluminum pages 4-6, rothfelder2023abriefhistory pages 10-11, fassinatoUnknownyearinfluenceofprocess pages 35-40).
