Question: Context: a lab-built desktop laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) machine for screening aluminium alloys (AlSi10Mg, 6061, 7075, grain-refined variants). 500 W single-mode CW fibre laser at 1070 nm into a SCANLAB basiCube 20 galvo (up to ~1.1 m/s) and a Thorlabs FTH160-1064 f-theta (f = 160 mm), ~96 um 1/e2 spot; 200-350 W; 30 um layers; plates 25-75 mm across heated to 200 C; argon at ~1 atm with a 2.5-3 m/s cross-flow (200 + 40 L/min) over the plate in a ~280 x 190 x 120 mm chamber. The design and what our models found:
1. Coaxial branch behind a short-pass dichroic (Thorlabs DMSP1000) that reflects 1070 nm into the galvo: a shared short-pass 1070 nm blocker; a second dichroic sends 955-990 nm to a monochrome IMX296 global-shutter camera at 7.4 um per pixel (0.94 x 0.71 mm field, ~500 fps cropped); a third splits a two-colour pair, 680-720 nm and 880-920 nm, onto two Si photodiodes (Thorlabs PDA36A2) behind 500 um pinholes (a 1.6 mm field on the plate); an optional InGaAs channel at 1625-1725 nm, or a coaxial SWIR area camera, to see the pool edge. 2. 3. 4. Side high-speed camera (Kron Chronos 2.1-HD) through a 31 mm port in the side wall at 13 deg elevation, 10-20 us exposures, pulsed 850 nm LED light; a steep second camera through the lid at ~60 deg.
5. Acoustic emission for cracking: a broadband sensor coupled to the plate's underside through a waveguide (or the plate's drawbar) down the hollow piston rod, preamp kept at or below 65 C, digitised at >= 2 MS/s.
6. Questions:
(a) Which of these findings conflict with published measurements or practice? ## Critical Review of In-Situ Monitoring Design for a Lab-Built LPBF Machine Processing Aluminium Alloys

### (a) Findings Compared with Published Measurements and Practice

**Melt-pool regime and size.** The operating point of 320 W with a 96 µm spot gives P_L/d_b ≈ 3333 W/mm. Leis et al. mapped the AlSi10Mg process window and placed the optimum at P_L/d_b = 3000–3500 W/mm, corresponding to "key-bowl melting" (KBM)—an intermediate regime between heat-conduction melting (HCM, aspect ratio <0.7) and deep-penetration melting (DPM, aspect ratio >1.4) (leis2021processwindowfor pages 8-10, leis2021processwindowfor pages 1-2). The KBM regime features a shallow vapour depression with an aspect ratio near unity—consistent with the design's claim that tracks remain conduction-shaped up to ~350 W while surface boiling begins from ~200 W. The model's calibrated absorptivity of 0.35 sits between the literature values for aluminium alloy conduction-mode absorptivity (~0.15) and transition-mode absorptivity (~0.425), and is therefore physically reasonable for the shallow-depression/KBM regime (patel2021meltingmodesin pages 63-66). Patel and Vlasea further noted that aluminium alloys have a narrow conduction-to-keyhole transition window because of their high reflectivity, low surface tension at boiling (~0.28 N/m), and volatile Mg content, so the boundary is sensitive to small perturbations in power or focus (patel2021meltingmodesin pages 81-85). The modelled pool dimensions (~324 × 208 µm at 25 °C, ~405 × 260 µm at 200 °C) could not be directly validated against the retrieved literature, which reports aspect ratios and cross-sectional areas rather than absolute widths at this exact parameter set. However, the trend of wider pools at elevated preheat is well established, and the simulated width/depth ratios fall in the expected HCM-to-KBM range.

**Two-colour pyrometer reading.** The design's finding that a 700/900 nm ratio pyrometer reads ~2570 K, nearly independent of speed and preheat, is qualitatively consistent with published pyrometry behaviour in LPBF. Gutknecht et al. demonstrated that the two-colour ratio temperature in LPBF can be misleading: near an overhang where excessive energy was physically present, the ratio temperature paradoxically decreased, and would have driven a feedback controller in the wrong direction (gutknecht2021relevanceofsingle pages 7-11, gutknecht2021relevanceofsingle pages 14-17). The ratio is not simply the vapour-depression wall temperature. Deisenroth et al. at NIST showed that hot metal vapour, condensate, and ejecta can emit, absorb, reflect, and refract radiation before it reaches the detector, with plume contributions biasing a solidification-temperature measurement by approximately 45 °C (16 °C standard uncertainty) even for well-characterized high-purity nickel (deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 3-5, deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 60-61). The design's interpretation that the ratio reads the "depression wall" is therefore an oversimplification. Rather, the signal is a radiance-weighted average over the spatially integrated field of view, dominated by the hottest emitting surface (likely the depression interior), but contaminated by plume emission and selective spectral attenuation. The near-independence from speed and preheat is consistent with the pyrometer being dominated by a hot region whose temperature is set by the boiling point and cavity geometry rather than by bulk thermal conditions—but published evidence cautions that this invariance can break down with changes in plume density, oxide state, or scanning geometry. No published two-colour pyrometry study specifically for aluminium LPBF at these wavelengths was found, so the 2570 K value remains an unvalidated model prediction.

**Silicon camera blindness to Al solidification.** The claim that a 12-bit silicon camera at 850–970 nm cannot see the Al mushy zone (750–925 K, i.e., 477–652 °C) is well supported. Taminger et al. found that a silicon/NIR camera operating at 0.875–1.05 µm was configured for approximately 700–2200 °C and was unsuitable for aluminium's lower temperature range; an InGaAs SWIR camera was specifically required and was calibrated over 300–1000 °C (taminger2016inprocessthermalimaging pages 9-11, taminger2016inprocessthermalimaging pages 11-11, taminger2016inprocessthermalimaging pages 6-9). Boone calibrated a silicon camera with an 850 nm long-pass filter over 600–825 °C (boone2020nearinfraredthermal pages 38-42). These observations confirm that silicon detectors at NIR wavelengths have practical detection thresholds around 600–700 °C for metallic surfaces with low emissivity, placing the Al mushy zone at or below the detection limit. The design's InGaAs channel at 1.55 µm extending sensitivity to ~800 K is therefore a well-motivated addition.

**Spatter reaching cover slides.** The literature confirms that spatter and vapour condensate contaminate optics and powder beds in LPBF (li2022areviewof pages 24-25, liu2023reviewofvisual pages 2-5). However, no published study was found that quantifies the fraction of spatter droplets reaching a cover slide at a specific height, so the design's modelled 1.5–5 % fraction reaching a slide 126 mm above the plate is a machine-specific CFD prediction that cannot be directly validated against literature values. The qualitative finding that cross-flow removes most airborne spatter and condensate is consistent with published studies showing that stronger, more uniform gas flow improves spatter removal and reduces redeposition (li2022areviewof pages 31-33, li2022areviewof pages 29-31).

**Thermophoretic deposition.** The design's prediction that a window 10 K colder than the gas collects 20–120× more condensate via thermophoresis is physically plausible but was not found in the LPBF-specific literature. Thermophoresis is well documented in aerosol science and welding fume contexts, but quantitative LPBF window-contamination studies with controlled temperature differentials were not retrieved.

**Plume attenuation.** The design's modelled 0.05–0.2 % beam attenuation appears significantly lower than published measurements. Brown et al. reviewed laser welding plume attenuation and reported total attenuation values of 11–23 % at atmospheric pressure for Yb-fibre laser welding, decreasing to ~2 % at reduced pressures around 3 kPa (brown2024atmosphereeffectsin pages 10-12). The dominant mechanism for 1 µm lasers is Rayleigh scattering by ultra-fine condensate particles rather than inverse bremsstrahlung absorption (brown2024atmosphereeffectsin pages 5-6). The design's value of 0.05–0.2 % may be reasonable for the aluminium LPBF case at modest power (320 W) with effective cross-flow removing fume, but it is 1–2 orders of magnitude lower than the welding measurements at atmospheric pressure and should be treated as an optimistic lower bound. The discrepancy may partly reflect the difference between welding (continuous deep keyhole, dense plume) and shallow-depression LPBF with active cross-flow, but the design should acknowledge this tension.

### (b) Failure Modes Not Addressed in the Design

**1. Scan-angle-dependent coaxial signal (vignetting and chromatic aberration).** This is the most critical omission. Brandau et al. demonstrated that coaxial monitoring intensity through an F-theta lens dropped by 36 % on solid samples and 51 % on powder-coated samples across the scan field, with only 30.2 % of the reference intensity remaining at 18.5° beam tilt (brandau2022angulardependenceof pages 8-9, brandau2022angulardependenceof pages 13-13, brandau2022angulardependenceof pages 9-11). The Thorlabs FTH160-1064 f-theta lens is designed for 1064 nm laser delivery, not for broadband monitoring. Chromatic aberration shifts the focal position for shorter wavelengths, so the 680–720 nm and 880–920 nm pyrometer bands may have different field-dependent collection efficiencies—corrupting the two-colour ratio spatially across the build plate. Without per-band, per-position calibration, the photodiode pyrometer data will contain systematic spatial errors that could exceed real process variations. A quasi-coaxial approach using reflective optics reduced residual deviation to 1.8 % (brandau2022angulardependenceof pages 8-9).

**2. Ghost reflections and back-reflections.** Caprio et al. identified internal back-reflections in the coaxial imaging chain as a source of bright artifacts (caprio2022externalilluminationenables pages 5-7). The DMSP1000 short-pass dichroic at 45° can produce ghost images from its second surface reflection. At 1070 nm, even 0.1 % leakage of the 500 W process laser through the dichroic yields 0.5 W—enough to saturate or damage the monitoring detectors. The design mentions a "shared short-pass 1070 nm blocker" but does not specify its OD or whether wedged optics are used to prevent retro-reflection. Inserting a beam splitter into the optical path can laterally displace or angle the transmitted laser beam, requiring careful alignment (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 13-13).

**3. Thermal drift of dichroics and filters.** The design does not discuss temperature-induced spectral shifts of the dichroic edge positions. Thin-film interference coatings typically shift ~0.01–0.02 nm/°C, so a 50 °C temperature rise in the scan head could shift a dichroic edge by 0.5–1 nm. While this is small relative to the pyrometer band widths, it could matter for the narrow stop-band avoidance of the 750–875 nm galvo-mirror coating region.

**4. Emissivity changes with oxide formation on aluminium.** Aluminium's emissivity can change dramatically with surface oxidation. The design processes four different alloys (AlSi10Mg, 6061, 7075, grain-refined variants) that will produce different oxide layers on solidified tracks. Published NIST measurements showed nickel emissivity varying from 0.39 (virgin surface) to 0.33 (solidified tracks) (deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 60-61). For aluminium alloys, which form tenacious oxide layers rapidly, the variation is expected to be larger and alloy-dependent. This will affect both the camera signal interpretation and the two-colour pyrometer ratio.

**5. EMI from galvo and servo drives.** The PicoScope 4444 digitizing photodiodes and AE signals at ≥2 MS/s is susceptible to electromagnetic interference from the galvo drivers, which switch currents at high frequencies. The design does not mention shielding, differential sensing, or galvo-synchronous noise characterization. Published monitoring reviews note EMI as a concern for sensitive AE and photodiode measurements in LPBF environments.

**6. AE noise from the recoater and gas loop.** Seleznev et al. found that the inert-gas circulation pump increased AE noise amplitude from approximately 0.2 V to 2 V—a tenfold increase that persisted throughout the build (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 2-3, seleznev2022insitudetection pages 3-5). The design routes the AE signal through a waveguide down a hollow piston rod, which may act as a mechanical antenna for vibrations from the recoater drive, the piston servo, and the gas-flow system. The design specifies keeping the preamp at or below 65 °C but does not address these mechanical noise sources.

**7. Data volume and real-time processing.** At 500 fps cropped, the IMX296 camera generates roughly 0.5–1 GB/min; photodiodes at ≥2 MS/s produce ~16 MB/min per channel; AE at 2 MS/s adds another ~16 MB/min. For multi-hour alloy screening builds, total data volumes can reach hundreds of gigabytes. The design does not describe a data reduction, feature extraction, or storage strategy. Published reviews emphasize that data volume is a major practical barrier to LPBF monitoring implementation (chang2026areviewof pages 10-14).

**8. Calibration drift.** The design does not describe a periodic recalibration protocol. Cover-slide contamination, dichroic thermal drift, and AE coupling degradation will cause progressive signal changes that could be mistaken for process variation. Lane et al. at NIST emphasized the importance of reference measurements and uncertainty quantification for LPBF monitoring (deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 3-5, deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 1-3).

**Discussion of risk ranking rationale:**

Risk 1 (scan-angle dependence) is ranked highest because it affects every coaxial measurement channel continuously and cannot be corrected post-hoc without a spatial calibration. The measured 36–51 % signal variation across the field (brandau2022angulardependenceof pages 13-13) exceeds many process signatures of interest, which are typically 10–30 % changes in photodiode intensity. For the two-colour pyrometer, differential chromatic aberration between 700 nm and 900 nm through the f-theta lens could corrupt the ratio itself, not just its amplitude, making flat-field correction insufficient without per-band calibration.

Risk 2 (pyrometer misinterpretation) is ranked second because the design explicitly interprets the ~2570 K ratio as a physical temperature of the depression wall. Published evidence shows this interpretation can be qualitatively wrong—the ratio moved in the opposite direction to true energy input near an overhang (gutknecht2021relevanceofsingle pages 7-11)—and that plume emission biases even well-calibrated single-wavelength measurements by tens of degrees (deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 60-61). For aluminium, where the plume contains volatile Mg and potentially Na/K with strong atomic emission lines, spectral contamination of the pyrometer bands is a particular concern.

Risk 3 (AE noise) is ranked third because the gas-loop pump noise was measured at 10× the baseline, potentially burying crack signals from hot-cracking-susceptible alloys like 7075 (seleznev2022insitudetection pages 2-3). The waveguide coupling adds unknown transfer-function modifications.

Risk 4 (window contamination) is ranked fourth because it creates a time-dependent systematic error that affects all coaxial channels simultaneously but at different rates depending on wavelength-dependent scattering by condensate particles.

Risk 5 (Si camera blindness) is ranked fifth because while confirmed by literature (taminger2016inprocessthermalimaging pages 9-11, taminger2016inprocessthermalimaging pages 6-9), the design already acknowledges this limitation and proposes the InGaAs channel as mitigation. The risk is that the InGaAs channel may be deferred as "optional," leaving the Si camera as the only imaging sensor, in which case solidification monitoring for the Al alloys would be impossible.

The monitoring design is well-conceived in its multi-modal approach and spectral band selection (avoiding galvo-mirror stop bands and atomic emission lines). However, the critical gap is the absence of a spatial calibration strategy for the coaxial channels through the f-theta lens, which published measurements show can introduce errors exceeding the process signatures of interest. The two-colour pyrometer should be treated as a qualitative regime indicator rather than a thermometer until validated against independent measurements. The AE system requires pre-build noise characterization with all machine subsystems running, and the plume attenuation estimate should be revisited given the order-of-magnitude discrepancy with published welding measurements.
