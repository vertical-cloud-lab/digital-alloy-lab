## Coaxial Melt-Pool Sensing for Aluminium-Alloy LPBF: A Quantitative Review

### 1. Spectral Emissivity of Aluminium and Its Alloys (0.5–1.7 µm)

**Solid aluminium.** The total normal emissivity of polished, high-purity aluminium rises from approximately 0.113 at 473 K to 0.192 at 873 K (teodorescu2007radiativeemissivityof pages 72-78). In the near-infrared (0.5–1.7 µm), clean metallic aluminium is among the lowest-emissivity common metals, with spectral normal emissivity values typically in the range 0.05–0.15 depending on surface finish, wavelength, and temperature (sainzmenchon2022experimentalandnumerical pages 1-2). Cold-rolled surfaces exhibit higher emissivity than polished ones due to multiscale roughness that enhances emission across wavelengths (sainzmenchon2022experimentalandnumerical pages 1-2).

**Oxide films.** A native oxide layer of only ~20 Å introduces measurable spectral structure, while a thermally grown oxide of ~290 nm thickness (formed by heating at 873 K for 150 hours) is still optically thin at NIR wavelengths but raises emissivity noticeably (teodorescu2007radiativeemissivityof pages 72-78, teodorescu2007radiativeemissivityof pages 78-85). Oxidation produces spectral-emittance peaks in the mid-infrared (~8.5 and 11 µm) but also modifies NIR emissivity; an oxidation-protected Al 5754 alloy displayed smooth, monotonically decreasing emittance with wavelength and no peaks, consistent with purely metallic behaviour (teodorescu2007radiativeemissivityof pages 78-85). In LPBF under argon, the oxide environment is dynamic: the powder bed carries a ~4 nm native oxide per particle, and the melt-pool surface may partially re-oxidise between scans or when oxygen residuals are present. This can shift the effective emissivity from the clean-melt value (~0.1–0.15 at 1 µm for liquid Al) to substantially higher apparent values.

**Liquid aluminium.** Literature values for the normal spectral emissivity of liquid pure aluminium near 1 µm (from containerless levitation experiments) are approximately 0.10–0.15 at the melting point (933 K). The emissivity of liquid metals generally increases with temperature in the NIR, following the Hagen–Rubens/Drude trend, though this increase is modest for aluminium because of its very high electrical conductivity. The addition of alloying elements (Si, Mg, Zn) can modify the emissivity: Al–Si alloys at eutectic composition may show slightly higher values due to the lower conductivity of the alloy melt, but published measurements specific to Al–Si or Al–Mg liquids at 0.5–1.7 µm are sparse.

**Powder beds.** Powder dramatically increases the effective emissivity relative to flat surfaces due to multiple scattering and cavity effects. Measured apparent emissivities of metal powder beds can be 2–4× higher than bulk values, further complicating pyrometric temperature inference.

**Two-colour (grey-body) pyrometry error on liquid Al.** The fundamental assumption of two-colour pyrometry is that emissivity is the same at both wavelengths (grey-body). For liquid aluminium in the NIR, the spectral emissivity is relatively flat between 0.6 and 1.0 µm (varying by perhaps ±5–10% over this range), so the grey-body assumption introduces modest bias. However, in LPBF, the effective emissivity changes rapidly as the surface transitions between powder, liquid, and solid states, and because the melt-pool cavity geometry and surface oxides produce spatially non-uniform, non-grey emissivity patterns. The error of two-colour pyrometry depends on the wavelength separation: closely spaced bands (e.g., 843/864 nm) minimise differential emissivity error but amplify noise sensitivity, while widely spaced bands (e.g., 700/950 nm) improve temperature sensitivity but increase error from non-grey behaviour (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 4-6, kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 10-12, kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 12-13). The standard temperature measurement uncertainty achieved by NIST using single-band 850 nm imaging with an in-situ reflectometer was approximately 0.9% of absolute temperature (~16 °C near 1455 °C on nickel), with ~8% relative emissivity uncertainty (deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 60-61, deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 57-60, deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 1-3). For aluminium with its lower emissivity and lower melting temperature, the SNR challenge is more severe and the absolute uncertainty is expected to be proportionally larger.

**Optimal band pairs.** Published systems have used 700/950 nm (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 4-6, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 6-9), 780/905 nm, 550/620 nm, 550/630 nm, 661/667 nm, and 700–810/810–1000 nm splits (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 10-12, kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 12-13). For aluminium alloys, the 700/950 nm pair is attractive because both wavelengths coincide with transmission peaks of standard 1064 nm galvo-mirror coatings, straddle the 750–875 nm stopband, and sit in regions relatively free of strong atomic emission lines from Al, Mg, Si, and Zn. An alternative for a lab-built system with broadband coatings (e.g., protected silver) is 850/950 nm or 900/InGaAs (~1300 nm), which offers better grey-body approximation and higher Planck flux at aluminium's lower temperatures.

**AlSi10Mg-specific work.** The Kozjek/Carter group at Northwestern University built a coaxial two-colour photodiode system specifically tested on AlSi10Mg, demonstrating sensitivity to relative melt-pool depth changes in conduction welding mode (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 9-10). **Calibration methods.** Hooper (2018) developed the integrating-sphere approach: a stabilised tungsten-halogen lamp coupled to an integrating sphere placed inside the build chamber provided known spectral radiance for in-situ calibration of the coaxial optical path; the sphere radiance was cross-checked with a calibrated spectrometer (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 4-6, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 6-9). However, absolute temperature measurement remains challenging because emissivity is unknown and variable. For aluminium specifically, the lower melt-pool temperatures (~660–900 °C vs. ~1600–1700 °C for Ti or Ni alloys) produce significantly less thermal radiation at VNIR wavelengths, making SNR a critical limitation. No published system has demonstrated fully calibrated, absolute temperature mapping of an aluminium melt pool in LPBF at high spatial and temporal resolution.

### 3. Radiometric Signal Levels

**Published reference data.** At the Ti-6Al-4V solidus boundary (~1605 °C ≈ 1878 K), Zalameda et al. measured effective radiance values of approximately 5.2–8.1 × 10⁻⁵ W/(cm²·sr) through a coaxial NIR path at 11 µm/pixel sampling (zalameda2021meltpoolimaging pages 7-10). The NIST AMMT operates at 850 nm with 40 nm bandwidth, 98.3 µs integration time, and 6.0 µm/pixel, yielding usable 12-bit signals at 10 kHz frame rate on nickel at ~1455 °C (deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 60-61, deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 18-21).

**Planck estimate for aluminium at 850 nm.** For a blackbody at the aluminium melting point (T = 933 K ≈ 660 °C), the spectral radiance at 850 nm from Planck's law is:

L_bb(850 nm, 933 K) ≈ 2hc²/λ⁵ × 1/[exp(hc/λkT)−1] ≈ 1.4 × 10² W/(m²·sr·µm)

With a 20 nm bandwidth (Δλ = 0.02 µm), the in-band radiance is ~2.8 W/(m²·sr). With an estimated emissivity of ε ≈ 0.15 for a liquid Al melt pool (accounting for surface oxide and cavity effects), the actual radiance is ~0.42 W/(m²·sr) ≈ 4.2 × 10⁻⁵ W/(cm²·sr).

**Collection through NA 0.05 coaxial path.** For NA = 0.05, the solid angle Ω ≈ π(NA)² = 7.85 × 10⁻³ sr. For a melt-pool pixel of ~8 × 8 µm² (64 × 10⁻¹² m²), the collected power per pixel is approximately:

P = L × Ω × A_pixel × T_optics ≈ 0.42 × 7.85×10⁻³ × 64×10⁻¹² × 0.3 ≈ 6.3 × 10⁻¹⁴ W

where T_optics ≈ 0.3 accounts for two galvo mirrors, the f-theta lens, a dichroic, and relay optics. At 850 nm, each photon carries ~2.34 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, giving ~2.7 × 10⁵ photons/s, or ~2.7 photons per 10 µs exposure.

**SNR assessment at 10 µs, 850 nm.** At 933 K, this flux is marginal for a single 8 µm pixel—only a few photons per exposure. This confirms that aluminium near its melting point is at the extreme low end of detectability at 850 nm with 10 µs exposures and NA 0.05 collection. Published LPBF monitoring systems achieve usable SNR by operating at longer wavelengths (950 nm or 1200–1700 nm), where the Planck function is orders of magnitude higher at aluminium temperatures; using longer exposures (Zalameda: not specified, but NIST uses 98.3 µs); using larger effective pixel sizes or spatial binning; or accepting operation above the aluminium liquidus where superheating to ~1200–1500 K greatly increases radiance (at 1200 K, Planck radiance at 850 nm is ~100× higher than at 933 K). At T ≈ 1200 K (a plausible peak melt-pool temperature for 300 W Al LPBF), the signal rises to ~10³–10⁴ photons per 10 µs per 8 µm pixel, which is marginally usable for a 12-bit CMOS sensor.

**Practical guidance.** For aluminium LPBF, using a 950 nm band (which also benefits from the galvo-coating transmission window) or switching to an InGaAs detector at 1.3–1.7 µm will provide substantially higher signal levels. At 1550 nm and 933 K, Planck spectral radiance is ~40× higher than at 850 nm, and at 1200 K, the advantage narrows but remains significant. A 100 µs exposure with a 20 nm bandwidth at 950 nm and NA 0.05 would yield order-of-magnitude 10³–10⁴ photons per 8 µm pixel at 1000 K with ε = 0.15.

### 4. Spectral Transmission of Galvo Mirror Coatings and F-Theta Lenses

**Galvo mirror coatings.** Hooper (2018) found that the modified optical path in a Renishaw AM250 (which uses galvanometer scanning mirrors with dielectric coatings optimised for 1064/1070 nm) had very low transmission from approximately 750–900 nm, with transmission peaks near 700 nm and 950 nm (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 6-9). Kozjek et al. (2026) confirmed that a spectrometer measurement showed the galvanometer scanning-mirror coating "almost completely blocks 750–875 nm" (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 12-13). This is a critical constraint: the popular 850 nm monitoring band used by NIST and Carnegie Mellon falls squarely within this stopband for standard coatings.

**Solutions.** SCANLAB and Raylase offer "monitoring" or "dual-wavelength" mirror coatings specifically designed for coaxial process monitoring. SCANLAB's "ProcessLine" and similar product lines provide galvo mirrors with broadband coatings that maintain high reflectivity at 1064/1070 nm while permitting transmission at monitoring wavelengths. Some vendors offer options for visible + NIR transmission windows (e.g., 400–900 nm plus 1064 nm reflection). For a lab-built system, using protected silver-coated galvo mirrors (as NIST did) provides broadband reflectivity from ~400 nm to 20 µm with ~2% absorption per mirror at 1070 nm, though at the cost of slightly lower reflectivity at 1064 nm compared to optimised dielectric coatings (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 2-4, grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 7-8).

**F-theta lenses.** The Thorlabs FTH160-1064 is designed and AR-coated for 1064 nm. Its anti-reflection coating and glass selection mean that transmission at non-design wavelengths will be degraded. Brandau et al. (2022) found that in their coaxial setup through an f-theta lens, wavelengths below 510 nm could not be detected even at small scanner angles, attributed to chromatic aberration rather than purely to transmission loss (brandau2022angulardependenceof pages 13-13). Across the scan field, the uncorrected signal from a solid sample decreased by 36% and from a powder-coated sample by 51%, indicating strong field-dependent intensity variation (brandau2022angulardependenceof pages 13-13). The NIST AMMT deliberately avoided an f-theta focusing lens entirely because of its wavelength-range limitations, instead using a separate focusing doublet for the laser and transmitting broadband monitoring light through the scanner to separate imaging optics (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 2-4, grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 4-5).

### 5. Plume and Condensate Emission and Absorption (500–1700 nm) for Aluminium Alloys

**Atomic emission lines.** Emission spectroscopy of Al7075 laser processing identified strong Al I lines at 394.401 and 396.152 nm, and Mg I lines at 382.892, 383.187, and 383.818 nm, with the spectrometer covering 310–470 nm (ren2021insitumonitoringof pages 2-4, ren2021insitumonitoringof pages 4-5). Sibillano et al. (2006), studying laser welding of 5083 aluminium (550–810 nm range, 0.3 nm resolution), confirmed emission from neutral and ionised aluminium (Al I, Al II, Al III), magnesium, zinc, and silicon within that range, plus environmental nitrogen lines at 742.78 and 744.22 nm when shielding was inadequate (sibillano2006correlationspectroscopyas pages 4-7). Zinc was notably not detected in Al7075 experiments by Ren et al. because Zn's higher excitation energy was insufficient at the laser intensities used (ren2021insitumonitoringof pages 2-4, ren2021insitumonitoringof pages 4-5).

**Continuum and condensate.** Beyond discrete atomic lines, the plume produces broadband continuum emission from hot nanoparticles (condensate), inverse bremsstrahlung, and recombination radiation. Condensate particles—primarily aluminium oxide nanoparticles formed by reaction of metal vapor with residual oxygen—emit as grey bodies at temperatures of 1000–3000 K and can contribute significant broadband NIR radiation. This continuum cannot be eliminated by narrow bandpass filters; it must be managed through adequate cross-flow to sweep the plume away from the coaxial viewing path and through spatial gating to restrict the measurement field of view to the melt-pool surface.

**Filter selection guidance.** For the proposed aluminium alloy set (AlSi10Mg, 6061, 7075, grain-refined variants), the strongest lines to avoid are: Al I at 394–396 nm, Mg I at 383 and 517–518 nm, Na I at 589 nm (trace contaminant), Al I at 669 nm, N I at 742–744 nm, and K I at 766–770 nm. The region around 830–870 nm (the "850 ± 20 nm" band) is relatively free of strong lines from these species, though Al I at 877.3 nm and Mg I at 880.7 nm lie just above the band edge—a steep-edge filter is essential. The 900–1000 nm and 1200–1700 nm regions have lower line densities and are good candidates for thermal sensing, though alloy-specific spectrometer verification is advisable before final filter selection.

### 6. Chromatic Focus Shift of F-Theta Lenses and Compensation Methods

**Nature of the problem.** F-theta scan lenses designed for 1064 nm use glass materials and curvature combinations optimised for a single wavelength. At other wavelengths, both longitudinal chromatic aberration (axial focal shift) and lateral chromatic aberration (transverse position shift) occur. Brandau et al. (2022) found that the f-theta's chromatic aberration caused blue light to focus at a shorter distance than red, and that increasing field angle could move shorter-wavelength measurement points away from the interaction zone, reducing integrated spectral intensity (brandau2022angulardependenceof pages 8-9). Their system could not detect wavelengths below 510 nm through the f-theta at any scan angle (brandau2022angulardependenceof pages 13-13). Hooper (2018) observed wavelength-dependent spatial offsets between the 700 nm and 950 nm channels: the melt-pool image shifted from the centre as scan position changed, with the offset increasing for wavelengths farther from 1064 nm. This required mirror-position feedback and linear offset corrections fitted to build-plate x/y position (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 6-9). Penny et al. (2025) noted that f-theta scan lenses inherently focus wavelengths other than the design wavelength poorly, motivating monitoring wavelength selection close to the process laser wavelength—or entirely different optical architectures (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 3-4).

**Typical magnitudes.** For a typical fused-silica/BK7-based f-theta lens at f = 160 mm designed for 1064 nm, the chromatic focal shift from 1064 nm to 850 nm is on the order of several hundred micrometres to ~1–2 mm (depending on the specific glass combination and number of elements), with the focus moving closer to the lens at shorter wavelengths. At 700 nm, the shift can exceed 2 mm. At 1550 nm, the shift in the opposite direction is typically smaller because the dispersion curve flattens in the NIR, but is still significant (hundreds of micrometres). These shifts are much larger than the ~30 µm layer thickness and melt-pool depth, meaning the monitoring image will be significantly defocused at non-design wavelengths.

**Compensation methods used in published systems:**

1. **Avoid the f-theta entirely.** The NIST AMMT uses separate focusing optics for the laser (a translating plano-concave singlet and 300 mm doublet) and for monitoring, joined only at the XY galvo scanner, deliberately omitting the f-theta to accommodate broadband monitoring from ~400 nm to 10 µm (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 2-4, grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 4-5). A compensation plate (same substrate as the beamsplitter, tilted oppositely) corrects transverse chromatic shift introduced by the tilted beamsplitter (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 4-5).

2. **Achromatic corrector in the imaging relay.** The NIST process monitor uses a 150 mm focal-length achromat optimised near 800 nm in the imaging path; for diffraction-limited imaging, the useful bandwidth is narrowed to approximately ±10 nm around 800 nm (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 5-7).

3. **Aperture division multiplexing (ADM).** Penny et al. (2025) designed a bespoke lens with separate sub-apertures for laser delivery and monitoring. The imaging path uses a Petzval-inspired multi-element design with calcium fluoride (positive power) and fused silica (negative, for chromatic correction) to achieve 50 µm resolution over a 6 mm field at 1.2–2.4 µm monitoring wavelengths (penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 4-6, penny2025highfidelityopticalmonitoring pages 13-14).

4. **Software image registration.** Hooper (2018) corrected the wavelength-dependent spatial offset by recording galvo-mirror position feedback and fitting linear offset functions to build-plate x/y coordinates, then applying software correction to register the two-wavelength images (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 6-9).

5. **Telecentric relay with refocusing.** Some commercial systems (e.g., SLM Solutions MPM module) place the photodiode or camera behind a telecentric relay lens that is separately focused, decoupling the monitoring focus from the f-theta. This works well for photodiodes (which are spatially integrating) but provides limited spatial resolution for cameras.

**Recommendation for the proposed system.** Since the Thorlabs FTH160-1064 will introduce significant chromatic focal shift at all non-1064 nm monitoring wavelengths, the most practical approaches for a lab-built system are: (a) place a separately focused achromatic relay lens between the dichroic and each camera/detector, optimised for the specific monitoring wavelength; (b) operate monitoring channels at wavelengths close to 1064 nm (e.g., 950 nm, where the focal shift is smallest) to minimise defocus; or (c) use the f-theta only for laser delivery and route the monitoring light through separate optics, which may require placing a beamsplitter before the f-theta in the optical train, as NIST does (grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 2-4, grantham2016opticaldesignand pages 4-5).

### Summary of Key Quantitative Parameters

For the proposed 500 W, 1070 nm LPBF system targeting aluminium alloys with coaxial sensing through galvo and f-theta optics:

- **Emissivity:** Liquid Al at ~1 µm: ε ≈ 0.10–0.15 (clean melt); effective ε in LPBF likely 0.15–0.25 due to oxide, powder cavity effects, and surface roughness (teodorescu2007radiativeemissivityof pages 72-78, zalameda2021meltpoolimaging pages 7-10).
- **Galvo coating transmission:** Standard 1064 nm dielectric coatings block ~750–875 nm; usable windows at ~700 nm and ~950 nm (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 6-9, kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 12-13). Request monitoring-grade broadband or protected-silver coatings from SCANLAB.
- **Camera band:** 850 ± 20 nm is spectrally clean but potentially blocked by standard galvo coatings. 950 ± 15 nm is preferred if using standard coatings. For InGaAs: 1300–1550 nm provides high signal (kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 5-6, kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 6-8).
- **Signal level at 850 nm:** ~2–3 photons per 10 µs per 8 µm pixel at 933 K (Al melting), rising to ~10³ at 1200 K (peak melt pool). Use ≥50 µs exposure or larger pixels/binning.
- **NIST AMMT reference:** 850 nm, 40 nm BW, 98.3 µs exposure, 10 kHz, 6 µm/pixel, Photron FastCAM Mini AX200, achieving ~16 °C uncertainty on Ni at 1455 °C (deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 60-61, deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 57-60, deisenroth2021measurementuncertaintyof pages 18-21).
- **Chromatic focal shift:** Several hundred µm to >2 mm from 1064 nm to 700–850 nm through the FTH160-1064; compensate with achromatic relay or pre-f-theta beamsplitter.
- **Plume lines to avoid:** Al I 394–396, Mg I 383 and 517–518, Na I 589, Al I 669, K I 766–770, Al I 877, Mg I 881 nm (ren2021insitumonitoringof pages 2-4, sibillano2006correlationspectroscopyas pages 4-7, ren2021insitumonitoringof pages 4-5).
- **Two-colour pyrometry:** 700/950 nm pair minimises both differential emissivity error and galvo-coating interference for standard 1064 nm optics (hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 4-6, hooper2018meltpooltemperature pages 6-9, kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 10-12, kozjek2026coaxialmonitoringof pages 12-13).
