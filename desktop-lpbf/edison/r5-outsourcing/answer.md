# Make-Versus-Buy and Outsourcing Analysis for a Custom Lab-Built LPBF Machine

## 1. Instant-Quote Platform Pricing, Lead Times, and Pitfalls

### 1.1 Comparative Pricing (Quantity 1–3)

Documented comparative quotations from academic case studies provide concrete pricing benchmarks. For a medium-complexity 6061 aluminum FSAE suspension upright, Xometry quoted $305–$490 across four design iterations, Protolabs quoted $572–$624, and Fictiv quoted approximately $318–$326 (liverett2023finiteelementanalysis pages 27-33). The same upright could be produced in the university's own machine shop for approximately $108 in materials (liverett2023finiteelementanalysis pages 33-38). In a separate study comparing quotations for a cast-iron mounting bracket, Xometry quoted NZD 473, Protolabs NZD 605, and RapidDirect NZD 447; for a simpler pneumatic valve, Xometry quoted NZD 155, RapidDirect NZD 136, and Protolabs NZD 349, demonstrating that pricing differences between platforms are part-geometry-dependent and can vary by factors of 2–4× (song2026automatingprocessplanning pages 9-11).

### 1.2 Lead Times

Reported standard shipping lead times were approximately 7 business days for Protolabs, 10 days for Fictiv, and 15 days for Xometry (liverett2023finiteelementanalysis pages 33-38). Outsourced additive-manufacturing and CNC parts generally quote lead times of 2–10 days for production, depending on part size and quantity (islam2025beyond3dprinting pages 14-15). Protolabs emphasizes automated quoting and manufacturing systems capable of delivering "within days" (lualdi2020cloudmanufacturingas pages 47-51), while expedited delivery generally carries a premium (tedaldi2023earlyadoptersof pages 7-9).

### 1.3 Pitfalls for Vacuum-Sealing Parts

Online manufacturing platforms operate through two fundamentally different models: Protolabs manufactures in-house with its own equipment, while Xometry operates as a marketplace connecting customers with external suppliers, scoring them on quality, on-time delivery, and communication (lualdi2020cloudmanufacturingas pages 47-51). Quotation differences between platforms stem primarily from differing assumptions about tolerances and finishing, as well as different pricing algorithms (song2026automatingprocessplanning pages 9-11). Setup and programming costs dominate quantity-1 orders, and suppliers allocate these costs differently between setup charges and per-piece cycle rates (lack2026aunifiedcost pages 6-10).

For vacuum-sealing parts specifically, the following pitfalls should be anticipated:

- **O-ring gland surface finish**: Published O-ring guidance specifies that grooves, scratches, pit marks, concentric marks, and spiral machining scores are fundamentally unacceptable on sealing surfaces. For static sealing (face glands), the recommended limits are Ra ≤ 1.6 µm (Rt ≤ 10.0 µm, Rz ≤ 6.3 µm); for dynamic sealing (piston bores), Ra ≤ 0.4 µm (Rt ≤ 2.5 µm, Rz ≤ 1.6 µm), with spiral-free grinding specified for dynamic surfaces (solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 55-57, solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 113-114). The industry standard roughness average for O-ring gland surfaces has historically been 32 Ra (microinch, approximately Ra 0.8 µm), with relaxed specifications allowing 63 Ra (approximately Ra 1.6 µm) for static seals (lach1993effectoftemperature pages 3-4).
- **Automated DFM limitations**: Online platforms may reject or misquote deep blind pockets, inaccessible O-ring gland geometries, unusual thread specifications, and features requiring specialized fixturing.
- **Material certification and traceability**: Standard online quoting does not automatically include mill certificates, alloy verification, or lot traceability.
- **Passivation and cleanliness**: 316L parts require explicit passivation specification (ASTM A967 or AMS 2700) and vacuum-compatible cleaning; these are not default services.
- **Inspection documentation**: Dimensional inspection reports, flatness measurements, and surface-finish verification must be explicitly requested and specified on drawings.

University in-house fabrication costs approximately 2–5× less than outsourcing for parts that match available equipment capability—a university comparison found in-house costs of approximately $108 (materials only) versus $305–$624 from external platforms for the same aluminum part (liverett2023finiteelementanalysis pages 33-38, liverett2023finiteelementanalysis pages 27-33). Louisiana State University successfully CNC-machined its custom LPBF powder chamber from a solid aluminum block in the university machine shop, with plates, rail components, and related aluminum parts cut or machined on campus (wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 17-22). Assembly challenges included alignment, felt sealing against powder contamination, and wiper-blade rail leveling (wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 17-22).

### 2.1 Honed Build Cylinders: Buy Versus Hone

The 316L build cylinders with 25.4/50.8/76.2 mm H7 bores and 3 mm walls represent the strongest case for outsourcing. The lab lacks a honing machine, and achieving H7 tolerance (e.g., +0/+0.025 mm on a 25.4 mm bore) with boring alone creates high scrap risk. Hydraulic cylinder tubes are commercially honed to Ra 0.2–0.4 µm with H7–H8 bore tolerances as standard products. However, 316L stainless steel honed tubing is less widely stocked than carbon-steel or chrome-plated hydraulic tubing. For the specified small diameters, options include:

1. **Buy pre-honed 316L seamless tubing** from hydraulic-tube distributors and weld to custom-machined flanges. Verify that the specific alloy, wall thickness, ID tolerance, and length are available.
2. **Buy near-size 316L seamless tubing** and send to a honing specialist for finish-bore/hone after flange welding. Welding a 3 mm wall to a flange can distort the bore, so honing should follow welding unless generous post-weld boring stock is left.
3. **DOM (Drawn Over Mandrel) tubing** should not be assumed to meet H7 or vacuum-cleanliness standards without verification; DOM provides better dimensional consistency than standard seamless but is not automatically H7.

Estimated cost for the three-size cylinder set fabricated by a specialist is approximately $2,000–$5,000, versus the unquantifiable risk and time of attempting precision boring on the campus lathe without honing capability.

### 3. Standard Vacuum Chamber Vendors

Standard vacuum chamber vendors (Kurt J. Lesker, MDC, Nor-Cal, Ideal Vacuum, LewVac) predominantly offer cylindrical chamber bodies optimized for mechanical strength under vacuum loading. The 312 × 222 × 140 mm rectangular form factor of this LPBF chamber does not correspond to standard catalog offerings, which are typically round cross-sections in CF, KF, or ISO flanged configurations. Kurt J. Lesker components (flanges, viewports, valves, manipulators, feedthroughs) appear repeatedly in documented academic vacuum builds (goodwin2026aflexiblesmallscale pages 6-7), and the company supplies individual components, sputter sources, and custom fabrication services.

Custom chamber fabrication from these vendors is possible but typically costs $5,000–$15,000+ for a bespoke rectangular box chamber of this size, as the work involves custom drawing review, welding, machining, leak testing, and certification. For comparison, the academic builds documented in literature took different approaches:
- Bidare et al. (2017) used a simple Perspex (acrylic) chamber of 350 × 230 × 220 mm, sealed to a baseplate with a silicon gasket in a machined groove, adequate for inert-gas purging to <0.5% O₂ but not for vacuum service (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5).
- Griggs et al. (2022) at MIT purchased a Parr Instrument Company cylindrical pressure vessel rated to 1,900 psig for their high-pressure LPBF testbed (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 3-7, griggs2022atestbedfor pages 1-3).
- Wen (2021) at LSU used a donated 16 × 16 × 20-inch chamber from the Chemistry Department, sealed with ISO-KF clamp connections and rubber O-ring sealed doors, evacuated below 150 Pa before backfilling with protective gas (wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 22-27).

For this application (inert-gas LPBF, not UHV), the custom-machined 6061 frame is likely the most practical approach, as it integrates the build-platform opening, gas-flow features, instrument ports, and door interface in a single monolithic body. Purchasing a standard catalog chamber would require extensive adaptation and would likely cost more than in-house machining of the designed frame.

### 5. Lessons from Labs That Built Custom LPBF and Inert-Gas Chambers

#### 5.1 Chamber Sealing and Gas Control

Multiple custom LPBF builds demonstrate that achieving and maintaining low oxygen levels is a primary design challenge. The Bidare et al. open-architecture system reduced O₂ below 0.5% using a detachable Perspex chamber sealed with a silicon gasket in a baseplate groove, with inlet and exhaust ports for filling and cross-flow (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5). The LSU system required evacuation below 150 Pa followed by backfilling with protective gas at slight positive pressure, using ISO-KF connections and rubber O-ring sealed doors (wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 22-27). Commercial GE Additive machines achieve O₂ levels below 0.01% in sealed argon-purged chambers (smith2025analysisandeffects pages 21-28).

#### 5.2 Gas Flow Design

Gas-flow design was consistently identified as a major challenge. The Griggs et al. MIT testbed required COMSOL simulations evaluating baffles, guide vanes, funneling, outlet enlargement, and secondary nozzles, with the final nozzle 3D-printed on a Formlabs Form 2 and validated with anemometers confirming flow speeds above 1.4 m/s at 12–15 NLPM (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 3-7). Optimal flow velocity is approximately 0.5–4 m/s across the build plate: below 0.5 m/s, porosity increases; above 4 m/s, powder denudation occurs (kjer2023experimentalanalysisand pages 2-4). Honeycomb flow conditioners with length-to-width ratios of 7–10 are recommended, with inlet geometry critical to preventing backward-facing step vortices (kjer2023experimentalanalysisand pages 2-4). Plenum geometry and final assembly alignment directly affect part quality because nonuniform flow creates stagnant regions and process variability (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 3-7, kjer2023experimentalanalysisand pages 2-4).

#### 5.3 Powder Contamination and Optical Protection

The LSU system experienced a critical failure when fine metal powder accumulated on scan-head mirrors after several printing cycles, causing power loss, coating damage, and thermal lensing. The powder entered through a fine screw used to attach the F-theta lens. The solution was replacing the gate valve with a 2 mm thick glass viewing port, which simplified operation but reduced laser power by approximately 10% (wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 27-33).

#### 5.4 Assembly, Alignment, and Rework

Practical challenges consistently reported across builds include:
- Dimensional adjustment and alignment during assembly rather than relying solely on machined tolerances (wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 17-22).
- Felt or flexible seals between moving platforms and chamber walls to prevent powder contamination of sliding interfaces (wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 17-22).
- Build-platform kinematic constraint systems with adjustable elements to align the powder bed with the laser focal plane and permit repeatable removal and replacement (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 3-7).
- The need for iterative design modification—the Griggs testbed used physical 3D-printed prototypes to validate gas-flow nozzle designs before final fabrication (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 3-7).

#### 5.5 Outsourcing Implications

The documented builds reveal a consistent pattern: labs fabricated most structural and precision-fit components in-house at their university machine shops (aluminum plates, powder chambers, adapter flanges, platform assemblies), while purchasing specialized commercial components (pressure vessels, optical windows, scan heads, lenses, gas sensors, motion stages) and standard vacuum hardware (KF/ISO flanges, viewports, valves) from established vendors such as Kurt J. Lesker (wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 17-22, wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 22-27, griggs2022atestbedfor pages 3-7). This hybrid approach preserves iterative design flexibility for the custom mechanical interfaces while leveraging vendor expertise for standardized precision components.

### 6. Summary Recommendations

For this 48-part LPBF machine build with an estimated 264 shop hours (of which 116 h is CAM programming and 99 h is setups), the following strategy is recommended:

1. **Make in-house** (~25–30 parts): The 6061 through-frame chamber (after waterjet core removal), floor plate, lid, door, heater blocks/plates, and most brackets and adapter rings. These parts exploit the Haas VF-4 with HRT210 and CNC lathe capabilities, allow iterative fitting, and avoid the 3–5× cost multiplier of outsourcing at quantity 1 (liverett2023finiteelementanalysis pages 33-38, liverett2023finiteelementanalysis pages 27-33, wen2021lasersurfacetreatment pages 17-22).

2. **Buy outsourced** (~15–20 parts): Sheet-metal hoppers, laser guard, and plenum panels (via SendCutSend, OSH Cut, or a local sheet-metal shop); 316L build cylinders with honed H7 bores (via a hydraulic-cylinder/honing specialist); Macor standoff rings (via a ceramic machining specialist); and 316L plenum weldments (via a qualified stainless fabrication shop with TIG capability and passivation).

3. **CAM programming time (116 h) is a sunk cost either way**: Whether parts are made in-house or sent out, programming and drawing preparation are required. For outsourced parts, replace CAM time with drawing/specification preparation time, which is comparable but cannot be eliminated.

4. **Invest the saved outsourcing budget in leak testing and inspection**: Specify helium or pressure-decay leak testing for the assembled chamber, build cylinders, and plenums. The cost of one comprehensive leak test is far less than the cost of debugging a leaking system during commissioning.
