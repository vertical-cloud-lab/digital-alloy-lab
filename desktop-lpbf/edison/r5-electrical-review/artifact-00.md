| Standard | Scope/Topic | Key requirements for this machine | What lab/hobby builds typically miss |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NFPA 79 — Electrical Standard for Industrial Machinery** | Machine electrical equipment from the supply connection onward; primary US machine-wiring reference. | Provide a readily accessible, externally operable, lockable main disconnect; branch and control-circuit overcurrent protection; documented conductor ampacity and insulation ratings; terminal identification; protective bonding; separation of power and signal wiring; controlled restart; drawings, nameplate data, and verification tests. Account for available fault current. NFPA 79 is identified alongside IEC 60204-1 and ISO 13849-1 as a core machinery-safety reference. (pqac-00000000, pqac-00000002) | No lockable disconnect; disconnect does not isolate all non-UPS energy; unfused DC branches; household cords/connectors; undocumented wire sizing; misuse of green conductors; missing labels, schematics, bonding tests, and restart controls. |
| **UL 508A — Industrial Control Panels** | Construction and marking practice for industrial control panels; often used by an NRTL or field evaluator even if the university-built machine is not listed. | Use listed or recognized components within their conditions of acceptability; calculate and mark panel SCCR; provide feeder and branch protection, required spacings, suitable enclosure ratings, field-wiring terminals, and wiring rated for voltage, current, and temperature. A UL 508A shop or NRTL field evaluation is the clearest acceptance path. | Assuming the building breaker establishes panel SCCR; overlooking low-SCCR terminal blocks, power supplies, SSRs, contactors, outlets, or docking connectors; mixing circuit classes without segregation; no SCCR or nameplate. |
| **NEC Article 670 — Industrial Machinery** | Installation requirements where the machine connects to university premises wiring. | Size supply conductors and overcurrent protection for the machine load; provide required disconnecting means; include required supply and short-circuit information on the machine nameplate; coordinate machine SCCR with available fault current at the dock. Article 670 does not replace NFPA 79 or hazardous-location rules. | Treating a multipin dock as automatically suitable for disconnecting or load breaking; omitting a local disconnect; not obtaining available fault current; moving the tray between supplies without reevaluating grounding, connector ratings, or SCCR. |
| **IEC 60204-1 — Electrical Equipment of Machines** | International machinery-electrical framework; useful as a design and verification specification where NEC/NFPA govern installation. | Requires supply isolation, electric-shock and overcurrent protection, protective equipotential bonding, conductor identification, emergency functions, technical documentation, and verification such as protective-bonding continuity, insulation-resistance, functional, and applicable voltage tests. It is identified as the machinery electrical-equipment standard paired with ISO 13849-1. (pqac-00000000) | Claiming IEC-style construction without test records; daisy-chained protective earth through removable equipment; relying on painted or anodized joints for bonding; no PE studs or straps across doors, chamber, piston, optical structure, and tray. |
| **ISO 13849-1 — Safety-Related Parts of Control Systems** | Risk-based design and validation of safety functions using required Performance Level, architecture, diagnostic coverage, reliability, and common-cause controls. | Perform an ISO 12100 or ANSI B11.0 risk assessment and assign a PLr to each safety function. For laser prevention, use monitored, fault-tolerant channels from guards and E-stops through the safety controller to independent final elements that remove emission permission; include manual reset, external-device monitoring, fault reaction, and documented validation. Category 3 is often used for PL d, but PLr must come from risk assessment. (pqac-00000000, pqac-00000002) | Calling a safety PLC “PL d” without evaluating the complete input–logic–output chain; both channels sharing a vulnerable connector, relay, cable, or supply; relying on PC software, RTC6 commands, analog zero, or one laser contact; no validation or proof testing. |
| **21 CFR 1040.10 — Laser Products** | FDA/CDRH laser-product performance requirements; particularly relevant if the university manufactures, transfers, distributes, or places the completed product into commerce. | A Class 4 product generally needs protective housing, interlocks, key control, emission indication, a beam attenuator such as a shutter, warning labels, aperture information, and specified control behavior. Determine reporting, certification, accession, and exemption status with the LSO and counsel. A compliant IPG source does not make the assembled LPBF machine compliant. Lab literature recognizes YLR sources as Class 4 but also illustrates reliance on simple interlocks. (pqac-00000034, pqac-00000035, pqac-00000038) | Treating eyewear as the primary safeguard; no safety-rated shutter or monitored final elements; no labels or product file; guard switch wired only to software; uncontrolled interlock defeat for alignment; confusing product rules with workplace controls. |
| **ANSI Z136.1 — Safe Use of Lasers** | Workplace laser-safety program managed by the Laser Safety Officer; complements FDA product requirements. | Have the LSO evaluate hazards and the nominal hazard zone; enclose the beam so routine operation is effectively Class 1; control keys; interlock access points; provide beam stops, suitable viewing windows, warning signs, emission indicators, alignment procedures, wavelength/OD-appropriate eyewear, training, and audits. At 1070 nm the beam is invisible, and aluminium can create hazardous specular reflections. | “Glasses-worn” switches, as reported on one custom printer, instead of engineered containment; uncertified windows; no reflection analysis; no controlled alignment mode; keys left installed; camera or viewport leakage not assessed. (pqac-00000035, pqac-00000036) |
| **NFPA 484 — Combustible Metals** | Fire and explosion hazards from aluminium and other combustible metal powders, including additive manufacturing and powder handling. | Conduct a documented dust-hazard analysis using actual powder and process data; particle size alone is insufficient. Testing can include ASTM E1226/E1515 and MIE, MEC, Kst, Pmax, ignition temperature, LOC, resistivity, and chargeability. Prevent fugitive dust, bond conductive handling equipment, control static, use compatible collection and cleaning methods, and document hazardous locations. Combustible metal dust is treated as Class II, Group E. (pqac-00000003, pqac-00000010, pqac-00000011, pqac-00000015) | Assuming argon removes hazards during loading, leakage, venting, cleaning, and filter service; no DHA; ordinary shop vacuum; inappropriate water response; nonconductive hoses or bins; unbonded powder containers; relays, motors, fans, connectors, or heaters exposed to dust. |
| **NEC Articles 500/502 and NFPA 499 — Class II, Group E** | Electrical classification and installation for conductive combustible metal dust; Article 506 may apply where the adopted code and AHJ accept Zones. | Define classified boundaries and Division from normal and abnormal release scenarios. Equipment in a classified area must be identified for Class II, Group E and the applicable Division and temperature limit, or use another accepted protection method such as intrinsic safety for eligible instrumentation. Preserve dusttight integrity and inspect for ingress. Class II metal-dust suitability is not equivalent to ordinary IP65, NEMA 12, or gas explosion-proof approval. (pqac-00000010, pqac-00000011, pqac-00000013, pqac-00000014) | Declaring the room unclassified without documenting release sources; assuming IP65 or gas-rated equipment is Group E suitable; ordinary blower motors, coils, sensors, lights, or filter switches in classified zones; assuming every sensor must be intrinsically safe instead of selecting an accepted protection method for the complete loop. |
| **ISO 14119 — Guard Interlocking Devices** | Selection and design of door and access-panel interlocks, including guard locking and defeat resistance. | Select coded or defeat-resistant devices; protect actuators from bypass; monitor safety channels; use guard locking where laser, heat, pressure, or motion hazards persist; choose power-to-lock or power-to-unlock behavior from risk analysis; provide safe release after power loss; prevent automatic restart; validate with ISO 13849-1. ISO 14119 is identified with ISO 13850 and IEC 62061 in the safeguarding framework. (pqac-00000000) | One reed switch; exposed spare actuator; guard lock powered only from a UPS without emergency-release analysis; immediate unlocking while residual hazards remain; deriving safety status from a standard PLC bit. |
| **ISO 13850 — Emergency-Stop Function** | Emergency-stop principles; an E-stop is a complementary measure, not a substitute for guards or process controls. | Provide accessible red mushroom actuators on yellow backgrounds with direct-opening, latching contacts; require deliberate reset; ensure reset cannot restart the machine; select stop category by risk assessment; stop hazardous emission and motion through the safety system. Address stored DC-bus energy, UPS-backed loads, heater contactors, vacuum/argon states, and robot coordination. ISO 13850 is cited with NFPA 79 and ISO 13849-1. (pqac-00000000) | E-stop only pauses software; UPS keeps laser enable or drives active; reset causes restart; heater SSR remains energized; insufficient actuator coverage; robot and LPBF machine have unrelated emergency-stop domains without a validated interface. |
