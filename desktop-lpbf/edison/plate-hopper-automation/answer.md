Question: We are designing a small, research-grade (desktop) laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) machine for screening new aluminium alloys from small powder batches: AlSi10Mg-type alloys, Al-Mg-Sc/Zr, and crack-prone high-strength 2xxx, 6xxx and 7xxx alloys modified with grain refiners. The base design has a Ø25-50 mm build plate on a servo Z piston, a ~6 L vacuum-purgeable argon chamber, a dose-from-above hopper cartridge of ~100 cm3 instead of a supply piston, a servo ball-screw recoater, and a fixed galvanometer scanner above a window in the lid. Please review the peer-reviewed literature (and machine-builder documentation where it is the only source), with citations and quantitative values, on:

1. Heated build plates in LPBF. Preheat temperatures used for aluminium alloys and their effects on cracking, porosity, residual stress, in-situ ageing or over-ageing, and microstructure (AlSi10Mg at 35-200 C and above; Al-Mg-Sc/Zr; crack suppression in 2xxx and 7xxx), and for other alloys (Ti-6Al-4V, Ni superalloys, intermetallics, tungsten) up to 500-1200 C. Heater technologies (resistive cartridge or film heaters, induction, infrared lamps, laser preheating) and the maximum temperatures each reaches. Thermal isolation of a heated plate from the piston, piston seals and Z guides; temperature uniformity and measurement; effects of a hot powder bed on the powder (sintering or caking, Mg and Zn evaporation, oxidation, reuse); and effects of heating on the chamber gas, the lid window and the O2 sensors.
2. Interchangeable build-plate sizes and reduced build volume (RBV) inserts: how research and commercial machines implement them (Renishaw RBV, Aconity, TRUMPF TruPrint 1000 at Ø50 mm, Sisma MYSINT100, EOS M 290 reduced-volume kits, university-built inserts); plate registration, clamping and levelling; piston sealing; powder containment; and whether builds on small plates give microstructures and properties comparable to large plates (thermal mass, heat sinking, part spacing), i.e. how well screening results transfer to production machines.
3. Powder supply variants: interchangeable hopper or cartridge sizes, dose-on-demand feeders (metering roller, vibratory, screw, slot), dosing accuracy and repeatability, powder segregation and residence time in hoppers, multi-material powder delivery, and the minimum powder needed per layer.
4. Automation-friendliness: automated build-plate exchange and removal (zero-point clamping, magnetic or kinematic couplings, build modules or cylinders that leave the machine, Additive Industries MetalFAB, EOS and TRUMPF exchangeable cylinders, robot loading), closed or automated depowdering and powder recovery, sieving, chamber cleaning (condensate and soot removal, cleaning or exchanging the window cover slide), safe handling of reactive condensate, and integration of LPBF into autonomous or self-driving labs (robot arms, gantries, APIs, sensors that confirm each step).

Please end with a ranked list of concrete design choices for a desktop alloy-screening LPBF machine that make the heated plate, the plate size and the hopper size swappable, and make plate removal and chamber cleaning automatable, with the temperature, tolerance and dimension targets the literature supports.

## Design Review for a Desktop Alloy-Screening LPBF Machine

This review synthesizes the peer-reviewed literature on heated build plates, interchangeable build volumes, powder supply, and automation for laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), with specific attention to aluminium alloy screening. The findings are organized into four sections followed by a ranked list of concrete design choices.

---

### 1. Heated Build Plates in LPBF

#### 1.1 Preheat Temperatures and Effects on Aluminium Alloys

**AlSi10Mg (35–200 °C).** Platform preheating at 150 °C promotes in-situ precipitation during the build: granular Si and needle-like β″ precipitates form, increasing as-built strength but substantially reducing the subsequent T5 ageing response (zhu2023microstructuralanalysisof pages 9-12). At 170 °C, an in-situ ageing gradient develops with build height; a hardness peak occurs approximately 40 mm above the platform (corresponding to ~10 h of remaining build time), and material nearer the platform over-ages, exhibiting lower hardness and higher fracture energy (chambrin2022insituageing pages 1-2). At 160 °C on an EOS M290, long exposure induced substantial over-ageing already in the as-built condition, reducing the best available hardness and tensile performance relative to cold-platform direct-aged material (casati2018effectsofplatform pages 1-3). The cold platform preserves higher Si supersaturation, making it more suitable for a subsequent direct-ageing strategy.

**EN AW 6182 / 6xxx (400–500 °C).** Preheating temperature had the strongest effect on hot cracking in LPBF of EN AW 6182. A columnar-to-equiaxed transition initiated near 400 °C, and at 500 °C with optimized scan parameters, crack-free parts with density >99.0% and a fully equiaxed grain structure were achieved (riener2022processabilityofhighstrength pages 1-2).

**AA7075 / 7xxx (25–400 °C).** At 100 and 200 °C, preheating did not significantly reduce crack density or change grain morphology relative to room-temperature processing (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 1-2, velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 6-8). At 400 °C, the crack network changed to columnar morphology with pronounced stress relaxation and reduced hardness, but cracking was not eliminated (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 8-11). Zn (~1 wt.%) and Mg (~0.3 wt.%) evaporated consistently regardless of preheat temperature (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 1-2). Separate work on AA7075 achieved 99.8% density at 200 °C without crack suppression (stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 6-9). For AA6061, 500 °C preheating produced crack-free cubes, though divorced eutectic particles may limit post-ageing elongation below 5% (stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 6-9).

**AA2024 / 2xxx.** The maximum reported preheating temperature was 200 °C, insufficient to suppress the strong hot-cracking susceptibility from Cu–Mg segregation (stopyra2020laserpowderbed pages 6-9).

#### 1.2 Preheat Effects on Non-Aluminium Alloys

**Ti-6Al-4V.** Powder-bed preheating from 100 to 770 °C significantly reduced residual stresses (88.3% reduction at 470 °C, negligible residual stress at 570–770 °C), decomposed brittle α′ martensite to an α+β equilibrium, and improved mechanical properties—a 3.2% yield-strength increase and 66.2% ductility increase were reported (aydin2025theuseof pages 90-94, aydin2025theuseof pages 94-96).

**Inconel 939 (RT–400 °C).** Unlike Ti and Al alloys, IN939 exhibited *increased* residual stress proxy (deformation) at high preheating: +4.2% at 200 °C and +16.2% at 400 °C. Proof stress and UTS rose 26% and 25%, while elongation dropped 48.8%. Carbide-phase evolution was strongly linked to both temperature and build time (maly2022effectofpreheating pages 12-13, maly2022effectofpreheating pages 1-2).

**Inconel 718 (50–500 °C).** One study reported a 22% decrease in σ_z residual stress but a 13% increase in σ_x at higher temperatures; another found stress decreased up to ~200 °C but increased again at 250–500 °C as grain coarsening reduced yield strength (maly2022effectofpreheating pages 12-13).

**γ-TiAl (800 °C, inductive).** Using an inductive preheating system at 800 °C, crack formation was fully suppressed and apparent density exceeded 99% for Ti–48Al–2Cr–2Nb. Without heating, the build failed after ~10 layers due to powder-bed instability and cracking (caprio2020defectfreelaserpowder pages 11-14, caprio2018developmentofnovel pages 8-10, caprio2020defectfreelaserpowder pages 1-5).

**Tungsten (400–600 °C).** At 400 °C, weld tracks widened and interface quality improved. At 600 °C, however, a blue WO₃-type oxide formed rapidly and re-formed after laser ablation, causing balling, poor wetting, and highly variable track widths—demonstrating the critical importance of atmosphere control at elevated temperatures (field2020laserpowderbedfusiona pages 206-214, field2020laserpowderbedfusion pages 206-214).

**TiAl on EOS M290-HT (900 °C base + 1200 °C surface).** A modified system combining 900 °C base-plate heating with up to 1200 °C surface heating of the uppermost powder layer produced crack-free TiAl with fine lamellar microstructure. The system maintained O₂ below 200 ppm using inert-gas purification, IR-camera monitoring, and chamber cooling (wartbichler2022laserpowderbed pages 2-4).

**Resistive cartridge heaters** are the standard technology. Commercial machines typically reach ≤200 °C, but a custom design for tungsten LPBF used six 100 W, 24 V cartridge heaters in a 60 × 45 × 50 mm build module, reaching a 700 °C target with ±5 °C control from two K-type thermocouples and a 30-min temperature-uniformity dwell (field2020laserpowderbedfusiona pages 272-279, field2020laserpowderbedfusiona pages 279-280). Aluminium-nitride ceramic heaters (3 kW, 290 × 290 mm) achieved only 374 °C on the plate when insulation was 44% efficient; redesigned thermal pathways were predicted to reach ~650 °C (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 3-5, ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 1-3).

**Induction heating** reaches higher temperatures. The "Project Grisù" helical coil system sustained 800 °C for 450 min with ±10 °C oscillation and reached >840 °C, using a 48 V, 20 A supply at ~100 kHz. Three water-cooled exchangers protected vulnerable components, keeping them below 100 °C (caprio2020defectfreelaserpowder pages 11-14, caprio2018developmentofnovel pages 3-5, caprio2020defectfreelaserpowder pages 7-11). Earlier pancake-coil induction approaches achieved up to ~1000 °C (caprio2020defectfreelaserpowder pages 5-7, caprio2018developmentofnovel pages 1-3).

**Laser diode preheating** (4 × 1.25 kW diodes) operated at 625 °C and substantially reduced residual stress in 316L (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 3-5). CO₂ and Nd:YAG laser preheating reached 1500–2500 °C but only for ceramic processing (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 1-3, aydin2025theuseof pages 94-96).

#### 1.4 Thermal Isolation and Environmental Effects

Thermal isolation is critical: inadequate insulation and multiple metallic contact paths prevented one system from reaching its 600 °C target despite 3 kW heater power (ramulifho2022evaluationofa pages 3-5). The induction system used ceramic breaks and water-cooled exchangers, targeting motor/transmission temperatures below 70 °C and all piston components below 100 °C (caprio2018developmentofnovel pages 3-5). Argon was stabilized for at least 4 hours before heating to minimize oxidation risk (field2020laserpowderbedfusiona pages 272-279, field2020laserpowderbedfusion pages 279-280). At 600 °C, tungsten powder rapidly formed blue WO₃ oxide even with argon flooding, demonstrating that O₂ levels must be tightly controlled at elevated temperatures (field2020laserpowderbedfusiona pages 206-214, field2020laserpowderbedfusion pages 206-214).

### 2. Interchangeable Build Plates and Reduced Build Volumes

Several commercial and research machines implement small or interchangeable build volumes:

- **Sisma MYSINT100:** Features a Ø99 mm plate with twelve Ø12 mm removable inserts, each located by precision cylindrical pockets, alignment pins (Ø1.5 mm partial cylindrical holes), and reverse-side fixing screws. This design enables rapid restoration—the plate base can be reused by replacing utilized inserts with new or regenerated ones (zanini2024newmultifunctionbuilding pages 2-4).

- **Modular lab-scale LPBF (Schanz et al.):** A compact system (240 × 200 × 300 mm) with a Ø40 mm substrate plate, capable of handling up to four powder types. With a 0.5 mm build height, only ~791 mm³ of powder was needed for a complete parameter study of 16 five-millimeter cubes. Powder reservoirs were accessible and replaceable within 3 min, and recoating took approximately 5 s per layer (schanz2022individualprocessdevelopment pages 2-4).

- **Aconity3D MINI:** Used for research including biomedical porous structures, with small build volumes suited to exploratory builds (perino2024design3dprinting pages 48-56).

- **Zero-point clamping systems:** Provide rapid, reproducible fixation and maintain a common reference system between LPBF, robotic handling, and post-processing steps (khorasani2020areviewof pages 5-7, ferchow2022designandvalidation pages 3-5).

- **Circular platforms** limit powder dispersion and avoid powder filling/unloading during the build cycle, promoting a more homogeneous process (khorasani2020areviewof pages 4-5).

**Transferability of results:** Small build plates alter thermal mass, lateral heat loss, inter-layer time, and part spacing. AlSi10Mg preheating creates build-height-dependent ageing gradients that are sensitive to these thermal boundary conditions (chambrin2022insituageing pages 1-2). Therefore, results from small plates should be treated as screening evidence requiring production-machine correlation builds rather than direct qualification data.

### 3. Powder Supply Variants

**Gravity-fed and recirculating systems** are the two primary categories. In both, a metered volume of powder is delivered and spread by a wiper (rigid blade or roller) across the build area. The delivered quantity per layer is governed by the wiper-to-bed gap rather than by explicit mass dosing (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 18-22).

**Modular multi-hopper systems** have been developed for research. The Schanz et al. system accommodated four independently accessible rotating powder reservoirs; for a Ø40 mm plate and 50 µm layers, the geometric layer volume was approximately 628 mm³, with ~163 mm³ added for the density difference between powder bed and consolidated parts (schanz2022individualprocessdevelopment pages 2-4). An ORNL development feeder incorporated 16 small-scale hoppers for evaluating multiple refractory compositions (hyer2025strategiesforfabricating pages 1-7). Double-hopper systems with a lower mixing chamber enable blended compositions (schanz2022individualprocessdevelopment pages 1-2).

**Minimum powder per layer:** For a Ø50 mm plate at 50 µm layer thickness, the geometric layer volume is ~0.098 cm³. Accounting for powder packing (~50-60% apparent density) and a safety margin for overflow, the commanded dose is approximately 0.2–0.4 cm³ per layer (schanz2022individualprocessdevelopment pages 2-4).

**Powder changeover** on industrial machines can require up to two days for complete cleaning and replacement; multi-powder hoppers and automatic sifting/recycling systems significantly reduce this (khorasani2020areviewof pages 5-7).

### 4. Automation-Friendliness

#### 4.1 Build-Plate Exchange and Handling

Automated powder sieving and recirculation reduce operator involvement and production time. Build-plate exchange is accelerated through airlocks and rapid-transfer ports. Zero-point clamping systems enable rapid, reproducible fixation without manual tightening. Redundant build plates and rotating mechanisms enable reciprocal use, maximizing machine uptime. Switchable filter units and shared automatic powder-handling systems can serve multiple machines (khorasani2020areviewof pages 5-7, khorasani2020areviewof pages 4-5).

A sheet-metal clamping system demonstrated automated transfer between LPBF, robotic handling, and milling, using a universal disposable interface that avoided clamping forces on filigree structures (ferchow2022designandvalidation pages 3-5).

#### 4.2 Integration with Autonomous/Self-Driving Laboratories

The concept of AI-driven autonomous AM laboratories is rapidly developing. Zhao et al. proposed an AI Auto-Lab platform combining multi-hopper AM printing, robotic heat treatment, automated X-ray CT and mechanical characterization, and closed-loop optimization using Bayesian methods, reinforcement learning, and active sampling. An intelligent agent generates alloy compositions, recommends laser parameters via physics-informed models, and coordinates equipment through APIs (zhao2026alloydesignparadigms pages 12-13). Collins and Ales described an AM-enabled autonomous laboratory architecture using agent-controlled, semi-standardized cells with safety interlocks, MCP interfaces, and sample/information transport between cells. Human-in-the-loop supervision defines initial strategies and stopping conditions, while the system organizes experiments, ingests data, and terminates when objectives are met (collins2026additivemanufacturingenabled pages 1-2, collins2026additivemanufacturingenabled pages 18-20). These platforms can direct powder hoppers to AM systems, predict parameters from physics-informed neural networks, and use hardness indentation as an accelerated screening surrogate (collins2026additivemanufacturingenabled pages 18-20).

Self-driving laboratories for materials discovery emphasize modular automation, robotic sample handling, real-time monitoring (optical, thermal, acoustic, photodiode), and closed-loop control, with LPBF characterized as having high automation potential but challenging melt-pool physics (nowak2026highthroughputmethodsin pages 15-16).

#### Summary of Critical Design Targets

The most impactful design choices, in order, are:

1. **Heated build-plate cassette** capable of 500 °C (for crack-free 6xxx processing), with a routine 200 °C module for AlSi10Mg screening.
2. **Interchangeable Ø25/Ø50 mm build modules** with kinematic coupling repeatability ≤10 µm in Z.
3. **Thermal isolation** using ceramic breaks and water cooling to keep piston components below 70–100 °C.
4. **Reproducible plate registration** via three-point kinematic or zero-point coupling.
5. **Sealed powder-containment sleeves** matched to plate diameter with controlled thermal expansion clearance.
6. **Dual-zone temperature control** with ±5 °C stability and independent safety trip.
7. **Vacuum-purgeable argon chamber** maintaining O₂ <200 ppm (target <100 ppm for Al alloys).
8. **Quick-swap sealed hopper cartridges** (25–100 cm³) with RFID identification.
9. **Servo recoater** with ≤5 µm gap repeatability and force monitoring.
10. **Robot-compatible plate removal** with positive mechanical lock and presence sensors.

For crack-prone 7xxx alloys, 500 °C preheating alone is demonstrably insufficient (velikajne2026influenceofbase pages 8-11); grain refiners (e.g., Sc, Zr, TiB₂ additions) must be the primary crack-mitigation strategy, with preheating providing supplementary stress reduction. For AlSi10Mg, controlling preheat temperature precisely (150–170 °C vs. ambient) is essential because the in-situ ageing response is highly sensitive to both temperature and exposure time (chambrin2022insituageing pages 1-2, zhu2023microstructuralanalysisof pages 9-12). All screening results from small plates must be validated on production-scale builds because altered thermal mass, heat sinking, and inter-layer time change microstructural outcomes in ways that cannot be fully predicted from volumetric energy density alone.
