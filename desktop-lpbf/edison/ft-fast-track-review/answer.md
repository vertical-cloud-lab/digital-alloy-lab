## Design Simplifications for a Rapid-Build Aluminium LPBF Screening Machine: A Literature Review

This report synthesises evidence from journal papers, theses, conference proceedings, and instrument descriptions to address seven questions on designing a minimal laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) machine for aluminium alloy screening, followed by a ranked list of time-saving simplifications.

**Heriot-Watt (Bidare et al., 2017):** This is arguably the most relevant precedent for a compact, rapidly built multi-layer LPBF machine. The system used a gravity-fed V-shaped powder reservoir travelling with a reciprocating spreader block, driven by stepper motors and a lead screw. The build plate (80 × 40 mm coupon) descended like a piston within the surface plate, sealed with a Teflon O-ring. A 3 mm silicone cord was ultimately chosen for the spreading element because rigid tungsten carbide knife-edge and cylindrical spreaders jammed from particle accumulation (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3). The chamber was a detachable, sealed 5 mm clear Perspex box (350 × 230 × 220 mm) with a UV-grade fused-silica laser window. Argon purging reduced O₂ below 0.5% (5000 ppm) as measured by a Grove O₂ sensor; the later high-pressure version reached <0.1% (1000 ppm) after approximately 10 minutes of argon flow (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3, bidare2018laserpowderbed pages 1-2). The laser was an SPI 400 W CW single-mode fibre laser, scanned with a Raylase MS-II-14 and 163 mm f-theta lens. Control used a PC, LabVIEW, Arduino Uno, and a SCAPS USC-2 scanner card with SAMlight software. The system produced >99% dense 5 × 5 × 5 mm cubes (~125 layers, ~1.5 h per component, dominated by the 10 mm/s recoater speed) (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 7-9, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 5-7). No construction timeline was explicitly reported, but the compact, modular, Perspex-box design implies rapid fabrication relative to a machined vacuum chamber.

**DTU OpenAM:** DTU's open-architecture system used an SPI-250C 250 W single-mode laser with a Thorlabs GVS012 galvanometer (early version) or a Sunny Galvo S9320D digital scanner (later version). The system controller is a Teensy 3.6 (180 MHz ARM Cortex-M4) driving the XY2-100 protocol at 2 MHz via DMA and timer interrupts, with Bresenham line interpolation for trajectory generation and synchronized 100 kHz laser modulation (hansen2019additivemanufacturingof pages 10-13, andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12, andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 1-3, aagaard2023legacylaserpowder pages 2-3). The controller was installed in three DTU open-architecture systems, including polymer SLS and bespoke metal-AM machines (aagaard2023legacylaserpowder pages 2-3). Detailed build area, chamber dimensions, O₂ levels, and construction timeline were not reported in the available sources.

**MIT Hart Group (Griggs et al., 2022):** The High-Pressure Laser Melting (HPLM) testbed used a custom cylindrical Parr pressure vessel (2.5 in diameter, 6.5 in bore) with a sapphire pressure window and a sacrificial quartz slide. Powder was manually spread as a single ~76 μm layer in a shim-defined recess using a machinist's edge (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 3-7, griggs2022atestbedfor pages 13-17, griggs2022atestbedfor pages 1-3). The laser was an SPI fibre laser (30–525 W, 1075–1080 nm) with a custom Novanta galvanometer scanner and Thorlabs f-theta lens. Gas flow used UHP argon at 12–15 NLPM baseline with a 3D-printed internal gas-knife nozzle producing >1.4 m/s across the ~10 × 60 mm print area (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 3-7, griggs2022atestbedfor pages 7-9). The optics enclosure was continuously nitrogen-purged at 1 LPM with thermistors for automatic laser shutdown (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 7-9). This was a single-layer/single-track testbed; the authors noted that multi-layer operation would require a larger chamber with a vertical stage and recoater (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 13-17).

**NIST AMMT:** The Additive Manufacturing Metrology Testbed used a modular, reconfigurable architecture with full user control of scan strategy, laser power, speed, and layer thickness. The control system was LabVIEW-based with an FPGA driving the XY2-100 protocol to galvanometer mirrors and synchronising laser power via the protocol's z-axis at 100 kHz. An independent Allen Bradley PLC managed safety interlocks (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 1-3, lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 11-13). The chamber was initially evacuated to 0.1 Pa (10⁻³ torr), then backfilled with argon or nitrogen, with the purge cycle repeated until O₂ fell below a configurable setpoint (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 11-13). After builds, dry air was introduced and the chamber was safe to open only above 19.5% O₂ (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 11-13).

**Synchrotron Replicators:** The ESRF miniature system (Lhuissier et al., 2022) used an SPI QUBE 500 W laser with a SCANLAB HSC II 14 scan head, an 8 mm diameter substrate on a piston (±5 μm accuracy), and a rotating scraper for automated powder coating. Its sealed polycarbonate tube was purged through three vacuum/argon cycles to <100 ppm O₂ (lhuissier2022miniaturelaserpowder pages 3-4, lhuissier2022miniaturelaserpowder pages 4-5, lhuissier2022miniaturelaserpowder pages 1-3). The SSRL system (Calta et al., 2018) used a 500 W fibre laser, a spherical-octagonal vacuum chamber (168.9 mm ID), and Be X-ray windows, described as a portable Class 1 enclosed system (calta2018aninstrumentfor pages 3-4, calta2018aninstrumentfor pages 1-3). Diamond Light Source's LAMPR was a compact ~15 kg portable device with BN X-ray windows, capable of operation at 10⁻³ torr vacuum or controlled gas flow (leung2018xrayimagingof pages 69-73). The PETRA III system (Uhlmann & Krohmer, 2020) used a modified AconityMINI with a 70 × 3 mm powder bed between glassy-carbon plates and an automatic gravity-funnel recoater with an elastomeric lip (uhlmann2020alaserpowder pages 6-8, uhlmann2020alaserpowder pages 4-6).

### 2. Fixed Powder Bed Designs and Rectangular Piston Wells

Several synchrotron replicators effectively use a fixed-bed concept. The ESRF system uses a small piston that lowers for recoating and rises for XCT scanning; only approximately 0.5 mm at the periphery of the 8 mm substrate was disturbed during piston movement over 30 layers (lhuissier2022miniaturelaserpowder pages 4-5). The PETRA III system had a 70 × 3 mm powder bed bounded by 1 mm glassy-carbon plates, using a gravity funnel and elastomer lip for recoating (uhlmann2020alaserpowder pages 6-8).

For rectangular piston wells with PTFE/felt seals in metal LPBF, the EOS build-volume reduction kit (Norouzi-Inallu et al., 2023) is the closest reported precedent. It used a circular piston with a Teflon sealing ring inside a modified EOSINT M-series machine. Reported practical problems included: (i) piston rotation and resultant scan-pattern drift due to inadequate mounting, (ii) excessive lateral powder spreading beyond the reduction-kit frame causing powder waste, and (iii) altered thermal behaviour and processing windows when using the small-volume insert compared to the full machine (norouziinallu2023developmentofa pages 3-6, norouziinallu2023developmentofa pages 9-11, norouziinallu2023developmentofa pages 6-9). Bidare's system similarly used a Teflon O-ring to prevent powder leakage around a rectangular support assembly (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3). No specific published precedent was found for rectangular piston wells with felt or PTFE wipers used specifically for fine aluminium powder at 200 °C in LPBF. Standard PTFE approaches its practical continuous-temperature limit near 200 °C, suggesting that thermal creep, reduced sealing force, and powder leakage could become significant concerns at that temperature.

### 3. Flow Purging Only (No Vacuum) in Small Chambers for Aluminium LPBF

**Oxygen levels and purge times:** The Bidare system achieved <0.5% (5000 ppm) O₂ in its standard Perspex chamber configuration, and its reinforced high-pressure version reached <0.1% (1000 ppm) after approximately 10 minutes of argon purging through a non-return inlet valve (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3, bidare2018laserpowderbed pages 1-2). For a small chamber of a few litres, the theoretical minimum for displacement purging is approximately 5 volume changes to reach ~1000 ppm and ~7 volume changes to reach ~100 ppm from ambient air (21% O₂), assuming perfect plug flow. In practice, dead volumes, leaks, and mixing mean that more flow or longer times are needed. The ESRF system, which uses three vacuum-and-backfill cycles, achieves <100 ppm reliably (lhuissier2022miniaturelaserpowder pages 3-4).

**Effect of oxygen on AlSi10Mg:** Baroutaji et al. (2023) directly compared AlSi10Mg parts produced at 100 ppm and 1000 ppm O₂. The effect on bulk mechanical properties was limited: reducing oxygen from 1000 to 100 ppm increased ductility by approximately 5.7%, while UTS decreased by approximately 3% and yield stress by approximately 1% (baroutaji2023theinfluenceof pages 11-12, baroutaji2023theinfluenceof pages 1-2). The expected print density was approximately 99.8% at both oxygen levels (baroutaji2023theinfluenceof pages 4-6). For TPMS lattice structures, the influence was geometry-dependent, with diamond lattices showing <2% changes and primitive lattices showing ~8% reduction in strength at the lower O₂ level (baroutaji2023theinfluenceof pages 11-12). These results suggest that for AlSi10Mg, 1000 ppm O₂ produces parts with properties broadly comparable to those at 100 ppm, making flow-purge-only systems adequate for initial screening.

**Moisture concerns:** Pauzon (2019) noted that oxygen dissolves at the melt-pool interface and elevated oxygen partial pressure stabilises oxides, with the standard commercial process level being approximately 1000 ppm (pauzon2019theprocessatmosphere pages 22-25). Deng et al. (2020) identified powder moisture as a potential source of liquid bridging and surface oxides that reduce flowability, though moisture effects on oxide inclusions and mechanical properties had not been systematically evaluated (deng2020theoriginand pages 4-7). For crack-prone alloys (6061, 7075), lower O₂ levels are likely more important than for AlSi10Mg, as these alloys are more sensitive to oxide-film disruption of grain-boundary liquid feeding during solidification.

### 4. Single-Track and Few-Layer Experiments as Alloy Screening Methods

**SST screening methodology:** Bosio et al. (2019) demonstrated a time-saving screening route in which a single 50 μm powder layer is recoated on a 40 mm-diameter substrate disc, and individual 9 mm-long laser tracks are produced across a matrix of power–speed combinations (25 combinations in their study). Tracks are classified by continuity, stability, regularity, and defects. Track width measurements are then used to select hatch distance, and bulk 10 × 10 × 5 mm validation coupons are produced subsequently, achieving <1.5% porosity for an AlSi10Mg + 4 wt% Cu alloy (bosio2019atimesavingand pages 2-3, bosio2019atimesavingand pages 1-2, bosio2019atimesavingand pages 3-4, bosio2019atimesavingand pages 4-6).

**Predictive limitations for crack-prone alloys:** Martucci et al. (2023) explicitly noted that SST-based methods cannot reproduce all layer-by-layer effects, and this limitation is "particularly important for crack-prone aluminum alloys." SSTs can narrow the P–v parameter window efficiently, but bulk-sample DOE remains necessary for complete process optimisation because SSTs do not reproduce interlayer remelting, heat accumulation, residual stress buildup, or hot cracking (martucci2023ongoingchallengesof pages 8-9). The thermal history that drives solidification cracking depends on multiscale conditions including repeated thermal cycles, segregation, brittle phase formation, and thermal stress that only develop over many layers (hussain2026metallurgicalaspectsof pages 11-12).

**Minimum coupon height for cracking assessment:** No specific minimum number of layers or coupon height for reproducing hot cracking in LPBF was found as a definitive consensus value. Bidare's system demonstrated that the powder bed reaches steady-state melted-layer thickness after approximately 10 layers (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 5-7). The Bosio validation coupons were 10 × 10 × 5 mm (~125–167 layers at 30–40 μm) (bosio2019atimesavingand pages 4-6). Based on the physics, coupons of at least 2–5 mm height (50–125 layers) are likely needed to establish representative thermal gradients and residual stress states for cracking assessment, as the first ~10 layers are non-representative in terms of thermal boundary conditions.

### 5. Second-Hand Industrial Lasers and Scanners in Research LPBF Rigs

The DTU Legacy paper (Aagaard et al., 2023) documented the challenges of upgrading a decommissioned Realizer 250 SLM machine. Its original Cambridge Technology 6240M galvanometer used a proprietary analog control mode, requiring bespoke demodulation and feedback circuitry if retained. DTU instead recommended replacing it with a modern Sunny Galvo S9320D digital scanner using the XY2-100 protocol, which offered larger aperture (20 mm vs 12 mm), comparable response time (<0.4 vs <0.35 ms), better linearity (>99.9% vs >99.5%), and improved repeatability (<8 vs <15 μrad) (aagaard2023legacylaserpowder pages 2-3, aagaard2023legacylaserpowder pages 3-3).

The KU Leuven thesis (Buls, 2018) described replacing the SCANLAB proprietary RTC board with an in-house FPGA-based controller to gain access to trajectory generation and scanner dynamics. This required designing a custom Scanner Interface Board with LTC487CSW RS485 drivers to convert FPGA signals to the ±15 V differential lines required by the scanner (buls2018asmartmachine pages 51-54).

**Key pitfalls for second-hand equipment** inferred from these sources include: (i) proprietary control interfaces that may be incompatible with open controllers; (ii) need for complete beam path inspection including fibre and connector integrity; (iii) requirement for independent laser power measurement and beam quality verification; (iv) scanner calibration and field-correction data may be machine-specific; (v) absence of manufacturer service and warranty. The MIT Hart group's system used multiple thermistors near optical components for automatic laser shutdown if misalignment caused stray beam exposure (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 7-9). No published study was found that specifically documents acceptance testing of decommissioned EOS M270/280/290 IPG lasers for research use, though the components (IPG YLR series, SCANLAB scan heads) are individually well-documented and widely available on the secondary market.

### 6. Scan Control: Open-Source XY2-100 Controllers vs SCANLAB RTC5

**DTU Teensy Controller:** Uses a Teensy 3.6 (180 MHz ARM Cortex-M4) to generate a 2 MHz XY2-100 digital stream via DMA and timer interrupts. Each XY2-100 control word contains 3 mode bits, 16 position bits, and a parity bit, providing a 16-bit × 16-bit positioning space. Laser trajectories use Bresenham line approximation. The controller supports laser-power ramping via a 10-bit DAC (0–10 V) and a 24 V PWM enable signal with modulation up to 100 kHz. It also supports three configurable sensor inputs (0–10 V or 4–20 mA) and coaxial optical monitoring. No explicit skywriting implementation was reported, though power ramping provides a foundation (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12, andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 1-3, andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 3-7).

**KU Leuven FPGA Controller:** Replaced the proprietary SCANLAB RTC with an FPGA-based system implementing three functions: a Trajectory Generator that calculates intermediate "microvectors" between coordinates, an XY2-100 Engine that packages coordinates according to the protocol, and a model-based feedforward generator for improved dynamic response. The Scanner Interface Board used LTC487CSW RS485 drivers for ±15 V differential conversion (buls2018asmartmachine pages 51-54).

**NIST AMMT:** Used an FPGA driving XY2-100 to galvanometers with synchronized laser power at 100 kHz, embedded within a layered LabVIEW control architecture (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 11-13).

**SCANLAB RTC5:** A commercial board with integrated DLL providing scan-pattern generation, skywriting, laser-on/mark/jump delays, polygon scanning, and field correction. It handles all trajectory interpolation, timing, and synchronisation internally.

**Assessment for fastest commissioning:** The SCANLAB RTC5 offers the lowest-risk path to first working scans because trajectory generation, skywriting, timing delays, and synchronisation are handled by mature, tested firmware and driver libraries. The DTU Teensy controller and the KU Leuven FPGA approach both required significant custom firmware development including trajectory interpolation, scanner dynamics modelling, and hardware interface design. For a team aiming to scan within 3–6 weeks, purchasing an RTC5 (or RTC6) with a compatible SCANLAB scanner head avoids weeks of control-system debugging, though it creates vendor dependence and limits later process-control research.

### 7. Laser and Powder Safety for a Quickly Built Research LPBF Rig

**Laser safety:** The NIST AMMT used an independently operating Allen Bradley PLC that continuously monitored temperatures, chamber oxygen, external oxygen, and door/panel status, automatically shutting down the laser and activating an alarm for any anomaly. After operation, the door could only be opened after oxygen exceeded 19.5% (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 11-13). The MIT Hart testbed placed the laser/scanner system behind a laser safety curtain, with the galvanometer assembly enclosed in a custom stainless-steel sheet-metal box. Three thermistors near optical components provided automatic laser deactivation if beam misalignment was detected (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 7-9). The SSRL synchrotron testbed was described as a portable, light-tight, Class 1 enclosed system (calta2018aninstrumentfor pages 3-4). The Bidare system was designed for remote operation within an X-ray test room (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 1-2).

**Powder/fume handling:** The MIT Hart system used a 95%-efficient filter rated for 10 nm particles to protect downstream equipment, with an oxygen sensor on the filtered exhaust before connection to building ventilation (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 7-9). The Bidare system passed exhaust through a water bath to remove potentially harmful nanoparticles (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3). The NIST AMMT used inert-gas recirculation through filtration, cooling, and dedicated air-blanket/air-knife flows to remove melt-pool vapour and ejecta (lane2016designdevelopmentsand pages 11-13).

**Minimum requirements for a rapidly built Class 4 → Class 1 system:** Based on the documented practices, the essential features are: (i) a fully enclosed, opaque enclosure around any transparent process chambers (the Perspex gas box is not itself a laser enclosure); (ii) interlocked access panels/doors with laser shutdown; (iii) a key switch or equivalent authorised-access control; (iv) filtered exhaust ventilation to capture nanoscale condensate and spatter; (v) an oxygen sensor for atmosphere monitoring with low-O₂ interlock; (vi) for aluminium powder specifically, earthing/grounding of all conductive components, antistatic handling practices, and minimisation of powder dispersal to avoid dust-explosion risk. Sargioti et al. (2025) documented that exhaust-derived Scalmalloy particles exhibited the highest oxidation and irregular morphologies, with significant cytotoxicity to lung epithelial cells, underscoring the need for effective fume capture even at small scales.

**Summary of the fastest path to first Al LPBF coupons (3–6 week target):**

The single largest schedule driver is the 10–12 week laser lead time. Sourcing an immediately available laser–scanner–lens package (new stock, demo unit, or verified used system) eliminates this bottleneck and is the highest-impact simplification (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5, griggs2022atestbedfor pages 7-9). The second-largest gain comes from replacing the machined vacuum-purgeable chamber with a small sealed metal or Perspex process box using flow-through argon purging, which Bidare demonstrated can achieve <1000 ppm O₂ adequate for initial AlSi10Mg screening (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3, bidare2018laserpowderbed pages 1-2, baroutaji2023theinfluenceof pages 11-12). Using a commercial SCANLAB RTC5 card rather than developing a custom open controller saves 1–4 weeks of firmware effort while providing proven skywriting, timing delays, and synchronisation (andersen2022aunifiedsystem pages 7-12, buls2018asmartmachine pages 51-54). A single small circular build module (20–30 mm diameter, 3–10 mm height) with a PTFE-sealed piston avoids the outsourced honed bore and interchangeable cartridge system (norouziinallu2023developmentofa pages 3-6, norouziinallu2023developmentofa pages 6-9). Beginning with single-track screening on manually spread layers provides the earliest possible laser commissioning data, while a simple travelling gravity reservoir with a compliant cord recoater enables automated multi-layer coupons with minimal mechanical complexity (griggs2022atestbedfor pages 13-17, bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 3-5, bosio2019atimesavingand pages 3-4). Commissioning with AlSi10Mg at room temperature before adding build-plate heating further derisks the schedule.

Together, these simplifications can plausibly reduce the time to first multi-layer AlSi10Mg test coupons from 10–16 weeks to 3–6 weeks, while the resulting machine architecture remains compatible with later upgrades toward the full design specification.
