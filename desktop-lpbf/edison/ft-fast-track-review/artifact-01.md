| 1 | Buy an **in-stock, tested laser–scanner–lens package**—new or used—instead of waiting for the specified 500 W laser | **Up to 8–12 calendar weeks** if it removes the stated laser lead time; allow several days for incoming inspection | Used source may have damaged delivery fibre/connector, uncertain back-reflection history, undocumented OEM interfaces, poor beam quality or no service support; mismatched scanner/lens coatings can also limit usable power | Research rigs successfully use modular 400–525 W single-mode fibre sources and commercial galvos, but the literature does not validate decommissioned EOS/IPG units specifically; acceptance should include low-power pilot-beam checks, output-versus-command measurement into a rated power meter, beam/spot measurement, fibre inspection and scanner step/field calibration before powder exposure (pqac-00000008, pqac-00000014, pqac-00000023) |
| 2 | Replace the machined vacuum chamber with a **small sealed metal box or qualified laser-safe enclosure using a simple removable process liner**; use flow-through argon rather than vacuum capability | **About 2–5 weeks** of chamber machining, outsourced honing and vacuum-component procurement | Leaks and poor displacement flow may prevent low O₂; polymer walls can be unsuitable as the primary 1070 nm safety barrier, can outgas, scratch or burn, and cannot automatically make the apparatus Class 1 | Bidare’s automated multilayer machine used a detachable 5 mm Perspex process chamber, 350 × 230 × 220 mm, and achieved <0.5% O₂; its reinforced version reached <0.1% after a 10 min Ar purge. Use a separate opaque, interlocked laser enclosure around any transparent gas box (pqac-00000008, pqac-00000010, pqac-00000011) |
| 3 | Use a **commercial SCANLAB RTC5/compatible laser-control card and vendor DLL** for the first machine; postpone the open controller | **Roughly 1–4 engineering weeks** of firmware, buffering, synchronization and trajectory-debugging effort | Driver/licence compatibility, proprietary API dependence and incorrect laser-on/jump/polygon delays; an RTC card does not remove the need to calibrate the scanner and laser response | Open alternatives are viable but nontrivial: DTU used a Teensy 3.6, 2 MHz XY2-100 stream, DMA, Bresenham interpolation and synchronized 100 kHz laser modulation; KU Leuven required an FPGA trajectory generator and custom differential interface. A mature RTC therefore offers the lower-development-risk route to first coupons (pqac-00000015, pqac-00000019, pqac-00000020) |
| 4 | Reduce the first build module to **one circular 20–30 mm bed with only 3–10 mm usable height**; omit interchangeable 25/50/75 mm modules | **About 1–3 weeks** of machining, sealing, alignment and integration | Small beds have strong edge effects, limited overflow space and different thermal/gas-flow behaviour; results may not transfer quantitatively to the later full machine | Heriot-Watt produced >99% dense multilayer material in an approximately 30 × 30 × 10 mm envelope. ESRF used an 8 mm substrate and demonstrated 4 mm cubes and stable 30-layer beds. Reduced-volume EOS experiments produced nine 7 × 7 × 1 mm coupons, although powder spill and changed processing windows were observed (pqac-00000008, pqac-00000012, pqac-00000021, pqac-00000051, pqac-00000052) |
| 5 | Use a **simple small circular build piston with a replaceable PTFE/Teflon seal**, rather than a honed long bore, sealed interchangeable cartridge and robot-ready interface | **About 1–3 weeks**, including avoidance of the outsourced honed bore | Powder leakage, piston tilt/rotation, seal creep at 200 °C, friction-induced layer errors and contamination; ordinary PTFE is near its practical continuous-temperature limit | Bidare used a descending rectangular support with a Teflon O-ring; an EOS reduction kit used a circular piston and Teflon ring. The latter reported piston rotation and scan-pattern drift, so add anti-rotation guidance and verify hot leakage before using Al powder (pqac-00000010, pqac-00000052, pqac-00000053) |
| 6 | Replace the dose-from-above hopper with a **travelling gravity reservoir and compliant cord/lip recoater**, feeding only enough powder for the small bed | **About 1–2 weeks** of hopper, valve, dosing and controls development | Dose variation, segregation, powder carried out of the bed, edge slumping and recoater jams; excess feed quickly defeats the small-batch objective | Bidare’s V-reservoir travelled with the spreader and ultimately used 3 mm silicone cord because rigid knife and cylindrical tools jammed. PETRA III used a gravity funnel and elastomer lip for automated 50 μm layers (pqac-00000008, pqac-00000010, pqac-00000038) |
| 7 | For commissioning, use **manual single-layer spreading**, then add the simplest powered recoater for multilayer coupons; do not make manual spreading the final architecture | **Several days to about 2 weeks** and provides immediate laser/process commissioning before automation is complete | Manual layers have poor thickness repeatability and require opening or repurging; they cannot credibly satisfy unattended multilayer operation and expose operators to powder | MIT’s high-pressure testbed formed a 76 ± 3 μm layer manually with a shim well and machinist’s edge. Bosio used one recoated layer on a 40 mm disc with 9 mm tracks to screen 25 power–speed combinations before bulk builds (pqac-00000005, pqac-00000047) |
| 8 | Commission with **flow purge and once-through cross-flow**, postponing vacuum evacuation and closed-loop argon recirculation | **About 1–3 weeks** of vacuum hardware, leak qualification, filter/blower and controls work | High argon consumption; stagnant pockets may remain above 100–1,000 ppm; moisture and condensate can persist; gas-flow nonuniformity causes location-dependent porosity | A small Bidare chamber reached <1,000 ppm after a reported 10 min Ar purge. For AlSi10Mg, changing O₂ from 1,000 to 100 ppm changed bulk elongation by about 5.7%, UTS by about 3% and yield stress by about 1%, suggesting 1,000 ppm can be adequate for first AlSi10Mg coupons—not proof for 6061/7075. Low local cross-flow is independently associated with greater porosity (pqac-00000011, pqac-00000028, pqac-00000040, pqac-00000041) |
| 9 | Omit the initial **200 °C heated bed** and qualify the machine first with room-temperature AlSi10Mg; add a cartridge-heated plate after multilayer operation is stable | **Several days to about 2 weeks** of heater, wiring, insulation, feedthrough and thermal-control validation | Results will under-represent the cracking mitigation available from preheating and may make 6061/7075 appear worse; thermal conditions will change after the upgrade | The literature supports AlSi10Mg as the lower-risk commissioning alloy, whereas crack-prone Al alloys require bulk thermal-history validation. Heated reduced-volume modules are feasible but add substantial mechanical and control complexity; one reported detachable Pt module exceeded 500 °C (pqac-00000034, pqac-00000051, pqac-00000054) |
| 10 | Define the first milestone as **single tracks → single layers → 1–5 mm coupons**, not full-height mechanical-test specimens or robot-ready operation | **Does not necessarily shorten hardware construction, but can advance first useful data by days to several weeks** | Single tracks identify stable melting but do not reproduce interlayer remelting, heat accumulation, residual stress, hot cracking or bulk porosity; a false process window is possible | Bosio screened 9 mm tracks on a single 50 μm layer, then validated with 10 × 10 × 5 mm bulk coupons and obtained <1.5% porosity for AlSi10Mg–4Cu. Reviews explicitly warn that SSTs cannot replace multilayer DOE, especially for crack-prone aluminium alloys (pqac-00000045, pqac-00000046, pqac-00000047, pqac-00000034, pqac-00000049) |
