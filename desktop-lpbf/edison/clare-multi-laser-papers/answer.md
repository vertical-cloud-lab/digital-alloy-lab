Question: What has Adam T. Clare (University of Nottingham, UK, and University of Nottingham Ningbo China) published, with his co-authors, on multi-laser, multi-beam, laser-array or beam-shaped additive manufacturing and laser processing of metals, in particular powder bed fusion or directed energy processing with several independently controllable lasers or emitters (for example fibre-coupled diode-laser arrays, multiple galvanometer-scanned lasers, spatial light modulators or diffractive optics)?

For each paper give the full citation with DOI, the hardware (number of lasers or emitters, wavelength, power per beam, spot size, how the beams are delivered and how each is addressed and synchronised), the materials processed, and the key quantitative finding (for example control of thermal gradients and cooling rates, in-situ preheating or heat treatment with a second beam, microstructure or texture control, crack or porosity suppression, in-situ alloying, or build rate). Also list Clare's closely related work on alloy design for additive manufacturing and on in-situ monitoring if it involves multiple beams, and say which research groups he collaborated with on the multi-laser hardware.

## Scope and principal finding

The located literature supports **one experimental Clare-coauthored study in which several independently assigned processing lasers melt the same metal part**, and **one later, explicitly simulation-only study of a leading and trailing processing laser**. It does **not** establish that Clare published an experimental metal-processing paper using an independently addressable fibre-coupled diode array, spatial light modulator (SLM), or diffractive optical element (DOE). Multiple *measurement* beams, repeated passes of *one* melting beam, and papers by other investigators must not be counted as demonstrations of Clare’s multi-source processing hardware. (sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies pages 1-2, bogo2026iterativelearningcontrol pages 1-2, hirsch2018targetedreworkstrategies pages 1-2, alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbedc pages 1-7)

### Independently controlled processing beams

**S. Sanchez, C. J. Hyde, I. A. Ashcroft, G. A. Ravi, and A. T. Clare, “Multi-laser scan strategies for enhancing creep performance in LPBF,” *Additive Manufacturing* 41 (2021), 101948. DOI: [10.1016/j.addma.2021.101948](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addma.2021.101948).** The authors identify Ravi with **Renishaw plc** and the other authors with the **University of Nottingham**. Their Renishaw RenAM 500Q has **four lasers rated at 500 W apiece**, capable of simultaneous continuous or modulated operation; the reported processing setting was **212.5 W per laser**. QuantAM assigned independently available lasers to **10-mm-wide stripe segments**, with **67° rotation between layers**: two lasers melted a vertical specimen’s cross-section, two melted the main 45° cross-section (up to four participated when supports are included), and four participated in the horizontal specimen’s cross-section. Thus this is genuinely *multiple lasers on one part*, not simply four lasers making four separate parts. The article specifies neither wavelength nor focused spot diameter, fibre routing, detailed galvanometer arrangement, nor a hardware-level timing/synchronization protocol; its documented coordination is software assignment to stripes and simultaneous firing capability. Specifications reported for other RenAM 500Q installations should not be silently imported into this experiment. (sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies pages 1-2, sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies pages 2-3, sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies pages 3-5, sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies media 7562c50c)

The feedstock was **nickel alloy 718**, subsequently solution-treated and aged; creep tests used **650 °C and 600 MPa**, with three replicates per condition. Across all specimens, measured density was **99.947–99.984%**—a range of approximately **0.04 percentage points**. The vertical multi-laser specimens had creep life **within 1% of wrought 718** and outperformed the corresponding single-laser specimens; horizontal and 45° results showed no general multi-laser penalty, although orientation dominated failure and the horizontal multi-laser case was somewhat poorer. Grain area changed with orientation and strategy: vertical **156 ± 9 versus 177 ± 21 µm²** (single versus multi), 45° **287 ± 35 versus 200 ± 14 µm²**, and horizontal **196 ± 19 versus 252 ± 25 µm²**. The authors state that multi-laser processing **more than halved build time**, but do not provide a reproducible absolute build-rate measurement in these cited passages. Crucially, the often-quoted **63% reduction and 14 cm³ h⁻¹ for IN625** belong to the *different*, non-Clare-authored Wong and colleagues benchmarking study, not this experiment. The Figure 2 schematic documents the stripe assignments. (sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies pages 3-5, sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies pages 5-7, sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies pages 1-2, sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies pages 13-14, sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies media 7562c50c, sanchez2021multilaserscanstrategies pages 2-3)

**L. Bogo, A. Clare, and D. Liao-McPherson, “Iterative Learning Control of the Cooling Rate in a Dual-Laser Powder Bed Fusion Process,” *2026 IEEE Conference on Control Technology and Applications (CCTA)* (2026), 323–329. DOI: [10.1109/CCTA62090.2026.11684174](https://doi.org/10.1109/CCTA62090.2026.11684174).** This is a **numerical control study, not a built-and-tested dual-laser machine**: two JAX-FEM thermal simulations respectively serve as a high-fidelity surrogate “plant” and a deliberately inaccurate differentiable control model. The modeled **Inconel 625** system superposes **two Gaussian beams of 50-µm modeled radius**: a leading melt source and a trailing reheat source following the same path at a controlled distance. Individually adjustable model powers are **25–100 W per beam**, trailing offset **0–250 µm**, and common scan velocity **0.25–1.0 m s⁻¹**. This fixed spatial lag and common velocity define the beams’ relative timing; no optical wavelength, physical beam-delivery train, electronic synchronization hardware, or real laser spot measurement is reported. (bogo2026iterativelearningcontrol pages 1-2, bogo2026iterativelearningcontrol pages 2-3, bogo2026iterativelearningcontrol pages 3-5)

For a representative simulated trial, iterative learning control selected **25.0 W leading/43.5 W trailing, 138 µm lag, and 0.92 m s⁻¹**, giving mean cooling **−2.34 × 10⁶ K s⁻¹** against a **−2.50 × 10⁶ K s⁻¹ target**; a one-beam baseline gave **−2.96 × 10⁶ K s⁻¹**, and model-only tuning **−1.36 × 10⁶ K s⁻¹**. Across seven initializations, the controller lowered the simulated plant’s temperature-profile cost by **more than an order of magnitude** and converged in roughly **20–40 simulated evaluations**. A three-stroke zigzag transfer test extended modeled residence in the solidification range by **480% at a stroke midpoint** and **364% at a turn** relative to the baseline. *Equiaxed grains, reduced cracking and improved properties were motivations, not experimentally verified outcomes in this paper*; the authors explicitly leave physical validation for future work. The paper lists its authors at the **University of British Columbia**, so this collaboration should not be described as an experimental Nottingham diode-array project. (bogo2026iterativelearningcontrol pages 1-2, bogo2026iterativelearningcontrol pages 3-5, bogo2026iterativelearningcontrol pages 5-6, bogo2026iterativelearningcontrol pages 6-7, bogo2026iterativelearningcontrol pages 7-7)

### Closely related metal processing and multi-beam inspection—not multiple melting sources

**M. Hirsch, P. Dryburgh, S. Catchpole-Smith, R. Patel, L. Parry, S. D. Sharples, I. A. Ashcroft, and A. T. Clare, “Targeted rework strategies for powder bed additive manufacture,” *Additive Manufacturing* 19 (2018), 127–133. DOI: [10.1016/j.addma.2017.11.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addma.2017.11.011).** A **single** ReaLizer SLM50 processing laser fabricated and selectively re-melted **IN718** at **100 W**, **40-µm spot diameter**, **560 mm s⁻¹**, and **40-µm layers**; its processing wavelength was not specified in the checked passage. Separate inspection used **two optical probe lasers**, a **1064-nm, 1–2-ns pulsed acoustic-generation source** projected through a grating (approximately **200-µm generation patch**) and a **532-nm continuous-wave detection source**. Thus three laser sources appear in the workflow, but **only one melts metal**, and SRAS inspection was performed on prepared surfaces, not shown as synchronized simultaneous multi-beam melting. Targeted hatch and spiral rework reduced mean surface-pore depth **50%** and **31%**, respectively, whereas single-shot rework achieved **17.1%** mean reduction and was not comparably reliable. Collaborators are Nottingham’s **Optics and Photonics Group**, **Advanced Component Engineering Laboratory**, and **Centre for Additive Manufacturing**. (hirsch2018targetedreworkstrategies pages 1-2, hirsch2018targetedreworkstrategies pages 2-3, hirsch2018targetedreworkstrategies pages 3-5)

### Adjacent alloy-design work and attribution limits

**M. Simonelli, N. T. Aboulkhair, P. Cohen, J. W. Murray, A. T. Clare, C. Tuck, and R. J. M. Hague, “A comparison of Ti-6Al-4V in-situ alloying in Selective Laser Melting using simply-mixed and satellited powder blend feedstocks,” *Materials Characterization* 143 (2018), 118–126. DOI: [10.1016/j.matchar.2018.05.039](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matchar.2018.05.039).** The comparison concerns **Ti–Al–V powder preparation and alloy homogenization**, not several lasers: the satellited Ti/Al/V blend was **90:6:4 by weight**; satelliting reduced but did not eliminate composition segregation relative to simply mixed elemental powders, while pre-alloyed feedstock was more uniform. No independent beam-by-beam power, wavelength, spot, delivery or synchronization specification is applicable to a multi-source claim here. (simonelli2018acomparisonof pages 7-8, simonelli2018acomparisonof pages 1-2)

**S. Catchpole-Smith, N. Aboulkhair, L. Parry, C. Tuck, I. A. Ashcroft, and A. Clare, “Fractal scan strategies for selective laser melting of ‘unweldable’ nickel superalloys,” *Additive Manufacturing* 15 (2017), 113–122. DOI: [10.1016/j.addma.2017.02.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addma.2017.02.002).** Hilbert/Peano–Gosper scan paths containing approximately **100-µm vectors** improved **CM247LC bulk density by 2 ± 0.7 percentage points** relative to island scanning and reduced cracking. This is **spatiotemporal path shaping with a scanning beam**, *not* holographic beam splitting or individually addressable emitters. Other identified Clare-associated alloy studies concern boron-alloyed or composition-tailored Ti–6Al–4V and titanium-aluminide synthesis, but the retrieved primary texts were insufficient to give verified paper-by-paper laser specifications or quantitative outcomes; they cannot be presented as demonstrated multi-laser results. (catchpolesmith2017fractalscanstrategies pages 1-5)

**Who supplied the multi-laser hardware?** In the verified experimental metal-processing study, the collaboration is **Nottingham’s Sanchez–Hyde–Ashcroft–Clare team with Ravi at Renishaw**, using Renishaw’s four-laser RenAM 500Q. The **Nottingham Optics and Photonics/Advanced Component Engineering** collaboration developed the separate SRAS inspection/rework approach. **Sheffield’s Groom and colleagues** collaborated with Clare on *OCT monitoring of polymer SLS*, but the separately located **808-nm, 4.5-W-per-source, fibre-coupled diode-array Ti–6Al–4V research** is **Mohammed T. Alsaddah’s Sheffield dissertation supervised by Kamran Mumtaz and Kristian Groom**, *not a Clare-coauthored paper*. Accordingly it would be erroneous to ascribe its emitter count, power or thermal-control findings to Clare. No verified Clare-coauthored paper in the examined record demonstrates a DOE/SLM-split metal-processing beam, an experimental independently adjustable trailing preheat beam, or an independently driven diode-array DED/LPBF print head.
