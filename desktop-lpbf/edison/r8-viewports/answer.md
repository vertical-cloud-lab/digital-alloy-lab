## Viewport and Monitoring Design for a Lab-Built Desktop LPBF Machine: Literature Synthesis

### 1. Commercial and Research LPBF Machine Monitoring Integration

**Commercial systems.** In-situ monitoring in commercial LPBF machines is broadly classified into coaxial systems (sharing the processing-laser optical path via a partially reflective or dichroic mirror) and off-axis systems (sensors positioned outside the optical path at a defined viewing angle) (grasso2017processdefectsand pages 18-20, lafirenza2025developmentofinsitu pages 35-37). The EOS EOSTATE MeltPool system uses both coaxial and off-axis sensors to map light emitted from the melt-pool region onto build slices for layer-by-layer defect identification; the EOSTATE PowderBed module employs a separate off-axis camera with optimized illumination to capture powder-bed uniformity (grasso2017processdefectsand pages 30-33). SLM Solutions provides a coaxial-pyrometer-based Melt Pool Monitoring (MPM) system and an off-axis Layer Control System camera that can detect non-homogeneous powder deposition and trigger automatic recoating (grasso2017processdefectsand pages 30-33). Concept Laser's QM meltpool3D uses a coaxial high-speed NIR CMOS camera (1280 × 1024 pixels) and photodiode at 10 kHz sampling, with a partially reflective mirror transmitting melt-pool radiation in the 780–950 nm band while reflecting the 1064 nm laser (grasso2017processdefectsand pages 18-20). The QM coating module uses an off-axis camera (grasso2017processdefectsand pages 30-33).

**Window materials and protection.** The processing-laser window in most commercial machines is typically fused silica or quartz, anti-reflection coated at the laser wavelength. In Bidare et al.'s open-architecture research PBF system, the laser beam passes through an anti-reflection coated UV-grade fused silica window in the top surface of the shielding gas chamber (bidare2017anopenarchitecturemetal pages 2-3). Side viewing windows were made from Zerodur glass flats of 75 mm diameter, polished to λ/4 on both surfaces (bidare2018fluidandparticle pages 2-3). The EOS M290 incorporates a Y-duct gas-inlet system with two nozzles: a lower inlet directing flow across the powder bed and an upper inlet with flow straightener specifically intended to prevent condensate and smoke from recirculating and clouding the laser lens/window (abeyta2024anexperimentalapproach pages 31-37). A filter cabinet with multi-step cartridge collects condensate from the recirculated argon (abeyta2024anexperimentalapproach pages 10-17).

**Gas flow for window protection.** The EOS M290 operates at approximately 120 m³/hr inlet volumetric flow, corresponding to ~7.2 m/s at the Y-duct entrance (Re ≈ 39,000) (abeyta2024anexperimentalapproach pages 31-37). A 1/3-scale M290 model tested with MRV showed a maximum velocity of ~4 m/s near the center of the lower vent (abeyta2024anexperimentalapproach pages 26-31). Commercial systems generally employ cross-flow designs from an inlet at the rear to an exhaust at the front, with optimized inlet/outlet geometry, nozzle height, and stable flow fields up to ~30 mm above the powder bed (li2022areviewof pages 31-33).

### 2. Window and Cover-Slide Fouling

Quantitative transmission-loss data specifically for aluminium alloys during LPBF is scarce in the accessible literature. Aluminium alloys generate substantially more vapour and condensate than steels due to the low boiling points of Mg (1091 °C) and Zn (907 °C) in 6061 and 7075 compositions. The principal fouling mechanism is condensation of metallic vapour on cooler surfaces, combined with spatter and nanoparticle deposition. The gas cross-flow is the primary defense: in the EOS M290, the upper inlet nozzle is specifically designed to prevent condensate and smoke from reaching the laser window (abeyta2024anexperimentalapproach pages 31-37). Gas-flow-based spatter and fume removal has been shown to be effective when velocity is uniform and sufficiently high across the build plate (li2022areviewof pages 31-33). Simulations indicate stable flow fields up to 30 mm above the powder bed, with high-velocity central flow helping to remove spatter (li2022areviewof pages 31-33).

For side viewports, windows positioned downstream of the cross-flow (i.e., near the exhaust side) will receive a higher condensate and spatter load than upstream windows. Windows mounted in the lid directly above the build zone are exposed to vertically ejected vapour plume and hot spatter. A recent patent (JP2026518480A) describes a device for monitoring protective-glass contamination in 3D printing by measuring changes in transmitted light through the window. For the proposed desktop machine, replaceable sacrificial cover slides (inexpensive borosilicate or soda-lime glass) placed inside the main viewport are a practical solution; these can be replaced between builds or when transmission degrades visibly.

**Low-elevation-angle imaging.** Bidare et al. achieved side imaging at approximately 10° above the horizontal through Zerodur glass flats in the side of their Perspex shielding chamber, using a Photron Fastcam Mini UX100 at 8,000 fps (1280 × 616 pixels) with a QiOptiq Optem Fusion 7:1 zoom lens at 135 mm working distance, providing adjustable fields of view from 14 × 11 mm² (DOF 2 mm) to 2 × 1.5 mm² (DOF 0.2 mm), illuminated by a 300 W tungsten filament lamp focused into ~15 × 5 mm² on the powder bed, with an IR-absorbing filter in front of the lens (bidare2018fluidandparticle pages 2-3, bidare2018fluidandparticle pages 3-4). For schlieren imaging of gas-density gradients, the same camera recorded at 16,000 fps (1280 × 312 pixels) with a 75–300 mm telephoto lens, using a z-type schlieren system with two 1.27 m focal-length mirrors and a 300 W tungsten lamp source, adding a polarizer and IR-absorbing filter (bidare2018fluidandparticle pages 3-4).

**Quantitative spatter metrics.** Key metrics extracted from high-speed imaging include:
- **Spatter count:** Barrett detected over 10,447 spatter events in 7 s of recording at 1,000 fps; more than 1,100 particles per parameter condition were used for statistical analysis (barrett2019highspeedstereovision pages 92-99, barrett2019highspeedstereovision pages 82-87).
- **Velocity:** Bulk distributions showed mean velocities of 9.73 m/s (σ = 6.74 m/s, range 0.26–79.64 m/s) in one recording (barrett2019highspeedstereovision pages 82-87); a parameter-specific subset yielded mean 2.02 m/s (σ = 1.96 m/s) (barrett2019highspeedstereovision pages 92-99). Ahmad reported molten spatter velocities of 3–8 m/s (ahmad2019largescaleselective pages 41-43).
- **Ejection angle:** Barrett reported a mean vertical ejection angle of 45.4° ± 2.14°, with parameter-specific averages ranging from ~39° to ~48° (barrett2019highspeedstereovision pages 99-106).
- **Thermal classification:** Ahmad classified 15% of spatters as molten, 60% as hot, and 25% as cold (ahmad2019largescaleselective pages 41-43).
- **Spatter influence:** Zhang et al. demonstrated that incorporating spatter count and ejection angle into regression models reduced layer surface roughness prediction error by >50% compared to process-parameter-only models (zhang2023influenceofspattering pages 1-4).

### 4. Viewport Design for Vacuum and Argon Chambers

**Window thickness formulas.** For a simply supported circular glass or fused-silica pressure window, Robinson (1980) provides the thickness equation t = d · k · √(q · F/S), where d is the unsupported diameter (inches), q is the pressure differential (psi), F/S is the safety factor, and k is a material coefficient (robinson1980designofviewing pages 12-17, robinson1980designofviewing pages 17-22). The recommended safety factor is 10 for chamber windows subjected to long-term loading, based on modulus-of-rupture values that account for time-dependent strength degradation of glass (robinson1980designofviewing pages 12-17). Ihracska et al. (2017) provide an alternative formulation: t_min = D₀ · √(K_w · SF · Δp / σ_des), with K_w = 0.75 for the supported case and K_w = 0.125 for the unsupported case, recommending safety factors of 3–5 for well-characterized systems (ihracska2017optomechanicaldesignfor pages 8-9). **Seal types and thermal limits.** Elastomeric O-ring seals (Viton/FKM) are limited to approximately 200 °C continuous operation and are suitable for the proposed argon chamber at 200 °C build-plate temperature, provided the O-ring groove is thermally isolated; EPDM O-rings are limited to ~150 °C and should not be used in this application (anheier2013summaryofthe pages 11-14). For higher temperatures (up to 450 °C), brazed sapphire viewports using active silver-copper alloys containing titanium are commercially available (anheier2013summaryofthe pages 9-11). Copper knife-edge gasket seals have been validated to 250 °C bakeout without leaking, with helium leak rates below 6 × 10⁻¹¹ Torr·L/s, using BK7, fused silica, sapphire, or silicon windows (crane2005nonmagneticuhvoptical pages 2-4, crane2005nonmagneticuhvoptical pages 1-2). The titanium-sleeve brazed sapphire viewport design by Crane & Ekstrom uses a 2.0 mm thick sapphire window with a 35.6 mm clear aperture and is bakeable to 450 °C (crane2005nonmagneticuhvoptical pages 1-2).

**Thermal considerations.** Standard vacuum viewports use a 200 °C maximum flange temperature as a guideline (weinmeister2023thermallimitsof pages 1-2). Fused silica has a thermal conductivity of only 1.38 W/m·K, while sapphire is 20.5 W/m·K and CVD diamond is ~1,000 W/m·K (weinmeister2023thermallimitsof pages 1-2). Under natural convection (h ≈ 10 W/m²·K), standard viewports can tolerate incident heat fluxes of approximately 1.5–1.7 kW/m²; with forced convection, the limit rises to >10 kW/m² (weinmeister2023thermallimitsof pages 2-4). With the build plate at 200 °C and the viewport at ~160–200 mm distance, radiative heat loading on viewports is modest, but thermal gradients between the window and flange must be evaluated (weinmeister2023thermallimitsof pages 4-4).

### 5. Laser Safety of Viewports on a Class 1 Enclosure

**Regulatory framework.** Under IEC 60825-1 and ANSI Z136.1, a Class 1 laser product is one where the accessible emission under all conditions of normal use does not exceed the accessible emission limit (AEL) for Class 1 at the relevant wavelength and exposure duration. For a 500 W CW fiber laser at 1070 nm enclosed in a protective housing, every aperture—including camera viewports—must attenuate any accessible laser radiation (direct, specularly reflected, or diffusely reflected) to below the Class 1 AEL (king2014lasersafetygram pages 14-16, king2014lasersafetygram pages 9-12).

**Optical density requirements.** The required OD at 1070 nm is determined by OD = log₁₀(H_accessible / MPE), where H_accessible is the irradiance or radiant exposure at the viewport. For the worst case of a specular (mirror-like) reflection of the full 500 W beam reaching a viewport at close range, the required OD would be approximately 5.7 (since OD = log₁₀(500/0.001) ≈ 5.7 for a 1 mW accessible-emission limit). King (2014) cites OD > 1.0 at 1045–1070 nm for a 100 mW CW source under routine access conditions; for a 500 W source, this scales proportionally higher (king2014lasersafetygram pages 14-16, king2014lasersafetygram pages 16-18).

**Specular vs. diffuse reflections.** At the proposed camera angles, both specular and diffuse reflections must be considered. Aluminium alloys have high reflectivity at 1070 nm (~60–90% for polished surfaces). At low grazing angles (10–14°), specular reflections from the flat powder bed or melt pool can redirect a significant fraction of the incident beam toward side viewports. Diffuse reflections from an ideal Lambertian surface decrease with the inverse square of distance and the cosine of the viewing angle; at 160–200 mm distance from a small melt pool, the diffuse irradiance is substantially below the direct beam but may still exceed Class 1 AEL depending on geometry (king2014lasersafetygram pages 14-16). The steep camera window at ~54° receives a lower specular-reflection risk but is exposed to the vapor plume that emits broadband radiation.

**Practical recommendations.** Camera viewports should incorporate OD ≥ 6 laser-line notch or bandpass filters at 1070 nm for all windows that have any line of sight to the powder bed or any reflective surface inside the chamber. A 975 nm short-wave-pass filter (as used by Thanki et al.) rejects the 1064/1070 nm processing laser while transmitting visible and near-IR process emissions for monitoring (thanki2024correlationbetweeninprocess pages 132-138). Such filters must be rated for the exposure duration of the laser (continuous for CW), not merely for pulsed operation. The protective housing construction, interlock design, and warning systems must be evaluated by a qualified laser safety officer in accordance with the applicable standard, as the specific geometry and worst-case reflection paths determine the actual hazard classification at each viewport (king2014lasersafetygram pages 9-12).

### Limitations

Several areas lacked quantitative published data: (1) Measured transmission-loss curves for laser and camera windows during aluminium alloy builds are not widely reported in the open literature, though it is well known that Al, Mg, and Zn vapour condensation is severe. (2) Specific viewport geometries, angles, and materials for Trumpf TruPrint, Renishaw, Aconity MIDI, and Additive Industries machines are not disclosed in the accessible peer-reviewed literature, as these are proprietary designs. (3) Laser safety OD calculations are geometry- and application-specific; the values presented here are framework estimates that must be verified through a formal hazard analysis by a qualified professional.
