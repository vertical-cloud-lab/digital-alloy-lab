# Multi-Laser Powder Bed Fusion for a Desktop Alloy-Screening LPBF Machine: A Literature Review and Design Guidance

## 1. Multi-Laser and Multi-Beam PBF Research at the University of Sheffield and Affiliated Groups

### 1.1 Clarification on Attribution

The user asked specifically about Adam T. Clare (University of Nottingham) and his group's multi-laser PBF work. While Clare has published extensively on LPBF topics including spatter formation, in-situ monitoring, and an additive manufacturing patent on powder composition, the most directly relevant body of work on **independently controlled multi-laser and multi-beam powder bed fusion with diode arrays** originates primarily from the **University of Sheffield** group led by Kamran Mumtaz and Kristian Groom, with doctoral contributions from Alsaddah, Caglar, Veetil, Aydin, and Erman. The doctoral thesis by Caglar on "Hybrid Multi-Laser Powder Bed Fusion" bridges Sheffield and Nottingham research, and Clare has co-authored several related LPBF studies. The systems and results described below derive from this closely affiliated research network.

### 1.2 Diode Area Melting (DAM) — 808 nm Systems

The original DAM concept uses an individually addressable fibre-coupled diode-laser array mounted on a mechanically traversed gantry head. The Sheffield 808 nm DAM system comprises a bespoke two-dimensional fibre-array head (2D-FAH) containing two rows of 50 multimode fibres (105 µm core, NA 0.22), for a head capacity of 100 independently addressable channels, although experimental work typically activated 10 lasers simultaneously (alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbeda pages 137-144, alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbed pages 137-144). Each 808 nm source provides 5 W, with relay optics at 0.6× magnification producing Gaussian spots of approximately 65 µm diameter at a 63.5 mm focal length (alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbeda pages 137-144, alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbed pages 137-144). An earlier Sheffield DAM module used 19 emitters at 2.5 W each (50 W total) forming a 4.75 mm × 0.25 mm linear stripe at 4.21 × 10³ W/cm² (alsaddah2022diodeareamelting pages 2-3). Three configurable beam-array layouts (LP1–LP3) provided different write widths and beam spacings: LP1 placed 10 beams in two arrays with 38.1 µm centre-to-centre spacing covering 0.381 × 0.13 mm; LP2 created a 1.72 mm strip; and LP3 produced a 2.5 mm write width with 254 µm pitch (alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbeda pages 137-144, alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbed pages 137-144). Each beam could be individually switched to perform melting, preheating, post-heating, or remelting functions in a single pass (alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbeda pages 137-144, alsaddah2022diodeareamelting pages 2-3). DOI: 10.1016/j.matdes.2022.110518.

**Demonstrated capabilities:** Thermal-camera measurements on Ti6Al4V showed that increasing the number of active 808 nm beams progressively reduced cooling rate, from 778 K/s with a single laser to 191 K/s with six lasers (veetil2024diodeareamelting pages 164-169, veetil2024diodeareameltinga pages 164-169). DAM simulated cooling rates of 2211–8400 K/s for 3–5 laser configurations at 100–250 mm/min scanning speed were 3–4 orders of magnitude lower than conventional LPBF rates of 10⁴–10⁷ K/s (liang2026diodeareamelting pages 1-2, liang2026diodeareamelting pages 11-13). This slower cooling produced coarser HCP lath spacings (0.8–1.3 µm) with evidence of retained β phase, contrasting with the predominantly martensitic α′ structure of conventional LPBF Ti6Al4V (liang2026diodeareamelting pages 1-2, liang2026diodeareamelting pages 11-13). Near-surface tensile residual stress was measured at up to 138.2 MPa, with scan-direction stress approximately 53% higher than transverse stress (liang2026diodeareamelting pages 11-13, liang2026diodeareamelting pages 1-2). The earlier 808 nm work achieved approximately 80% density in multi-layer 17-4 PH stainless steel (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowderb pages 36-43, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 36-43). DOI: 10.1007/s40964-026-01951-x.

### 1.3 Blue-DAM — 450 nm Diode Arrays

A second-generation DAM system uses 450 nm blue fibre-coupled diode lasers for improved absorptivity. Two principal configurations have been reported: (i) six independently addressable 450 nm diodes at approximately 3.5 W each (21 W total), with individual spot diameters of approximately 70 µm and a measured beam-profile height of 107 µm (erman2025diodeareamelting pages 4-6); and (ii) nine 450 nm diodes at approximately 4–4.5 W each (36–40.5 W total), with individual spots of approximately 72 µm and a nine-beam FWHM width of 659 µm (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowder pages 76-81, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 76-81, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowderb pages 76-81, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 123-128). Both configurations use purpose-built multi-laser heads with 60 mm focal-length optics, fibre delivery through approximately 1 m of optical fibre, and approximately 3% measured head losses (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowder pages 76-81, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 76-81). DOI: 10.26153/tsw/51029.

**Demonstrated capabilities:** The six-laser blue-DAM system processed single-layer SS316L to 99.99% relative density at selected parameters, with best surface roughness of Ra = 3.469 µm (erman2025diodeareamelting pages 1-2, erman2025diodeareamelting pages 12-13, erman2025diodeareamelting pages 4-6). The slower DAM thermal cycle produced sub-grain cellular structures approximately 4 µm in size (a 100% increase over conventional LPBF) and substantially more δ-ferrite, potentially beneficial for stress-corrosion cracking resistance (erman2025diodeareamelting pages 1-2). DAM cooling rate at 450 nm was approximately 600 °C/s, some four orders of magnitude slower than conventional LPBF (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowderb pages 36-43, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 36-43, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowder pages 36-43). DOI: 10.1007/s00170-025-15090-0.

### 1.4 Hybrid Multi-Laser PBF — 1064 nm Fibre Laser + 450 nm Array

A hybrid platform combined a conventional SPI 200 W single-mode fibre laser (1064 nm, M² = 0.98, approximately 100 µm spot, 450 mm focal length) with the 450 nm blue diode-laser arrays described above (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowder pages 76-81, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 76-81, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowderb pages 76-81). This allowed direct comparison of conventional LPBF, blue-diode DAM, and hybrid operation where both sources contribute to the same build. Melt-pool width was found to be approximately 75% greater than the irradiated array width, and the smoothest single tracks were produced at scanning speeds of 40–80 mm/min, with markedly reduced spattering below 80 mm/min (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 123-128).

### 1.5 DLAH/DPM — Dynamic Laser-Assisted Heating with Diode Point Melting

The DAMX platform at the University of Sheffield combines a 43.5–44 W, 450 nm blue diode point-melting (DPM) laser (approximately 100 × 150 µm spot) with a 140 W, 915 nm defocused dynamic laser-assisted heating (DLAH) source, adjustable from approximately 6.35 × 6.35 mm to 12.8 × 12.8 mm top-hat profile (aydin2025theuseofa pages 110-114, aydin2025theuseofa pages 106-110, aydin2025theuseofa pages 191-195). The two lasers are mounted on a common gantry but independently operated and spatially/temporally synchronised, allowing the heating beam to lead, surround, or trail the melting spot. DLAH provided localised heating up to approximately 400 °C, stabilised melt pools, and enabled Ti6Al4V processing to 99.99% density with Ra = 2.84 µm (aydin2025theuseofa pages 156-160, aydin2025theuseof pages 156-160). Microstructural control was demonstrated: α′ martensite lath width increased from below 2.6 µm to approximately 3.0 µm, and lath aspect ratio decreased from above 2.8 to 2.4–2.5 (aydin2025theuseofa pages 156-160, aydin2025theuseof pages 156-160). Static heating rates of approximately 30.6 °C/s were reported. The literature on Ti6Al4V preheating cited by this work gives quantitative residual-stress benchmarks: 71% reduction at 370 °C, 88.3% at 470 °C, and effective elimination at or above 570 °C (aydin2025theuseofa pages 156-160, aydin2025theuseof pages 156-160, aydin2025theuseof pages 94-96).

## 2. Other Multi-Laser LPBF Research Platforms and Architectures

### 2.1 Dual-Laser Overlap Interaction Studies

Yin et al. (2021) studied dual-beam laser–matter interaction in multi-laser PBF and found that overlap regions between two laser fields are significantly more susceptible to surface-quality degradation, internal gaseous pores, and lack of fusion than isolated single-laser regions (yin2021dualbeamlasermatterinteraction pages 1-2). Melt-pool collision caused liquid accumulation and large-droplet spatter. Quantitatively, AlSi10Mg elongation in the dual-laser overlap specimen was **17.4% lower** than in isolated single-laser specimens (yin2021dualbeamlasermatterinteraction pages 1-2). "Size first" multi-laser systems have overlap regions ranging from several millimetres to tens of millimetres, while "productivity first" systems have overlaps of hundreds of millimetres (yin2021dualbeamlasermatterinteraction pages 1-2). DOI: 10.1016/j.addma.2021.102178.

### 2.2 Multi-Laser Scanning Strategies and Residual Stress

Zhang et al. (2022) computationally modelled three coordinated dual-laser configurations for Ti-6Al-4V: preheating (leading auxiliary beam), post-heating (trailing auxiliary beam), and parallel heating (adjacent beams). All three strategies lowered residual stress relative to single-laser scanning, with post-heating identified as the most effective approach, achieving up to **10.41% residual-stress reduction** (zhang2022processparametersoptimisation pages 10-12). DOI: 10.3390/met12030420.

### 2.3 Multi-Laser AlSi10Mg Fatigue Studies

Nagalingam et al. (2025) compared single-laser and multi-laser L-PBF configurations for AlSi10Mg, investigating the impact on microstructure, tensile properties, and fatigue performance. Single-laser builds exhibited superior strength and ductility, while multi-laser builds showed reduced performance due to localised defects such as lack of fusion and microcracks (nagalingam2025impactofmultiplelaser pages 2-4). Critically, gas flow effects became more pronounced with multiple-laser processing because more spatter is generated from laser interactions in a small scan area. Reduced gas flow led to fatigue degradation due to increased defect density. Optimised multi-laser strategies could achieve fatigue performance comparable to single-laser builds (nagalingam2025impactofmultiplelaser pages 2-4). DOI: 10.3390/met15070807.

### 2.4 Stitch Zone Research

Rindler (2022) investigated stitch zones in multi-laser LPBF and found that 500 µm of model overlap between STL files produced clear contour-hatch overlap and was required for defect-free stitching, whereas 50 µm was insufficient (rindler2022aninvestigationof pages 88-97). The nominal contour offset reference was 85 µm (half a weld-track width), and an additional 10 µm of hatch offset produced no observed flaws. Contour-hatch lack of fusion was identified as the key stitch-zone risk (rindler2022aninvestigationof pages 88-97). In a quadrant multi-laser study, density reached approximately 99.93% in separate regions and 99.88% in overlapping regions; overlap zones showed larger melt pools, bottom pores, stronger {100}β grain orientation, and larger laths (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 32-36, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowderb pages 32-36, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowder pages 32-36).

### 2.5 Dual-Laser Beam Interaction Studies

Zhang and Arnold (2020) demonstrated that combining two identical low-power laser beams with a spatial offset created an elliptical composite beam that improved line roughness reduction by over 20% compared to a single Gaussian beam of the same total power, by producing a longer molten pool (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowder pages 76-81). DOI: 10.1088/2631-7990/ab94c6.

## 3. Process Effects of Multiple Beams Acting Near Each Other

### 3.1 Beam Spacing and Overlap Effects

When two laser fields generate adjacent or intersecting tracks, melt-pool collision produces liquid accumulation and large-droplet spatter (yin2021dualbeamlasermatterinteraction pages 1-2). The DAM nine-laser arrangement spanning 659 µm FWHM produced melt pools approximately 75% wider than the irradiated array width (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 123-128). In the multi-laser diode array, beam configurations using FWHM widths of 52, 215, 430, and 659 µm (from 1, 3, 6, and 9 lasers respectively) demonstrated that melt-pool depth reached a penetration limit as laser count increased (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 123-128).

### 3.2 Cooling Rate and Solidification Control

The most dramatic process effect of multiple co-acting beams is modification of the thermal cycle. DAM cooling rates of 191–8400 K/s compare with conventional LPBF rates of 10⁴–10⁷ K/s, representing a reduction of approximately 3–4 orders of magnitude (liang2026diodeareamelting pages 1-2, veetil2024diodeareamelting pages 164-169, caglar2023multiple450nm pages 1-3, veetil2024diodeareameltinga pages 164-169, liang2026diodeareamelting pages 11-13, veetil2024diodeareameltinga pages 187-193). Increasing beam count from one to six lasers reduced measured cooling rate from 778 K/s to 191 K/s (veetil2024diodeareamelting pages 164-169, veetil2024diodeareameltinga pages 164-169). This slower cooling promoted retained β phase in Ti6Al4V rather than complete martensitic transformation, and produced coarser HCP lath spacings of 0.8–1.3 µm (liang2026diodeareamelting pages 1-2, liang2026diodeareamelting pages 11-13, veetil2024diodeareamelting pages 199-206). In SS316L, the slower DAM thermal cycle produced sub-grain cells approximately 4 µm in size versus approximately 2 µm in conventional LPBF, with substantially more δ-ferrite (erman2025diodeareamelting pages 1-2).

### 3.3 Plume, Spatter, and Gas-Flow Interactions

Gas flow is critical when multiple lasers operate simultaneously. Spatter generation increases significantly with multi-laser processing because of the interaction of multiple plumes in a confined area (nagalingam2025impactofmultiplelaser pages 2-4). The laser plume from one beam can attenuate or scatter the beam of an adjacent laser, causing melt-pool instabilities and defects (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 38-41, revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 36-38, santecchia2020materialreusein pages 5-8). Higher flow speeds near the build platform are preferable, and gas velocity varies strongly with vertical position and location along the flow direction (weaver2021inertgasflow pages 20-23). For multi-laser systems, scan scheduling from upwind toward downwind and ensuring uniform cross-flow across the entire overlap field are essential (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 38-41, santecchia2020materialreusein pages 5-8).

### 3.4 Quantitative Thresholds

Key quantitative findings include: (i) AlSi10Mg elongation degradation of **17.4%** in dual-laser overlap zones versus single-laser (yin2021dualbeamlasermatterinteraction pages 1-2); (ii) AlSi10Mg elongation decreases of up to **10%** with multiple-laser scanning (nagalingam2025impactofmultiplelaser pages 2-4); (iii) density of **99.93%** in separate quadrant regions versus **99.88%** in overlap regions (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 32-36, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowderb pages 32-36); (iv) optimal stitch-zone model overlap of **500 µm** required for defect-free multi-laser stitching (rindler2022aninvestigationof pages 88-97); (v) residual-stress reduction of up to **10.41%** with an optimised dual-laser post-heating strategy (zhang2022processparametersoptimisation pages 10-12); and (vi) preheating at 370 °C providing **71%** residual-stress reduction, and 470 °C providing **88.3%** reduction (aydin2025theuseofa pages 156-160, aydin2025theuseof pages 156-160, aydin2025theuseof pages 94-96).

## 4. Design Implications for the Base Machine

### 4.1 Wavelength Selection for Aluminium

For aluminium alloys, shorter laser wavelengths provide dramatically better absorptivity. AlSi12 powder absorptivity was measured at approximately **48% at 450 nm**, **26% at 808 nm**, and **19% near 1060 nm** (alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbeda pages 149-153, alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbed pages 149-153). This represents a 153% increase from 1060 nm to 450 nm (alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbed pages 153-158, alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbeda pages 153-158). Blue 450 nm diode lasers therefore offer a substantially more energy-efficient route for aluminium processing, though current commercially available individual blue diodes are limited to approximately 3.5–4.5 W each (aydin2025theuseofa pages 78-82, aydin2025theuseof pages 78-82, erman2025diodeareamelting pages 1-2, erman2025diodeareamelting pages 4-6). Multiple blue diodes can be combined (arrays of 6–9 or more) to provide sufficient total power, but individual beam quality (M²) and power per channel remain below those of single-mode 1070 nm fibre lasers.

### 4.2 Fibre-Coupled Diode Arrays vs. Multiple Fibre Lasers for Aluminium

Fibre-coupled diode arrays at 450 nm are potentially a better route to multiple independent beams for aluminium than several 1070 nm fibre lasers, for several reasons: (i) approximately 2.5× higher powder absorptivity at 450 nm reduces required power per channel (alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbeda pages 149-153, alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbed pages 149-153); (ii) low cost per emitter ($10–100 each vs. $10,000+ per fibre laser) enables research-grade scaling to 10–100 channels (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowder pages 36-43, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 36-43); (iii) the DAM architecture has demonstrated full melting of multiple alloys at powers of only 3.5–5 W per channel (erman2025diodeareamelting pages 1-2, erman2025diodeareamelting pages 4-6). However, the principal limitation is that DAM scanning speeds (50–500 mm/min) are orders of magnitude slower than galvo-scanned fibre-laser LPBF (100–3000 mm/s), producing fundamentally different thermal histories and microstructures (caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowderb pages 36-43, liang2026diodeareamelting pages 1-2, caglar2024hybridmultilaserpowdera pages 36-43). For alloy screening where controlling cooling rate is itself a research objective, this is advantageous. For production-representative processing of aluminium, the 1070 nm fibre laser on the initial galvo scanner remains essential.

### 4.3 Lid, Window, and Scanner Geometry

Over a Ø50 mm build plate, an f = 254 mm f-theta lens produces a scan field of approximately ±25 mm (Ø50 mm), well within typical galvo scan-cone angles. For future multi-laser capability, the lid design should accommodate: (i) one primary Ø70–90 mm clear window for the existing galvo/f-theta scanner (1070 nm AR-coated); (ii) a separate auxiliary port of Ø40–60 mm for a second laser source (heating beam, diode-array head, or second scanner); and (iii) replaceable window frames with water cooling and sacrificial cover-glass capability. If two galvo scanners are desired in the future, a single larger window of Ø120–150 mm or two separate Ø70–90 mm ports would be required. All window coatings should be specified for 1070 nm initially, with mounting provisions for quick substitution to broadband or blue-rated windows.

### 4.4 Controller Architecture

The base machine should include: (i) one XY2-100 (or SL2-100) digital scanner channel for the primary galvo; (ii) reserve board space and cable routing for a second XY2-100 channel; (iii) isolated analogue/TTL laser-control outputs for at least 2 additional sources, expandable to 8–16 channels for a diode-array head; (iv) a shared hardware clock and deterministic trigger bus for synchronising scanner position, diode-array state, and gantry encoder; and (v) an emergency blanking line that can simultaneously disable all sources.

### 4.5 Cooling Budget

The base 300–500 W fibre laser and scanner require approximately 1.5–3 kW of chiller rejection. For future upgrades, reserve an additional 1–3 kW of cooling capacity for: a second fibre laser (additional approximately 2–3 kW), or a diode-array head (approximately 0.5–1.5 kW for a 6–9-emitter research head), or a 140 W heating laser (approximately 0.5–1 kW). Manifold space and independent flow/temperature monitoring for at least two additional coolant loops should be provided.

### 4.6 Gas Flow Direction and Multi-Laser Considerations

The argon cross-flow must be oriented so that plume and spatter from any active laser are swept away from other active beams and from the unprocessed powder. For a future diode-array head traversing the bed, the active beam row should be transverse to the cross-flow direction. For dual galvo scanners, tracks should be scheduled from upwind toward downwind to prevent plume interference (revanur2024novelscanstrategies pages 38-41, weaver2021inertgasflow pages 20-23, santecchia2020materialreusein pages 5-8, nagalingam2025impactofmultiplelaser pages 2-4). Gas-flow speed should be highest near the build platform and uniform across the full scan field (weaver2021inertgasflow pages 20-23).

### 4.7 Laser Safety

For multi-source operation, each laser source requires: (i) an independent keyed enable circuit; (ii) a wavelength-appropriate shutter or beam dump; (iii) emission indicators visible from all operator positions; (iv) monitored safe-torque-off contactors; and (v) a master safety PLC chain that can simultaneously remove permission from all sources. The enclosure must be classified for the highest-hazard wavelength present. If 450 nm blue lasers are added, enclosure panels, windows, and operator eyewear must be rated for visible-wavelength laser radiation in addition to 1070 nm IR.

### Summary Recommendations

For a desktop LPBF machine intended for aluminium alloy screening with future multi-laser capability, the evidence supports the following priority actions in the base design:

1. **Reserve a secondary window/port** (Ø40–60 mm) adjacent to the primary scanner window for a future auxiliary heating laser or diode-array head. This is the simplest and most broadly useful provision.

2. **Provide stiff, dowelled mounting rails** on the chamber lid for a gantry-mounted diode-array head or auxiliary laser, with adequate clearance from the recoater and scanner cone.

3. **Install a hardware-synchronised controller** with at least two XY2-100 channels and 8+ isolated TTL/analogue outputs, with deterministic timing and a shared emergency-blanking bus.

4. **Oversize the chiller** by approximately 1.5–2 kW beyond the initial single-laser requirement.

5. **Orient the gas cross-flow** perpendicular to the direction in which any future diode-array row would traverse, and ensure uniformity across the full Ø50 mm field.

6. **Consider 450 nm blue diode arrays** as the primary multi-beam upgrade path for aluminium, given the approximately 2.5× higher absorptivity at 450 nm versus 1060 nm (48% vs. 19% for AlSi12) (alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbeda pages 149-153, alsaddah2023multilaserpowderbed pages 149-153). However, retain the 1070 nm fibre laser as the primary melting source for production-representative processing conditions.

The most impactful first upgrade would be adding a **defocused auxiliary heating laser** (DLAH-style, 100–200 W at 915 nm or 808 nm), which requires minimal mechanical modification, directly addresses crack susceptibility and residual stress in experimental aluminium alloys, and has demonstrated 99.99% density and measurable microstructure control in Ti6Al4V (aydin2025theuseofa pages 156-160, aydin2025theuseof pages 156-160).
