Strength is not the issue: the joint holds 24× the worst separating load. What counts against screws for a robot is the rest: 3.9 N·m on an 18-8 screw in an 18-8 insert is the galling pair (Pencom, ASSDA), the cobot screwdrivers top out at 4–5 N·m and need magnetic screws (**vendor**, https://onrobot.com/storage/datasheets/screwdriver/datasheet_screwdriver_v1.7_en.pdf), and no source uses robot screwdriving to exchange fixtures. For a person swapping modules between campaigns, the screws are fine; give them an MoS₂- or silver-plated screw or a Nitronic 60 insert, and 1.5D of engaged thread instead of 6 mm.

Method: rated pull (**vendor**) × 0.65 for a 430 target (Goudsmit 60–75 %, SCHUNK 65 %) × the vendor's own force-vs-gap curve (Magnet-Schultz G MH, same pole size) × (1 + α_Br ΔT)² for the reversible loss (Adams chart). A Ø25 pole keeps 27 % at 0.1 mm and 7 % at 0.25 mm, so a powder grain or a 0.05 mm flatness error costs more than half the force. Vendors rate magnetic holding with a factor of 3 (Goudsmit, EN 13155) to 5 (Magswitch). Magnets are weak in shear (22–35 % of pull), so the dowels or a kinematic seat must take all lateral load. **For the module, magnets would need 3–12 units to reach 3× LC2 and are not a good fit**; even with LC2 removed it takes two Ø55 fail-safe magnets per module at zero gap.

- **Contact pressure (assumed 0.1 MPa).** The thermal FEA used h = 5,000 W/m²K (2 K·cm²/W) under the plate. Flexible graphite reaches well under 1 K·cm²/W at a few tenths of a MPa in published joint-conductance work (e.g. Marotta, Mazzuca & Norley, IEEE TCPT 28(1), 2005), so 0.1 MPa is taken as enough; no vendor curve was read in this pass. That sets the preload: S25 23 N, M50 173 N, L75 424 N. The recoater (10 N drag, 50 N strike, 10 mm above the face) needs much less.
- **(i) Bare bayonet:** only the plate's weight presses it on the foil (0.31 kPa for M50), and a strike can tip it until a lug meets the ledge, lifting the far edge up to 0.31 mm. It needs a preload.
- **(ii) Bayonet + disc springs:** Almen–László for McMaster 97125K111 (718 nickel, to 593 °C, $1.69) gives 453 N at its 0.10 mm working deflection (McMaster: 423 N), within 7 %, but 1,149 N flat where McMaster lists 601 N: catalogue heights are maxima and real discs have edge radii, so the ideal equation overstates the stiff end; size from the catalogue working point and keep s ≤ 0.10 mm. Stress at the working point σ_OM -579 MPa. The ramps (0.4 mm over 40°, μ 0.3) need 0.61 N·m to turn (self-locking: tan α = 0.079 < μ). The 6061 lugs see 28 MPa (M50) and 68 MPa (L75) in bending against 103 MPa (204 °C) and ~77 MPa (225 °C) after long exposure (ASM): L75 needs thicker lugs or a 17-4 cup (CalculiX H1).
- **(iii) Drawbar (machine-tool style):** a pull stud in the plate, six Ø4 balls in a conforming groove, the disc-spring stack and the release at the cold rod end. Ball contact p0 1,754 MPa in a 0.54-conformity groove (L75) vs 4,663 MPa on a flat, so the groove must conform; 17-4 H900 allows 1,872 MPa at first yield. Release push 656 N (a Ø32 cylinder at 6 bar gives ~480 N). The Ø4 drawbar carries 0.26 W (316) or 0.12 W (Ti-6Al-4V) down the rod: negligible. Only passive parts sit in the 225 °C block, which is how the research says to get past the 200 °C rating of every actuated zero-point module.
- **(iv) 3 × M4 316 screws (EOS style):** 1,383 N each at 1.7 N·m dry. Heating 20 → 200 °C adds 1,250 N per screw (Al grows 7.1 µm/m/K more than 316 over a 7.5 mm grip), so the screw reaches 300 MPa against ~160 MPa at 200 °C and the 6061 under the head 117 MPa against 103 MPa: both yield, the joint loses preload every cycle, and 6061-T6 overages at 200 °C. Add to that galling and a robot screwdriver in powder.
- **(v) Magnet:** no small switchable magnet is rated near 225 °C; Sm2Co17 would be needed, every plate would need a 430 insert (α 10.4 vs 23.6 µm/m/K in an Al plate), and the graphite foil is itself a 0.13 mm gap. One Ø25 pot gives ~16 N, so L75 needs ~33. Not viable.

- **Radial growth:** the block runs 88–132 K hotter than the head (M50), so at the screw circle it grows 42–62 µm (M50) relative to it. A slot or a clearance hole only helps if the block can actually slide; the friction under the screw head (μ·F) holds it unless the preload is small. Bending a guided M3 316 screw (Roark: M = 6EIδ/L², L = 15.5 mm) by that much gives 239–359 MPa, past its ~160 MPa yield at 200 °C. So either **keep the 316 screw's spring preload ≤ 55 N** (McMaster 9713K53, 302 stainless, ~49 N at 0.1 mm) so the block slides on the Macor first, or **use Ti-6Al-4V screws**, which take the full growth elastically (E 114 GPa, S_p ~650 MPa at 200 °C) and carry less heat.
- **Axial:** in a heat-up the block reaches temperature before the screw. Without a spring that stretches an M3 by ~30 µm, which is past its proof load; a disc spring under the head turns it into a few newtons. With Ti screws the steady-state growth is larger still (Ti expands less than Al), so Ti screws need the stiffer 718 spring (97125K111) and a set deflection.
- **Heat:** the thermal FEA (thermal_module.py, which models the screws since 2026-11-23) puts 3.72 W through the three solid Macor standoffs at build start (M50, no screws); through the bored Macor and the screws, 4.58 W with 316 screws and 3.97 W with Ti-6Al-4V, against 73 W of heater power. The hand estimate (screw conduction over 15.5 mm at the FEA's break ΔT) gave 5.29 and 4.04 W.
- **Macor bearing:** 1.2 MPa (302 spring) to 12.7 MPa (718 spring + 100 N) on the standoff annulus against 345 MPa compressive strength (Corning).
