\subsection{Problem and objectives}

Much of the Navy's seawater service runs on two kinds of copper alloy.
Copper-nickel alloys, 90-10 (UNS C70600) and 70-30 (UNS C71500), carry
seawater in piping, condensers, and heat exchangers, and nickel-aluminum
bronze (NAB, UNS C95800) is cast into propellers, valves, and pump
parts~\cite{milt16420,milb24480,cda222}. Both were developed for casting or wrought processing.
Directed energy deposition (DED), in which a laser melts a stream of powder
blown into a moving melt pool, can build large parts and restore worn or
damaged ones without a foundry~\cite{saboori2019,cao2019}. DED, however, cools each
layer from the melt within seconds and reheats it many times as later layers
pass, so it produces microstructures these alloys were never designed for. In
NAB, fast cooling can retain a martensitic phase that corrodes selectively in
seawater~\cite{liu2021,dobson2024}, and the Navy specification for NAB deposited
by wire-arc DED requires the same 6-hour anneal as castings~\cite{milprf32816}.
That step is costly for a large part and impractical for many repairs in
place. Navy specifications for printed copper alloys use existing
compositions~\cite{milprf32816,milprf32804,milprf32815}, and designing
compositions for laser deposition has only begun~\cite{jia2026}.

Designing a new composition runs through a chain of steps: a thermodynamic
calculation, a powder, deposited test specimens, microscopy, and property
tests (Fig.~\ref{fig:network}). The steps differ greatly in cost. A thermodynamic calculation takes
minutes, a powder lot a day, a DED session days including turnaround, and a
corrosion immersion weeks, because the protective film that gives
copper-nickel its resistance takes weeks to months to
mature~\cite{ma2015,ekerenam2018}. Experimental campaigns often send each
candidate through the whole chain, spending much of the budget on full tests
of candidates that a cheap early measurement could have ruled out.
Computational campaigns often work as a funnel: many simulations, then a few
candidates passed on to experiments. If the simulation misses a physical
effect, the funnel discards good candidates before any experiment can catch
the error.

The powder is a second obstacle. Single-stream blown-powder DED, the form in
use today, feeds a pre-alloyed powder from one hopper.
Each new composition therefore needs its own powder lot, in a size range that flows through the nozzle and that the melt pool catches~\cite{ancalmo2025,singh2020}.
The lot must also
fill the hopper, about 2~kg on the larger of the two systems this project
will use (Section~\ref{sec:task2}).
Industrial gas atomizers make powder in lots of tens of kilograms or more, so
new compositions are rarely tried. Our ultrasonic atomizer makes a lot of a new composition in a day
from one charge of about 1~kg, and 2~kg in two runs (225~cm$^3$ crucible; a 400~cm$^3$ one is available). Ultrasonic
atomizers have already made powder for blown-powder DED from NAB
propeller grinding chips~\cite{muller2023,uhlmann2024} and from copper cable
scrap~\cite{cabrioli2025}.
Both kept the existing compositions.
Making our own powder turns it from a purchase into a choice the search makes.
A small lot buys cheap deposits of many compositions on a small DED system near BYU, and a full lot goes only to the compositions worth building on the larger system.
Making the powder also lets the search design it along with the alloy.

This project asks a basic question. When the steps of a materials workflow
differ in cost by orders of magnitude, and results arrive at different times,
how should one choose the next measurement and the next candidate? It answers
the question on a concrete design problem: a
pre-alloyed copper alloy powder for single-stream blown-powder DED. The
powder must deposit without cracks or pores, on itself and on the alloys it
would repair, and the deposit must resist seawater without heat treatment at
least as well as 70-30 copper-nickel.
The project has five objectives:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Extend a cost-aware search method that learns from partial
evaluations to multiple objectives: dense and crack-free deposits, strength,
ductility, and resistance to corrosion in seawater.
\item Extend it to many pending experiments at once, with asynchronous
results.
\item Give it physics computed for the process: thermodynamic calculations
evaluated along the thermal history of a DED deposit. Measure what that
physics is worth.
\item Demonstrate the method on a physical workflow that designs copper alloy
powders for blown-powder DED, from powder to deposit, repair coupon, tensile
test, and seawater corrosion test.
Test whether its stated confidence matches what the deposits and tests show.
\item Measure how well a one-hour electrochemical screen predicts immersion
tests that take weeks, so that the long tests run only where they can change
a decision.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Copper alloys in naval seawater service}
\label{sec:naval-cu}

Navy specifications cover copper-nickel tube for the piping systems of
surface ships and submarines~\cite{milt16420} and NAB castings for seawater
service~\cite{milb24480}. Copper-nickel owes its resistance to a thin,
adherent film of corrosion products, mainly cuprous oxide with nickel and
iron, which builds up over weeks to months of exposure~\cite{ma2015}. In one
study the films on 90-10 tubes in natural seawater were still changing after
six months~\cite{ekerenam2018}. The iron content of the alloy changes the
makeup of the film~\cite{zhu2021}. NAB is a cast alloy of copper with about
9\% aluminum and 4 to 5\% each of iron and nickel (by weight). It is widely
used for valve bodies, pump casings, and impellers, with a large market in
submarine fleets~\cite{cda222}. Its microstructure of $\alpha$ grains and several
intermetallic $\kappa$ phases resists seawater well, but the $\alpha$ phase
in the lamellar $\alpha+\kappa$ eutectoid, and any retained $\beta$ phase,
corrode selectively, above all in crevices~\cite{wharton2005,culpan1979}.
Navy NAB castings therefore get a temper anneal at 1250 $\pm$
50\,\textdegree{}F (about 677\,\textdegree{}C) for at least 6 hours,
propellers excepted~\cite{milb24480}, which improves their corrosion
resistance~\cite{cda222}.

Printing changes these microstructures, and with them how the alloys
corrode~\cite{sander2018}. The microstructure of NAB follows its processing
route~\cite{orzolek2022}, and NAB has now been printed by laser
powder bed fusion~\cite{murray2020}, wire-arc deposition~\cite{ding2016,dharmendra2019},
and laser DED~\cite{liu2021,li2021}. Laser-deposited NAB contains martensitic
$\beta'$ along with $\alpha$ and $\kappa$ phases~\cite{liu2021,li2021} and
is much stronger than cast NAB~\cite{li2021}. Its corrosion depends on the
process. NAB from laser powder bed fusion formed stronger films than wrought
NAB but resisted localized corrosion worse, and annealing reversed
both~\cite{morshed2024}. Wire-arc NAB corroded in its residual $\beta$
phase~\cite{cai2023}. Whether annealing helps depends on the starting
microstructure. After 480 hours in salt solution, the same 700\,\textdegree{}C
anneal had lowered the corrosion current of cast NAB about threefold and raised that of sintered and hot isostatically pressed NAB by about as much.
The authors call the differences small~\cite{fayanas2025}.
As-built wire-arc NAB
resisted corrosion better than annealed coupons, in a test with unequal
immersion times~\cite{shahriari2023}. The substrate matters as well.
Laser-deposited NAB on stainless steel was attacked to a depth of
3.4\,$\mu$m in 21 days in salt solution where iron from the steel had diluted
it, and to 1.8\,$\mu$m over a nickel-chromium interlayer~\cite{zhao2022}. In
welded NAB, retained $\beta'$ in the heat-affected zone corroded selectively
in seawater~\cite{dobson2024}, a warning for repairs. Repair work has used an
NAB consumable for laser cladding of cast NAB~\cite{hyatt1998}. DED repair of
cast NAB has used a low-nickel copper alloy powder~\cite{yao2022a,yao2022b},
later blended in the melt pool with an aluminum alloy powder to vary the
aluminum content~\cite{yao2025}.

Copper-nickel depends on the process as well. In wire-arc 70-30, the
dendrite cores hold more nickel than the regions between them, and those
regions corrode first~\cite{song2024}. Powder-bed 70-30 has been studied
too~\cite{liu2024}, and its resistance depends on the laser
settings~\cite{nadimi2025}. At their best settings, both wire-arc and
powder-bed 70-30 have matched or beaten wrought plate in electrochemical
tests in salt solution~\cite{song2024,nadimi2025}.
Laser cladding of Cu-30Ni dates to 2005~\cite{adak2005}, and later
blown-powder DED of copper-nickel has mixed copper and nickel-alloy powders
in the melt pool to make graded layers and thermoelectric
alloys~\cite{karnati2019,yadav2020,williams2025}. The one corrosion study
we found of copper-nickel deposited by blown-powder laser DED is from 2011:
Cu-30Ni made by CO$_2$-laser direct metal deposition on wrought 70-30
plate~\cite{bhattacharya2011}. Its abstract and a later review's summary
of it~\cite{issc2012} disagree on whether the deposit or the plate corroded
faster; the review attributes the difference to porosity.
% Oct 28: the full text was not reachable (Elsevier sign-in). The abstract ranks
% the deposit as most resistant; ISSC 2012 says it corroded faster; a secondary
% source quotes ASTM G31 rates of 0.36 mpy (deposit) and 0.08 mpy (plate).
NAVSEA has written specifications for 70-30 copper-nickel made by
wire-arc DED and by laser powder bed fusion, and for NAB made by wire-arc DED.
The wire-arc ones are alternatives to castings, and all three keep the
existing compositions~\cite{milprf32816,milprf32804,milprf32815}. We found none for
either alloy made by blown-powder laser DED. In 2023 a Navy official said,
as Defense News reported, that for naval metals ``including copper-nickel,''
``more research is needed'' on how printing ``affects the metals' fatigue and
corrosion properties''~\cite{defensenews2023}. A composition chosen for casting is not
necessarily the best one for DED, and each composition and set of deposition
settings needs its own tests.

Corrosion tests of copper alloys follow the same cost pattern as the rest of
the workflow. Open-circuit potential, linear polarization, and impedance
measurements in substitute ocean water~\cite{astmD1141} take about an hour and
give a corrosion rate and a measure of the surface film~\cite{astmG59,astmG102}.
Immersion tests with mass loss~\cite{astmG31,astmG1} and cross-sections that
measure the depth of selective attack take weeks. Tests in flowing seawater,
which set the velocity limits used in piping design, take months. The search
can therefore treat corrosion the way it treats cracking and strength: a cheap
screen for many candidates and long tests for a few. The cheap screen can
mislead, though. An hour-long measurement on a fresh surface sees neither the
film that forms over weeks on copper-nickel nor slow selective attack in NAB.
On wrought 70-30 copper-nickel in synthetic seawater, the corrosion current
fell more than eightfold over the first ten days~\cite{yuan2007}, and welded
NAB corroded faster in natural seawater than in simulated
seawater~\cite{dobson2024b}.
A quick measurement can also rank wrongly.
At Carderock, scanning Kelvin probe measurements in air put the copper-rich $\alpha$ phase of C63200 NAB at the highest Volta potential, yet in salt solution that phase dissolved first and the $\kappa$ phases were untouched~\cite{iannuzzi2017}.
Knowing when the screen can be trusted is part of the research.

Supply and melting add one more objective. Nickel has been on the U.S. list
of critical minerals since 2022, and copper joined it in
2025~\cite{usgs2022,usgs2025}. Nickel also raises the melting range: 70-30
copper-nickel melts between about 1,171 and 1,238\,\textdegree{}C, 90-10
between 1,099 and 1,149\,\textdegree{}C, and NAB between 1,043 and
1,060\,\textdegree{}C~\cite{cdadata}. A replacement for 70-30 copper-nickel
that needs less nickel would use less of a critical mineral and would be
easier to atomize and deposit. The search will carry nickel content as an
objective to minimize~\cite{mulukutla2025} where the corrosion trade-offs allow.

\subsection{Scientific concepts}
\label{sec:concepts}

The search method treats the manufacturing workflow as a function
network~\cite{astudillo2021}. Each step gets its own statistical model (a
Gaussian process), which predicts the outputs of that step and how uncertain
they are. The models are linked in the same order as the steps
(Fig.~\ref{fig:network}), so the outputs of one step are inputs to the next
and uncertainty carries through the whole network. Because each step has its
own model, the network learns from partial evaluations, and a candidate can
stop at any step. Buathong et al.\ added to this network a cost-aware
acquisition function, the rule that chooses the next
experiment~\cite{buathong2024}. For each possible experiment, the rule
estimates how much the result is expected to improve the search, divides that
by the cost of the experiment, and runs the best one. In this project the
deposition step is itself split in two by cost. A small DED system at the
University of Utah, 45 miles from BYU, deposits tracks, walls, and small
blocks from grams to half a kilogram of powder on a day trip. A larger one at
the Colorado School of Mines, 500 miles away, builds tensile, corrosion, and
repair specimens from 2~kg lots, with results in weeks. Our rule treats the
two as linked steps with their own costs and pays for the larger one only
where the smaller one shows that a composition could change the outcome
(Section~\ref{sec:task2}).

The physics comes first, in the manner of integrated computational materials
engineering (ICME)~\cite{olson1997,nrc2008,arroyave2019,arroyave2026}: calculations decide where
experiments go, and experiments correct the calculations. Calculation of
phase diagrams (CALPHAD) in Thermo-Calc and pycalphad~\cite{andersson2002,otis2017}
gives the liquidus and freezing range of each composition and an index of how
prone it is to cracking as it solidifies~\cite{kou2015}. It also gives how
nickel, iron, and aluminum divide between the dendrite cores and the last
liquid to freeze, and which phases are stable at each temperature, including
the $\beta$ and $\kappa$ phases of the bronzes. Crack indices of this kind
have been checked against printed aluminum alloys, steels, and nickel
superalloys, but not copper alloys~\cite{jordan2026}.
DED adds a thermal history.
We model it with a moving point source of heat~\cite{rosenthal1946,pinkerton2015}, using the temperature-dependent properties of copper-nickel and NAB measured at NSWC Carderock~\cite{norkett2025,norkett2026a,norkett2026b,orzolek2023}, calibrated to measured melt-pool sizes.
The temperature dependence matters: in a thermo-mechanical model of laser hot-wire NAB deposition from Penn State and Carderock, constant room-temperature properties changed the predicted distortion, at times reversing its direction~\cite{hatala2021}.
For each set of deposition settings, the model gives the cooling rate through solidification and the peak temperatures that later passes impose on each layer.
The CALPHAD
calculations are then evaluated at those temperatures, to estimate which
phases can form as the deposit solidifies and which can form or dissolve as
it is reheated. These outputs enter the statistical models as inputs with
learned weights, so the search can discount any of them that proves
uninformative. The calculations run continuously on BYU high-performance
computing, and each new result is available to the next decision.

\subsection{Comparison with other approaches}

To our knowledge, no cost-aware search over linked models that can stop a
candidate at any step has been extended to multiple objectives or to
asynchronous results, and none has been demonstrated on a physical alloy
workflow. Published tests of the linked-model methods use test functions and
simulators~\cite{astudillo2021,buathong2024}. Multi-stage Bayesian
optimization for self-driving laboratories was tested on synthetic and
past data with one objective~\cite{torresi2026}. Multi-information-source alloy optimization treats measurements of unequal
cost as separate sources, not linked steps, even with asynchronous results, in
computational campaigns~\cite{khatamsaz2021,alvi2026,khatamsaz2024}. Standard multi-objective Bayesian optimization
handles competing properties and several experiments at
once~\cite{daulton2021,hastings2025,khatamsaz2022} but treats the whole chain as one costly experiment.
Closed-loop campaigns on printed alloys use one model and a fixed set of
measurements~\cite{talbot2026}. A search guided by model uncertainty has
chosen multi-principal element alloys deposited by DED,
scored on hardness~\cite{nelaturu2024}, and another study combined a cheap
analytical heat-flow model with costlier finite-element simulations to map
DED settings~\cite{menon2022}. Neither linked the steps nor their costs.

Alloys designed for printing by computation, such as an
oxide-dispersion-strengthened NiCoCr alloy~\cite{smith2023} and
high-strength aluminum alloys~\cite{thapliyal2020,taheri2025}, were designed
and then printed as single compositions. A nickel superalloy has been
designed with a neural network for laser repair~\cite{markanday2022}.
Machine learning has refined powder-bed settings for 70-30 copper-nickel at
the Naval Postgraduate School~\cite{sparrow2026}, and Bayesian optimization
has tuned a gas atomizer~\cite{tamura2021}. Both tune the process for one
fixed alloy. Printed copper alloys for seawater have used the cast or wrought
compositions~\cite{milprf32816,milprf32804,milprf32815}, and their corrosion
studies characterize one alloy and one process at a
time~\cite{morshed2024,cai2023,song2024,nadimi2025}. A 2026 study adjusted
the composition of NAB for laser additive manufacturing to improve its
as-deposited strength and corrosion resistance~\cite{jia2026}, and adding
0.1\% titanium to wire-arc NAB lowered its corrosion current by
16\%~\cite{zhang2026}. Both show the payoff of designing for the process.

Autonomous and high-throughput corrosion work has used a scanning droplet
cell to screen coatings and has run fast screens before slower, more thorough
tests~\cite{decost2022,sur2023}, but without deciding, candidate by
candidate, when a long test is worth its cost. An ONR-supported perspective
on autonomous laboratories built around additive manufacturing sets out a
ten-year road map and takes as its worked example a naval brass deposited by
DED, screened by a surrogate test before erosion-corrosion
tests~\cite{collins2026}. The workflow proposed here is a working instance of
that idea: real step costs, DED bought by the session, and audited
screens. This project combines the pieces: the linked network, the
cost of each step, physics computed for the process, multiple objectives
including corrosion, and asynchronous results. It then tests the combination
on real deposits.

\subsection{Method advances}

\subsubsection{Advance 1: multi-objective optimization}
The published cost-aware method optimizes a single objective. A copper alloy
for DED repair must balance deposit quality (dense, crack-free, and sound at
the fusion line with the part it repairs), strength, ductility, and corrosion
resistance in seawater, with no weighting known in advance. We will extend
the cost-aware rule to multiple objectives. In place of the expected
improvement in one objective, it will use the expected growth of the set of
best trade-offs (the Pareto front), measured by the
hypervolume~\cite{daulton2021} and divided by cost as before.
The difficulty is that each cheap measurement informs some objectives strongly
and others weakly. Single tracks show cracking and dilution well but say
little about strength or corrosion, and a polarization measurement says much
about corrosion rate and nothing about strength. The rule has to account for
that through the network. We will derive the rule, check it against
brute-force estimates on small problems, and time it on
BYU high-performance computing.

\subsubsection{Advance 2: pending experiments and asynchronous results}
The published method chooses one experiment at a time and waits for its
result. In this workflow a DED session deposits many specimens, microscopy
results arrive days later, immersion tests run for weeks, and thermodynamic
calculations finish in minutes. We will extend the rule to batches of experiments
with asynchronous results, so that new experiments are chosen while others
are still pending. Pending experiments will enter the models as
``fantasies,'' outcomes drawn from the current predictions, a standard way to
avoid choosing the same experiment twice~\cite{snoek2012,khatamsaz2024}. We will also test how
stable the choices are when a late result contradicts early ones. For
example, a block that looked dense on its surface may show cracks under the
microscope, or an alloy that passed the polarization screen may show
selective attack after a month of immersion.

\subsubsection{Advance 3: physics computed for the process}
Standard CALPHAD inputs describe equilibrium, or solidification without
diffusion in the solid, and ignore the thermal history of the
process~\cite{debroy2018}. We will add inputs computed along the DED thermal
history (Section~\ref{sec:concepts}): cooling rate through solidification, the peak
temperatures of reheating, and the phases that CALPHAD allows at those
temperatures. Hypothesis H3 tests whether these inputs make the models'
predictions more reliable than equilibrium inputs, and whether equilibrium
inputs beat data alone. The answer measures what incomplete, process-aware
physics is worth inside a search, which sets how much simulation a campaign
should buy before it buys experiments.

\subsubsection{Advance 4: a physical workflow from powder to repair}
We will demonstrate the advances on a physical workflow (Fig.~\ref{fig:network})
in which the search chooses the composition, the atomizer settings, and the
deposition settings, and decides at each step whether a candidate goes on.
After microscopy, specimens go to tensile tests or to the corrosion screen,
and selected candidates to immersion. Candidates that deposit well get a
repair coupon on cast NAB or wrought copper-nickel plate, checked for cracks,
dilution, and hardness across the fusion line, after pre-placed tracks on
them at Utah. It has real scatter between deposits, outputs measured
on micrographs, and inputs that enter late.

\subsubsection{Advance 5: checking what the screens discard}
A cheap screen saves money only if it does not discard good candidates, and
Section~\ref{sec:naval-cu} shows that a short corrosion screen can also pass
bad ones. Each round, the search will send a small random share of the
candidates it would stop, about one in ten, on to the next step anyway. A few
candidates just either side of a screen's cutoff go on as well, where its
errors matter most. The audited candidates measure how often each screen is
wrong, and they keep the models from learning only from candidates that the
screens favored. Each year we will report, with confidence intervals, how
often the one-hour corrosion screen ranks candidates as the 30-day immersion
does and how often it rejects one that immersion would have passed.

\subsection{Hypotheses and rejection criteria}

\textbf{H1 (cost).} The cost-aware, multi-objective search that handles
pending experiments will reach a target set of trade-offs at lower total
cost than standard multi-objective Bayesian optimization, which carries
every candidate through the full chain. H1 will be rejected if, across
matched campaigns with the same budget, the median cost to reach the target is
not lower than that of the full-chain baseline.

\textbf{H2 (calibration).} The predicted probabilities that a candidate will
deposit dense and crack-free, fuse to the repair substrate without cracks,
and stay close to its intended composition will match the observed rates on
held-out deposits. H2 will be rejected if observed success rates differ from
predicted probabilities by more than 10 percentage points.

\textbf{H3 (process-aware physics).} CALPHAD outputs evaluated along the DED
thermal history will improve that calibration over CALPHAD outputs at
equilibrium, which will in turn improve it over models trained on data
alone. H3 will be rejected if the process-aware inputs do not reduce
calibration error on unseen deposits compared with the equilibrium inputs.

\textbf{H4 (pending results).} Choosing new experiments while others are
pending will reach the target in less calendar time than waiting for each
batch to finish, at equal spend. H4 will be rejected if, in median calendar
time, working around pending results is no faster than waiting.

\textbf{H5 (corrosion screen).} Corrosion rates, breakdown potentials, and
impedance from the one-hour screen, together with composition and
microstructure, will predict which candidates pass a 30-day immersion test.
To pass, a candidate needs a mass loss no greater than that of the 70-30
copper-nickel reference and selective attack no deeper than in the cast NAB reference. H5 will be
rejected if, among tested candidates (audited ones included), those that the
model ranks in the top half pass no more often than those in the bottom half.

The targets, baselines, budgets, and number of matched campaigns will be fixed and posted before each test starts \todo{confirm a public pre-registration site, e.g., OSF}.
H1 and H4 are tested first on simulated and retrospective benchmarks, where many matched campaigns are affordable, and then in live campaigns, where only a few are.

\subsubsection{Task 2: experimental platform (Ph.D. student 2)}
\label{sec:task2}
We will make powder for each candidate in our laboratory.
Oxygen-free copper bar is machined into cups that hold the charge.
Nickel, iron, manganese, aluminum, and other additions are weighed in to 0.1~mg as pure metals or copper master alloys.
Each run melts about 1.1~kg; the first copper run, 1,080~g of NAB, gave an estimated 97\% powder (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}). Minor additions that come as powder are dispensed into the cup by our
powder doser (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}), which weighs and logs each dose, so every lot carries a record of what went into it. The charge is
melted in a graphite
crucible in the induction module of the ultrasonic atomizer (AMAZEMET
rePOWDER) and poured through a 0.5~mm nozzle onto a vibrating plate, where it
breaks into droplets.
Nickel and iron react with graphite.
The crucible is therefore coated with boron nitride, and the sealing rod, nozzle, and other parts the melt touches are ceramic or ceramic-coated.
NAB has already been atomized from a
graphite crucible on a rePOWDER~\cite{muller2023}. Many alloys wet bare carbon-carbon
composite poorly~\cite{choma2026}, and a bare carbon-fiber plate left fibers in
NAB powder~\cite{uhlmann2024}, so the plate is tungsten-coated carbon fiber,
as in our first NAB run. The first part of
each pour heats the plate until the melt wets it, and atomization is then
continuous. With such plates on our model of atomizer, the manufacturer
turned 80 to 90\% of each charge of a tin bronze (CuSn8) into
powder~\cite{choma2026}.

The median droplet size follows the wavelength of capillary waves on the
plate, so it falls as the frequency to the power $-2/3$~\cite{lang1962}. The
plates on our unit vibrate at 40~kHz, where the Lang relation gives a median
near 47~$\mu$m for a copper melt. Measured powders are coarser. NAB atomized
at 40~kHz had a median of 98~$\mu$m~\cite{uhlmann2024}, about twice that
estimate, and much of the CuSn8 powder made at 40~kHz on our model fell
between 63 and 150~$\mu$m~\cite{choma2026}. Both suit the DED system
described below, which takes powder of about 50 to 150~$\mu$m, sometimes up
to 200~$\mu$m, and is usually fed cuts of 45 to 105 or 100 to 200~$\mu$m.
Published blown-powder work on bronzes used 45 to 106 and 45 to 150~$\mu$m
cuts~\cite{li2021,pi2025}. Each lot is sieved into the two cuts that system
is fed. We then measure its size distribution by sieve analysis and from SEM
images, and its flow and apparent density with a Hall
funnel~\cite{astmB213,astmB212}. SEM also shows its shape, satellites, and
stray plate fibers. NSL Analytical, an accredited laboratory, certifies the
composition, oxygen, and carbon of every lot sent to Mines, after a full
survey of the first copper lots.

Along with the composition, the search chooses the atomizer settings: the
melt temperature, the pour pressures, and the plate amplitude. The share of
each lot in each cut is an output of the powder step, and with the gas flows
and the laser spot, the size distribution sets how much of the powder the
melt pool catches~\cite{ancalmo2025}. The powder, not only the alloy, is thus
designed for blown-powder DED.

We buy DED as machine time at two facilities, and the search treats them as
two steps of different cost (Fig.~\ref{fig:network}). Neither facility is a
subawardee. For the small step we use an InssTek MX-Lab in the laboratory
of Professor Z.~Zak Fang at the University of Utah, 45 miles from BYU, which
his group has offered to the project. It has a 500~W fiber laser, 150~mm of
travel on each axis, control of melt-pool height, and six powder feeders that
can each be run separately~\cite{insstek,utahded}. \todo{confirm with Fang: the laser power (300 or 500~W), the rate, the
smallest powder charge per feeder, the argon chamber and any preheat, and
that a BYU student runs it after training}. We start every composition
here, spreading powder from a first lot in grooves machined in plates,
including cast NAB and wrought copper-nickel plate, and melting it at several
laser powers and speeds. These pre-placed tracks use a few grams of powder each and show
cracking, porosity, dilution, and fusion to the repair substrates. They
differ from blown-powder tracks in heat input and in how the powder reaches
the melt pool, so the models treat them as a cheaper, less faithful
measurement. Once its tracks are sound, a composition gets blown-powder
deposits from one 0.5~kg lot. Single tracks at several laser powers, travel
speeds, and powder feed rates show melt-pool size, dilution, cracking, and
the share of the powder caught in the melt pool. Thin walls show cracking
and heat buildup, and small blocks show density and porosity. The heat flow model is
calibrated separately for each kind of track, and the audit of Advance~5
measures how often the tracks mislead.

For the large step we buy sessions on a FormAlloy X2 blown-powder laser DED
system at the Colorado School of Mines, run by facility staff at its external rates for
machine time and labor.
A typical session, four 25~mm cubes, takes about 3~hours of machine time and 2~hours of labor.
The staff plan on the melt pool catching about half the powder fed and need about 2~kg of each powder to fill the hoppers of the X2.
Since 2~kg takes two atomizer runs, only compositions whose small blocks at Utah reach 99\% relative density are scaled up.
Their sessions at Mines build blocks for sub-size tensile bars (ASTM E8/E8M) and corrosion coupons at the chosen settings, and repair coupons on thick cast NAB and wrought copper-nickel plate (Advance~4).
Fusing the first layer to such a large heat sink needs more laser power than the MX-Lab has; the X2 has 1~kW~\cite{adaptmines}. Commercial 70-30 copper-nickel and NAB powders, deposited
at both facilities, are the baselines \todo{confirm commercial powder
sources, at least 2~kg of each; Mines can also supply powder at cost}. The
search decides which compositions go to Mines at all.

At BYU, selected deposits are examined by SEM and by electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) for grain size, texture, and phase fractions, including martensitic $\beta'$ in the bronzes.
EDS and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry measure their composition, since manganese and aluminum can be lost to evaporation and oxidation~\cite{debroy2018}.
Replicate tracks and blocks at fixed settings measure deposit-to-deposit
scatter, and image analysis scripts turn micrographs into porosity, crack
length, dilution, grain size, and phase fractions with stated uncertainty.

At the end of each year, the year's leading composition is gas atomized at
Ames National Laboratory by the powder group of Dr.~Jordan A. Tiarks, an
unfunded collaborator with state-of-the-art facilities and deep expertise in gas
atomization. One run a year is budgeted as a purchased service. The
gas-atomized lot is deposited at Mines beside the ultrasonic lot of the same
composition, so the project measures what changes between the two routes.
That lot also supplies the finalists' replicate and repair builds and goes to
Carderock in the form a supplier would make.

Corrosion tests follow the same staging (Fig.~\ref{fig:screen}). The screen runs on the
liquid-handling gantry in our laboratory (CubXL, Ursa Laboratories), which is
already driven by scripts, so the search can schedule it like any other
step. The gantry fills small O-ring wells, 3 to 4~mm across, clamped on
polished faces, with substitute ocean water~\cite{astmD1141}, and a
potentiostat dedicated to the loop measures open-circuit potential, linear
polarization, impedance, and cyclic polarization~\cite{astmG59,astmG61}.
ASTM G61 is written for iron-, nickel-, and cobalt-based alloys, so the
project will report how its procedure behaves on deposited copper alloys.
A potentiostat lent by a BYU colleague was integrated into the gantry's automated
workflow in October 2026, although the screen itself has not yet been run; the
budget buys a dedicated instrument with impedance capability.
Small cells can add crevice attack at the seal and shift breakdown
potentials~\cite{frankel1998}, so each spot is photographed before and after,
and the wells are checked against a conventional 1~cm$^2$ flat cell on the
references. Several wells fit on one specimen, so a repair coupon is measured
in the deposit, at the fusion line, and in the heat-affected zone of the
substrate, where welded NAB was attacked selectively~\cite{dobson2024}. The
gantry then pipettes the used electrolyte of each well into a tube for
inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, which shows which elements left
the surface, for example aluminum from NAB or nickel from copper-nickel.

re-measure specimens left immersed for days at little extra cost, a step
between the one-hour screen and the 30-day immersion.

Candidates that the search selects get 30-day immersion tests in capped
vials on the deck of the gantry. It draws a few microliters of each solution
at about 1, 3, 10, and 30 days for the same analysis. The metal released
over time then enters the models as an intermediate result between the
one-hour screen and the final mass loss. The coupons are weighed before and
after~\cite{astmG31,astmG1} and sectioned to measure the depth of selective
attack.
Repair coupons are immersed as galvanic couples, the deposit on its cast NAB or wrought copper-nickel substrate with the fusion line exposed and the area ratio recorded, and are sectioned across the fusion line to find the deepest attack.
The leading candidates get crevice and longer immersion tests \todo{confirm the durations, and natural or substitute seawater}.

The search space was chosen the way the laboratory chose its aluminum palette: a literature review of each candidate element, then a check of its coverage in the CALPHAD database.
Fifty elements from the periodic table (Fig.~\ref{fig:space}) were reviewed one at a time, including every element that appears in a commercial copper alloy, a copper alloy or welding filler specification, a deoxidizing practice, or the atomizer's crucible and plate materials.
Each review asked four questions.
Does the element help seawater resistance, deposit soundness, or repair?
What sets its upper limit?
Can it be melted and atomized below 1,300\,\textdegree{}C, where the atomizer typically runs, without attacking the crucible or plate?
Does Thermo-Calc's copper database (TCCU7~\cite{tccu7}) assess its systems?
Eight elements are searched from
the start (Table~\ref{tab:space}), with copper as the balance at no less
than 65\% and a calculated liquidus no higher than 1,250\,\textdegree{}C.
From Year~2 we will also test five microalloying additions (Table~\ref{tab:space}).

Rules tied to the aluminum content keep each candidate near known
metallurgy. Above 7\% aluminum, nickel must at least equal iron, as it does
in the nominal composition of Navy NAB castings~\cite{milb24480}. Below 1.5\%
aluminum, iron stays at or below 2.3\%, the upper limit of the iron-rich
70-30 grade C71640, so that it remains in solid solution~\cite{astmB111}.
Chromium and niobium are used only where aluminum is at most 3\%, and silicon
above 0.1\% only with niobium, as in cast 70-30
copper-nickel~\cite{astmB369}. No fixed formula limits retained $\beta$,
which the process-aware CALPHAD inputs predict (Advance~3). The space
includes the region between the two families,
copper-nickel-aluminum alloys such as the high-strength
MARINEL~\cite{campbell2002}, where low-nickel candidates may lie. Beryllium
and cadmium (toxic powder and fume), zinc (it evaporates in the melt pool and
dezincifies in seawater), and the refractory metals (immiscible, and far
above the melting limit) are excluded. Lead, sulfur, phosphorus, carbon,
bismuth, and the other impurities are held at or below the welding-grade
limits for copper-nickel~\cite{awsA57}. All the searched elements but iron
are on the 2025 U.S. list of critical minerals~\cite{usgs2025}. Nickel
dominates by mass and is carried as an objective.

If successful, the project will produce:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}

asynchronous results.
\item A measured answer to how much a staged, cost-aware search saves over

\item A measured value of process-aware physics: how much CALPHAD evaluated
along the DED thermal history improves predictions over equilibrium
calculations and over data alone.
\item Corrosion data for copper alloys deposited by DED, and a measured link

weeks.

Results that reject a hypothesis are also new knowledge.
If H1 fails, the data will show which step's cost or noise erased the advantage.
If H3 fails, they will show which process effects the calculations missed, and if H5 fails, which microstructural features the screen missed.

\subsection{Future Naval Relevance}

\textbf{ONR S\&T Focus Areas.} The primary focus area is Manufacturing,
research area Integrated Manufacturing and Sustainment Technologies. The
secondary focus area is Materials and Electronics, research area Sustainable
and Operationally Robust Materials. Within Code 332 the project fits the
program Materials \& Processes for Additive Manufacturing~\cite{onram}. That
program lists five thrusts: ``(i) rapid qualification frameworks; (ii)
tailored AM materials and processes; (iii) large scale AM; (iv) additive
repair and (v) AM and digital logistics''~\cite{onram}. Among its research
challenges are to ``develop and validate computational approaches for AM
materials and processes for engineering applications'' and to ``develop
large-scale AM processes for naval materials of interest''~\cite{onram}. The
project serves
thrusts (ii) to (iv) with an alloy designed for a large-scale process that
also repairs, and thrust (i) with a search method that decides which test to
run next and states its confidence in the outcome. It is ICME for additive
manufacturing, the approach that the ONR Structural Metals program calls ``an
efficient rigorous framework for optimizing processing and manufacturing
schemes''~\cite{onrsm}: computation first, and experiments where they change
a decision.
It also fits two other Code 332 programs.
Corrosion Science and Corrosion Control Technologies asks for a ``fundamental understanding of corrosion mechanism in additive manufactured (AM) alloys''~\cite{onrcorr}, and Computer-Aided Materials Design lists high-throughput screening concepts and informatics among its thrusts~\cite{onrcamd}.

\textbf{Copper alloys, castings, and repair.} The slow production of large
castings ``has historically gated progress and throughput for both the
Columbia and Virginia submarine programs''~\cite{navsup2026}, and the Navy is
replacing castings with printed parts. In August 2026 it set out ``a
streamlined, repeatable technical pathway to substitute legacy cast and
wrought parts with 3D-printed metallic materials''~\cite{navy2026ppd}. A
printed copper-nickel deck drain, made ``to provide an alternative to
traditional copper-nickel castings,'' has been installed on a
new-construction submarine~\cite{hii2023,nns2025}, and the Navy Maritime
Industrial Base program has made copper-nickel ship-service ball valves by
wire-arc deposition~\cite{dvids2025}. These parts use the cast compositions,
as does ONR-funded research on wire-arc 70-30 copper-nickel~\cite{song2024}.
Suppose a copper alloy designed for blown-powder DED resisted seawater as deposited.
A shipyard or depot could then build a part that now waits on a casting, or restore a worn part in place without the heat treatment the cast alloys need.
ONR's corrosion program asks for this kind of work: ``modify the
chemistry of feed stock materials to enhance AM processing and corrosion
resistance of additive-manufactured alloys''~\cite{onrcorr}.

\textbf{The longer horizon.} The project designs for single-stream,
pre-alloyed powder, which existing DED systems can use now. Blended powders
separate in the feed stream and are caught by the melt pool at different
rates, which shifts the deposited composition~\cite{li2018,chen2020,karnati2019};
a pre-alloyed lot carries one measured composition into every
deposit~\cite{chen2020}. The same models
can later choose blends of elemental and master-alloy powders fed from
several hoppers and alloyed in the melt pool, which would let a depot make a
new composition without a new powder lot. The guide of Task~4 will describe
that extension. The method needs only a network of steps, their costs, and
their measurements, so it also applies to wire-arc and laser-wire deposition
and to other Navy alloys, including titanium. A plasma melting module for our
atomizer (not part of this request) would extend the powder work to alloys
that melt above the limit of the induction module.

\subsection{Gold Standard Science}

Executive Order 14303 lists nine tenets of Gold Standard
Science~\cite{eo14303}: reproducible, transparent, communicative of error and
uncertainty, collaborative and interdisciplinary, and skeptical of its
findings and assumptions. Such science is also structured for falsifiability
of hypotheses, subject to unbiased peer review, accepting of negative results
as positive outcomes, and without conflicts of interest. The plan follows
each point. Each hypothesis above has a stated rejection
criterion, and we fix the targets and baselines before each test. Every
prediction carries an uncertainty, and the calibration tests (H2, H3) check
whether that uncertainty is honest. The random audit of Advance~5 tests the
assumptions behind each screen. Replicate deposits and
reference specimens measure scatter so that it is not mistaken for a real
effect.
Code, data, and models are released each year, so others can rerun every analysis.
Results that reject a hypothesis will be published like any other result.
The PI's record supports this: he co-authored a review of self-driving laboratories and led open benchmark and software projects~\cite{mptimesplit,baird2024}.

\section{Management Approach}
\label{sec:mgmt}

This is a single-PI project with no co-PIs or subawards. The PI will lead the
method development, set the campaign plans, and supervise all students.
Ph.D.\ student 1 will lead Task 1 (methods, physics inputs, benchmarks,
software). Ph.D.\ student 2 will lead Task 2 (powder, deposits, microscopy,
tensile and corrosion tests). An undergraduate researcher will help make and
check powder, prepare specimens, and run the corrosion screen. Both Ph.D.\
students share Task 3, so the methods are tested on the data they will face.

DED time is bought from the University of Utah and the Colorado School of Mines, and any outside tests from a commercial laboratory.
None is a subaward.
A BYU student runs the Utah sessions after training by the Utah group, and staff at Mines run each session there, both to the settings the search chooses.
Dr.~Jordan A. Tiarks of Ames National Laboratory is an unfunded collaborator for the gas atomization runs; Fang and Webster are unfunded collaborators too, and all three are listed under current and pending support.
Our budget buys about twelve half-day sessions a year at Utah, several compositions to a session, 3, 7, and 4 sessions at Mines in Years 1 to 3, and one gas atomization run a year \todo{the Utah line is an allowance until Fang sets a rate}.
All other work is at BYU: the PI's atomizer laboratory, the BYU Electron Microscopy Facility (SEM, EDS, EBSD), and
BYU high-performance computing. The team meets weekly to review new results and
choose the next experiments. A shared database records every step, so the
search always sees the latest results.

\textbf{Risks and responses.} If the multi-objective rule is too slow to
compute, we will use a cheaper approximation and measure the loss on the
simulated benchmark. If deposit scatter is large, we will add replicates and
enter the scatter in the models directly. If a composition cannot
be atomized, for example because it melts too close to the limit of the
module, the search records the failure as a result, since predicting which
compositions can be made is part of the task. If the coating of a plate
wears and the melt stops wetting it, we replace the plate or move to a
molybdenum-alloy plate~\cite{ciftcli2025}. If melts that contain nickel or iron
still pick up carbon, which the carbon analysis of each lot will show, we will
recoat the crucible before every run instead of every few. If a
lot flows poorly, the search sees that at the powder step and can change the
atomizer settings before paying for a deposit. The pre-placed tracks on cast NAB and
copper-nickel plate show early whether the first layer fuses to thick plate.
If it does not, we will preheat the repair coupons or make them thinner. If 500~W
at Utah cannot melt a composition well, its tracks move to Mines at higher
cost, and the search learns which compositions need the larger
system. If making 2~kg lots slows the campaigns, the Utah step will carry
more of the screening, and fewer compositions will be scaled up. If either
facility becomes unavailable, the other takes its work at its known cost.
If DED turnaround is slow, the rule for
pending results is designed for that case. If the electrochemical screen
predicts the immersion tests poorly (H5 rejected), the search will rely more
on immersion, at higher cost, and the audited candidates will show which
features the screen missed.

Professor of Mechanical Engineering at BYU since December 2025, his first
tenure-track position. Before BYU, he was Director of Training and Programs

Consortium, University of Toronto, where he built its development
laboratory with about \$600,000 of equipment and helped direct the
organization's growth from 6 to 60 full-time employees (35 staff scientists)
in his three years there.

developed Honegumi, which helps experimentalists set up Bayesian
optimization, consulted for Meta on the version 1.0 release of the Ax
platform, and organized an international Bayesian optimization hackathon. He
wrote the structural materials section of the \emph{Chemical Reviews}
survey of self-driving laboratories and led a review of low-cost
self-driving laboratories. His M.S. work used Bayesian inference with
propagated uncertainty to model grain boundaries. He also designed a
semi-automated induction furnace for temperatures up to
2500\,\textdegree{}C~\cite{baird2026furnace}. His electrochemistry experience
includes battery research, among it lithium-oxygen batteries at NASA Langley
Research Center, hydrogen embrittlement studies, and electrochemical
metallographic preparation. At BYU he supervises about 20 undergraduate
researchers and one M.S. student and has set up the
CALPHAD workflow, the atomizer, the powder doser, and the powder-to-part

\section{Data Management Plan}
\label{sec:dmp}

\textbf{Types of data, software, and other materials.} We will produce
thermodynamic and heat flow calculation results, and atomizer settings with
records of powder size, flow, density, oxygen, and carbon. Settings and
session logs cover the pre-placed tracks and DED deposits. Microscopy gives
optical and electron micrographs, EDS spectra, and EBSD maps. Mechanical
tests give tensile and hardness data, and corrosion tests give polarization
and impedance data, immersion mass losses, and cross-sections of corrosion
attack. The software products are the search software, benchmark problems,
and trained models.

\textbf{How the data will be acquired.} Calculations run on BYU
high-performance computing. We make powder in our laboratory. DED
sessions are bought as a service (Section~\ref{sec:mgmt}). Microscopy,
corrosion screens, immersion tests, and mechanical tests are done at BYU.

\textbf{Time and location.} Data are collected at BYU and at the two DED
facilities (University of Utah and Colorado School of Mines) from April 2027
to March 2030.

\textbf{Processing.} Scripts kept under version control turn raw images and
test records into derived quantities (porosity, crack length, dilution, grain
size, phase fractions, strength, corrosion rate, attack depth) with stated
uncertainty. Each derived value links to its raw files.

\textbf{File formats and naming.} Open formats: CSV and JSON for tables, TIFF
for images, and the instruments' native formats alongside open exports.
Each sample receives an identifier that encodes powder lot, deposition
session, and specimen, used in every file name.

\textbf{Quality assurance and control.} Replicate deposits measure scatter.
We measure reference specimens of wrought 70-30 copper-nickel and cast NAB
in every round of corrosion screening. Scripts are tested, and the same
benchmarks are rerun before each software release.

\textbf{Existing data.} The retrospective benchmarks use our own
aluminum data from 2026, with its origin recorded in the metadata.

\textbf{Standards.} Metadata will record every step from charge preparation
through atomization, sieving, storage, transport, deposition, and testing.
For each step it will hold the composition, powder properties, process and
instrument settings, dates, operators, and the ASTM method behind each test,
in a documented schema informed by the one used by NIST AM
Bench~\cite{ambench2022schema}, which covers builds, specimens, and
measurements; we extend it to powder making and corrosion tests.

\textbf{Preservation.} Data and software will be deposited in public
repositories at each annual release and kept for at least 10 years.
Data go to Zenodo or figshare with a DOI, as the PI's earlier datasets did~\cite{mptimesplit}, and the property databases of the finalists also go to Materials Commons, where Carderock released the raw files of its HSLA-80 database~\cite{fisher2022mc}.
Code goes to GitHub.

\textbf{Balance of value and cost.} We will keep raw session logs and
micrographs in full. Very large intermediate files that scripts can
regenerate will not be archived.

\textbf{Release restrictions.} Results are expected to be fundamental
research. Any data that cannot be cleared for public release under DoD
Instruction 5230.09 will not be made public.
