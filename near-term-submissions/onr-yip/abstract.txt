Cost-Aware Selection of Experiments for Printable, Corrosion-Resistant High-Strength Aluminum Alloys

Research problem and objectives. Developing a new alloy for laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) runs through a chain of steps whose costs differ by orders of magnitude: a thermodynamic calculation takes minutes, a printed build takes days, and a corrosion exposure takes weeks. Most campaigns send each candidate through every step, so much of the budget goes to full tests of candidates that a cheap early measurement could have ruled out. Most high-strength aluminum alloys crack when printed, each change in composition requires new laser settings, and printing changes how the alloy corrodes, so every new composition repeats a long test program before a printed part can be used at sea. This project asks how to choose the next measurement and the next candidate when steps differ in cost and results arrive at different times. Its objectives are to extend a cost-aware search method that learns from partial results to several competing objectives (free of cracks and pores, strong, ductile, and resistant to corrosion in seawater), to extend it to many experiments in progress at once, to demonstrate both on a physical workflow for printable high-strength aluminum alloys, and to measure how well a one-hour electrochemical screen predicts corrosion tests that take days to weeks.

Technical approach. Each step of the workflow (thermodynamic calculation, powder made by ultrasonic atomization, printed specimens, microscopy, tensile tests, and corrosion tests) gets its own statistical model, and the models are linked in the order of the steps so that uncertainty carries through the network and a candidate can stop at any step. A cost-aware rule chooses the experiment with the largest expected gain per dollar. The project will extend this rule to several objectives, to batches whose results arrive out of order, and to a workflow that branches into tensile and corrosion tests. A small random share of the candidates that the screens would stop will be tested anyway, to measure how often the screens are wrong. Five hypotheses with stated rejection criteria test the cost savings, the calibration of predictions, the value of physics inputs from thermodynamic calculations and of working around pending results, and the corrosion screen. Targets and baselines are fixed before each test, and software, data, and models are released each year.

Anticipated outcomes. If successful, the project will deliver the first cost-aware method of this kind for competing properties and staggered results; a measured answer to how much a staged, cost-aware search saves over full-chain testing on a real alloy workflow; corrosion data for printed high-strength aluminum compositions and a measured link between a fast electrochemical screen and long exposure tests; and new printable high-strength aluminum compositions with their laser settings, tensile properties, and corrosion behavior, using less scandium where the trade-offs allow.

Impact on Department of War capabilities. The Navy sustains ships and aircraft that operate in seawater, and printing replacement parts shortens supply lines for parts that are obsolete or slow to procure. Printed aluminum parts for use at sea must resist corrosion in their printed condition, and the sensitization of aluminum-magnesium superstructure alloys shows the cost of learning about corrosion in service. The project will provide printable high-strength aluminum compositions with measured corrosion behavior and a method that lowers the cost of qualifying each new composition by running long tests only where they change a decision. The method applies to other alloys, printers, and test sequences at Navy laboratories and depots. The project will train two doctoral students and undergraduates in printing, corrosion testing, and autonomous experimentation.
