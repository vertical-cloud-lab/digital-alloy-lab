Yes. # Powder-collector precedents for the rePOWDER failure

**The closest published protection against the reported failure is a separate catch can *upstream* of the normal powder collector.** In an Ames Laboratory magnesium atomizer, the researchers suspended a can near the bottom of the chamber specifically to catch metal if the melt descended as a stream rather than atomizing. They also investigated an oil bath because large particles might still be liquid on reaching the chamber bottom and form splats. These are direct precedents for treating gross melt arrival as a design case, not merely for choosing a hotter-rated receiver seal. Steinmetz, *Investigation of a Novel Passivation Technique for Gas Atomized Magnesium Powders* (2011), https://doi.org/10.31274/etd-180810-3181. (steinmetz2011investigationofa pages 38-42, steinmetz2011investigationofa pages 42-46)

## Findings most relevant to the proposed replacement

**Intercept hot shot before it reaches the receiver mouth.** The Ames catching can is the strongest experimentally described analogue to a sacrificial catch feature. A published *ultrasonic* atomization patent instead proposes a water-cooled chamber ending in a cone with a periodically opening baffle between cone and collector. Its drawing and description establish a possible separation architecture, but not that the baffle can stop a molten charge or that the patented device has been built and tested. WO2025060171A1, https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2025060171A1/en. Neither source establishes that an ordinary thin sleeve at the receiver entrance will prevent hot bronze from accumulating on its lip or conducting heat into the upper joint. (steinmetz2011investigationofa pages 42-46, WO2025060171A1 pages 4-6, WO2025060171A1 pages 6-9)

**Metal collectors and upstream cooling have precedents; their exact receiver seals usually do not.** A roughly 500 g copper-capacity university gas atomizer uses a stainless chamber, cyclone and stainless powder collector, plus a chamber pressure gauge and relief valve. Another laboratory atomizer seals its powder container after cyclone separation and handles it in a glovebox; it records chamber pressures, oxygen, gas flow and temperatures. An ATO Lab+ ultrasonic study reports water-jacketed chamber walls and cyclone cooling before powder settles into a promptly sealed container. None of these reports specifies a hot-shot-rated receiver O-ring, a sacrificial puck, or an approved KF clamp pressure rating. Khan *et al.*, *The Nucleus* (2011), https://doi.org/10.71330/thenucleus.2011.866; del Rio *et al.*, *IOP Conference Series: Materials Science and Engineering* (2022), https://doi.org/10.1088/1757-899X/1249/1/012034; Wróbel *et al.* (2026), https://doi.org/10.1007/s00170-026-18599-0. (khan2010developmentofindigenous pages 2-4, rio2022laboratoryscalegasatomizer pages 4-6, wrobel2026enhancingniobiumpowder pages 5-7)

**An isolation valve has a documented purpose, but its position and seat exposure matter.** Hohmann and Jönsson describe a collection cone closed at its *lower end* by a valve so the connected powder container can be changed without losing the tower’s inert atmosphere. This supports including isolation when required for handling; it does **not** show that the present EPDM flap, or the proposed full-port ball valve, survives partly molten shot. A plasma-processing apparatus also uses a ball valve before stainless collection containers, but deliberately quenches powder into water, making it a poor dry-receiver thermal analogue. Hohmann and Jönsson, *Vacuum* (1990), https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-207X(90)94217-E; Khor, *Journal of Materials Processing Technology* (1992), https://doi.org/10.1016/0924-0136(92)90441-T. (hohmann1990modernsystemsfor pages 3-4, khor1992productionoffine pages 4-8)

**Instrumentation should address the process as well as the vessel.** An outside-flange thermocouple can warn of conducted heating, but it measures neither the temperature of a pile at the entrance nor whether atomization has ceased. The Ames work shielded in-chamber thermocouples from falling metal and recorded chamber gas temperatures; del Rio *et al.* measured oxygen, pressure, gas flow and melt temperature. A separate documented gas-atomizer failure began with misleading melt thermocouple readings and excessive heating that broke ceramic components; the authors selected optical pyrometry as a corrective measure. Thus, the proposed flange alarms are supplementary rather than demonstrated protection against the fast-arriving shot described here. Steinmetz (2011), https://doi.org/10.31274/etd-180810-3181; del Rio *et al.* (2022), https://doi.org/10.1088/1757-899X/1249/1/012034; McWilliams *et al.*, *Journal of Failure Analysis and Prevention* (2020), https://doi.org/10.1007/s11668-020-00887-w. (steinmetz2011investigationofa pages 42-46, rio2022laboratoryscalegasatomizer pages 4-6, mcwilliams2020failureanalysisof pages 1-4, mcwilliams2020failureanalysisof pages 4-10)

## Assessment of A–C against the evidence

- **A — catalogue KF nipple and blank:** Removing the glass and the process-facing flap seat removes two components damaged in this run; a removable liner and bottom catch piece are plausible *proposed* sacrificial measures. However, no located atomizer publication validates this particular DN40–50 nipple, clamp, centring ring, FKM seal, liner or puck under a kilogram-scale hot-shot upset. The bottom blank leaves a second potentially hot joint. Verify the **actual mating flange standard**—KF and sanitary clamp parts are not interchangeable—and obtain assembly-specific ratings for full external-pressure evacuation, positive pressure through the relief setting, temperature and supported hanging load. A pressure-relief setting alone does not certify the receiver joint. The rationale for an upstream catch rather than relying only on a receiver liner follows the Ames experiment. (steinmetz2011investigationofa pages 38-42, steinmetz2011investigationofa pages 42-46)
- **B — welded metal vessel:** An integral bottom eliminates A’s bottom O-ring from the location of a hot pile and is consequently the more direct containment response to the described bottom-seal damage. Its weld, bottom, top flange and clamp still require design review and testing for the machine’s pressure/vacuum cycle and credible thermal upset. Stainless construction is precedented; the particular 10 mm bottom and 63–76 mm geometry are **not literature-validated specifications**. (khan2010developmentofindigenous pages 2-4, yıldırım2017designandconstruction pages 44-52)
- **C — quartz glass rebuild:** Quartz has greater high-temperature capability than borosilicate, but it does not make the glass-to-plate seals, EPDM valve seat, or hot entrance safe. A vacuum-engineering thesis describes quartz for high-temperature use while cautioning that glass is brittle and poorly suited to mechanical loading; its material property is not a qualification for a clamped receiver exposed to impact and steep local temperature gradients. A sleeve that covers the rim must actually prevent direct shot contact and keep every seal below its compound-specific service temperature. On the evidence available, C retains more of the observed failure path than either metal concept. Rißlegger, *Design and Construction of an Acetylene Capable PVD Based Deposition System* (2020), https://doi.org/10.34726/hss.2020.67941. (risslegger2020designandconstruction pages 21-24)

Replacing EPDM with **FKM or silicone is not a substitute for thermal separation**. Trelleborg’s reference tables give typical standard-compound upper operating temperatures of 130–150 °C for EPDM, 175 °C for silicone VMQ and 200 °C for FKM. The guide explicitly says actual allowable temperatures depend on the compound, medium and loading; none approaches direct contact with bronze poured at approximately 1300 °C. Trelleborg Sealing Solutions, *O-Rings and Back-up Rings*, technical guide, https://www.tss.trelleborg.com/. (solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 43-45, solutions2016oringsandbackup pages 45-47)

## Failure evidence and limits

Published failures confirm that atomization hardware can depart sharply from its normal thermal trajectory: in a calcium centrifugal atomizer the mild-steel disk separated about 10 seconds into a run, while melt continued atomizing on its ceramic support; the authors discuss splashing, skull formation and uneven thermal stress. At Ames, bonded stopper/pour-tube components could lift and drop a melt assembly, cracking the pour tube and allowing molten-material leakage. Neither paper reports a burned collector seal or cracked glass receiver. Tian *et al.*, *Powder Technology* (2017), https://doi.org/10.1016/j.powtec.2016.12.011; [name redacted] *et al.*, *Plasma Sprayed Pour Tubes and Other Melt Handling Components for Use in Gas Atomization* (2011), described in the retrieved Ames manuscript; McWilliams *et al.* (2020), https://doi.org/10.1007/s11668-020-00887-w. (tian2017productionoffine pages 3-4, tian2017productionoffine pages 4-5, [name redacted]2011plasmasprayedpour pages 5-13, [name redacted]2011plasmasprayedpour pages 13-16, mcwilliams2020failureanalysisof pages 4-10)

**No independently verifiable published report of hot metal reaching an atomizer’s glass collection tube and burning its elastomer seals was located.** The November 13 event in the question is therefore the direct evidence for that *specific* failure sequence; the cited studies establish relevant catch-can designs, cooling and isolation arrangements, and adjacent failure modes—not a previously reported identical incident. Likewise, no accessible rePOWDER manual verified the construction or performance of its proprietary cone shields or the manufacturer’s simple stainless cup. Those details, and the exact flange standard, remain to be confirmed against the machine and manufacturer rather than inferred from another atomizer. (steinmetz2011investigationofa pages 38-42, steinmetz2011investigationofa pages 42-46, wrobel2026enhancingniobiumpowder pages 5-7, WO2025060171A1 pages 4-6, mcwilliams2020failureanalysisof pages 1-4, tian2017productionoffine pages 4-5)
