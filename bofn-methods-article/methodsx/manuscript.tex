\begin{abstract}
\noindent In multi-step materials workflows, such as alloy design, powder making,
printing and tensile testing, step costs differ by orders of magnitude.
Partial-evaluation Bayesian optimization of function networks chooses which
step to run next, and for which candidate, by expected gain per dollar. Whether
that pays depends on the workflow, so it should be tested in simulation first.
This method is a benchmark for that test: a synthetic alloy powder-to-part network
with a known optimum, physical precedence rules, reusable powder lots and a knob,
$\kappa$, for how badly a cheap crack screen misjudges the best alloys, with eight
reference policies and a matched-seed protocol. In 16--20 seeds per policy,
choosing the step bought an early lead but no better final alloy; screen-then-test
was best only when the screen was right; expected improvement per dollar with
powder reuse was the most robust policy, and the knowledge gradient never beat
it.
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item Knowledge gradient and expected improvement per dollar, each with partial
and full-chain evaluation, on one network model.
\item Seeded runs log every purchase and reproduce action for action across CPUs.
\item Every comparison between policies is a matched-seed bootstrap interval.
\end{itemize}
\end{abstract}

\section*{Background}

Finding an alloy that prints well is a chain of steps: calculate its phases
(minutes of computing), make powder (about a day), print a coupon, and test it
in tension. The steps differ in cost by orders of magnitude, and each needs the
product of the one before. Campaigns usually spend in a fixed order.
Screen-then-test calculates many compositions and carries a few through the
rest, which is cheap and works when the screen is right. Carrying every
candidate through the whole chain does not depend on the screen, but spends most
of the budget on candidates that a cheap early step could have ruled out.

Bayesian optimization of function networks (BOFN) models such a chain as linked
surrogate models, one per step~\cite{astudillo2021}. Its partial-evaluation
extension, p-KGFN, picks a step and a candidate whose inputs already exist by
the knowledge gradient of that one result per unit cost, and on its authors'
test problems it beat evaluating the whole network each
time~\cite{buathong2024,buathong2025}. Related multi-stage and cost-aware
schemes target self-driving laboratories and alloy
design~\cite{torresi2026,alvi2026}. All were tested on synthetic or
retrospective problems, while physical alloy campaigns choose compositions and
send each through a fixed chain~\cite{taherimousavi2026}; none of these tests has
the constraints of alloy powder: powder must
exist before it is printed and a build before it is tested, and one powder lot
can be printed many times. Benchmarks of experiment planners in chemistry and
materials~\cite{hase2021,felton2021,liang2021} score single-step objectives, so
they cannot test partial evaluation.

Whether choosing the step pays is a property of the workflow (its costs, what
the cheap steps reveal, how wrong the screen is) as much as of the algorithm,
and a physical campaign can be run only once. This method customizes BOFN and
p-KGFN into a benchmark that asks the question many times with the answer known.
(i) One network model carries p-KGFN, a full-chain knowledge-gradient
counterpart (KGFN), and network expected improvement per dollar with and without
powder reuse, so the policies form a $2\times2$ of acquisition function $\times$
evaluation mode, beside screen-then-test, standard Bayesian optimization and
random sampling. (ii) Its synthetic truth has a known optimum and one knob,
$\kappa$, for the effect the screen cannot see: Zr and Sc nucleants suppress hot
cracking in printed high-strength aluminum~\cite{martin2017}, which a
freezing-range crack criterion~\cite{kou2015} misses. (iii) Runs are seeded and
logged, reruns are checked action for action, and every comparison is a
matched-seed bootstrap interval. The benchmark is the dollar-budget, sequential
base on which a clocked simulation of the same laboratory is
built~\tbd{ref once public}, and it tests the optimizer of the companion
article~\tbd{ref}.

\section*{Method details}

\subsection*{The synthetic workflow}

A design $x\in[0,1]^7$ has four composition variables, $c_1$ (Zn+Mg), $c_2$
(Cu), $c_3$ (Zr) and $c_4$ (Sc), and three process variables: an atomizer
setting $a$, laser power $P$ and scan speed $v$. Six hidden nodes connect them
to the objective (\cref{fig:network}; \cref{tab:nodes}). One CALPHAD call
(\$10) returns three outputs from the composition: a strengthening-phase
fraction $s$, a freezing range $f$ and a primary-nucleant (Al$_3$Zr/Al$_3$Sc)
fraction $g$. Atomization (\$1,500) returns a powder quality $q$ from $a$ and
$g$. A print (\$2,500) returns the dense, crack-free fraction $\rho$ of the
build from $P$, $v$, $f$, $g$ and $q$. A tensile test (\$200) returns a
strength $U$ from $s$ and $\rho$. The objective is
$y = U - 0.3\,B(c)$, where $B$ is a known critical-mineral burden that weighs
Sc five times as heavily as Zr~\cite{nassar2020} and costs nothing to compute.
Costs per query are assumptions chosen to span the range of a laboratory with
its own atomizer and printer (the companion article measures them).

\paragraph{The screen-error knob $\kappa$.} Nucleants, as in Zr- and Sc-modified
printed alloys~\cite{croteau2018,schmidtke2011}, cut the effective freezing
range that drives cracking by up to 76\% at $\kappa=1$ (65\% at the optimum)
and not at all at $\kappa=0$. A screen that keeps compositions with $f\le0.25$ (the
screen-then-test rule below) is therefore conservative but right at $\kappa=0$,
and at $\kappa=1$ it rejects the optimum: a high-solute, fully Zr-inoculated,
Sc-free alloy ($c=(0.65,0.60,1.00,0)$) whose freezing-range index is 0.70 but
which prints 94\% dense because the nucleant brings $f_\mathrm{eff}$ down to
0.25. The best design that passes the screen at $\kappa=1$ scores 65\% of the
optimum. At $\kappa=0$ the optimum is a lean alloy without nucleant
($f=0.27$), and the screen keeps 90\% of it. The optimum $y^*$ at each $\kappa$
is found by random search over 400,000 designs refined locally
(\code{analyze.optimum}); multi-start L-BFGS-B agrees to within 0.1\%.

\paragraph{Rules and shared start.} A powder lot must exist before it is
printed and a build before it is tested; a lot can be printed any number of
times; the first experiment on a new alloy runs its CALPHAD call automatically
and pays for it. For a given seed, every policy starts from the same data:
CALPHAD on 32 scrambled-Sobol compositions~\cite{owen1998} and five random
designs carried through all four steps, both drawn from the seed. This start is
sunk: budgets count only new spend, up to \$100,000. A policy stops when its
cheapest next purchase no longer fits, so the full-chain policies stop after 23
chains at about \$96.8k and the others between \$97k and \$100k. Observations
are noise-free by default.

\subsection*{One network model for the four network policies}

pKGFN, KGFN and the two BOFN policies share one network model. Every node is an
exact Gaussian process (GP) on its own inputs (the design variables it uses plus
its parents' outputs): BoTorch's \code{SingleTaskGP} with its default ARD RBF
kernel (unit output scale), a constant mean, and outputs standardized by their
mean and standard deviation, the latter floored at 0.25 so that early, similar
observations keep a physical prior scale. Hyperparameters are fitted by
maximizing the marginal likelihood with BoTorch's default priors (a maximum a
posteriori estimate)~\cite{balandat2020,gardner2018,paszke2019}, with a fixed
noise of $10^{-6}$ (in standardized units) as a numerical nugget. pKGFN and
KGFN refit only the nodes whose data changed after a purchase; the BOFN policies
refit every node. Fit failures are counted and logged with every run. The
network posterior at a design is carried as Monte Carlo samples: 64
standard-normal base samples per node are pushed through the nodes in
topological order, each node drawing its value from its GP at the sampled values
of its parents~\cite{astudillo2021}. There is no Markov chain; hyperparameters
are point estimates. Standard BO and screen-then-test instead fit one GP of the
same kind from the seven design variables to the objective; random sampling uses
no model.

\subsection*{Policies}

All policies spend the same budget, start from the same data for a given seed
and are scored the same way (\cref{tab:policies}). The $2\times2$ is not a
perfectly controlled factorial: besides the acquisition function and the
evaluation mode, the knowledge-gradient and expected-improvement rows differ in
their candidate pools and in what ``partial'' allows (any single step for pKGFN;
a reprint and test of an existing lot for BOFN with powder reuse), as detailed
below.

\paragraph{pKGFN.} Each decision starts from a pool of 512 Sobol designs, 8
perturbations (standard deviation 0.05) of every tested design, and 24
perturbations (0.03) around the 8 designs with the largest posterior mean. The
incumbent is the largest network posterior mean in the pool, and ``network EI''
below is the expected improvement over it. The candidates are: for CALPHAD, the
compositions of the 12 designs with the largest network EI, of the four with
the largest posterior mean, and four space-filling compositions; for
atomization, the 16 simulated compositions whose best atomizer-and-laser
setting has the largest network EI; for printing, the 16 (lot, laser-setting)
pairs with the largest network EI over 32 settings per existing lot; for
testing, every untested build. The knowledge gradient~\cite{frazier2009,frazier2018} of one more result at a
candidate is the expected increase in the maximum network posterior mean over
an inner set of designs. The inner set is shared by all candidates and anchored
on the incumbent: the 32 designs with the largest posterior mean, the 16 with
the largest network EI, and the design attached to every candidate. Its 64 base
samples per node are shared by every candidate (common random numbers). The
fantasized result is integrated by Gauss--Hermite quadrature (10 nodes; a
$2^3$ product rule for CALPHAD's three outputs), the fantasized GP update is the
closed-form rank-one update, and the current value is computed with the same
estimator, so the estimated KG is never negative. pKGFN buys the candidate with
the largest KG per dollar. If KG is numerically zero for every candidate
($<10^{-10}$ per dollar), it buys the atomize, print or test candidate with the
largest network EI per dollar instead, much as fast p-KGFN uses EI to propose
candidates~\cite{buathong2025}. As in the original, pKGFN atomizes only alloys
that have been simulated.

\paragraph{KGFN.} KGFN uses pKGFN's model, pool, candidates and inner set, but
each query runs the whole chain (with CALPHAD if the alloy is new) at the
design attached to one of pKGFN's CALPHAD, atomization or print candidates,
about 52 designs per decision. A fantasy draws one standard normal per node
(32 scrambled-Sobol quasi-Monte Carlo points in six dimensions) and pushes it
down the chain, so that every node gets its fantasized input and value; each
node's GP then takes its own rank-one update. KGFN buys the design with the
largest KG per dollar (\$4,200, or \$4,210 for a new alloy), with the same EI
fallback.

\paragraph{Expected-improvement policies.} Their EI is over the best objective
tested so far, from 64 Monte Carlo samples per design. BOFN scores 1,024 Sobol
designs plus 16 local perturbations around every tested design, adds 32
perturbations around each of the eight best, and runs the whole chain at the
design with the largest network EI. BOFN with powder reuse scores the 1,024 Sobol
designs and the perturbations of tested designs (without that refinement) at the
full-chain cost, and 64 laser settings per existing lot at the reprint-and-test
cost (\$2,700), and buys the largest EI per dollar, as cost-aware EI divides by
the cost of an evaluation~\cite{snoek2012}. Standard BO scores 2,048 Sobol
designs plus 64 perturbations around each of the five best. Screen-then-test
calculates 224 more compositions (256 with the shared 32), keeps the four with
the largest strength minus 0.3 times the burden among all simulated compositions
with $f\le0.25$, and then fits one GP to every tested design; for each of the
four it compares 128 new (atomizer, power, speed) settings at \$4,200 with 64
(power, speed) settings per existing lot at \$2,700, by EI (not per dollar).

\subsection*{Scoring}

The primary metric is the best alloy demonstrated: the true objective of the
best design that has been printed and tensile-tested so far, divided by $y^*$
at that $\kappa$, recorded after every purchase and read on a \$250 grid of new
spend. A policy gets no credit for designs it has not tested, and the five seed
alloys count: at $\kappa=1$ the start averages 35\% of the optimum and ranges
from $-15$\% to 84\% across seeds. Per run, we read the score at \$25k, \$50k
and \$100k (for the full-chain policies, their final value at about \$96.8k),
and average it over the whole \$0--100k grid (the anytime score, the normalized
area under the curve, which needs no choice of budget). Two secondary metrics are logged: the true value of the printed
design that the policy's own model ranks best, and the argmax of the network
posterior mean over the whole space~\cite{buathong2024}; the latter swings
widely from one \$10 query to the next on this network and is not used for
conclusions.

\subsection*{Statistical protocol}

A seed fixes the shared start, so every policy sees the same five seed alloys
and 32 simulated compositions for a given seed, and comparisons are paired by
seed. All eight policies are run on the same seeds: 20 at $\kappa=1$ and 16 at
$\kappa=0$ and $0.5$. Intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over seeds
(10,000 draws)~\cite{efron1993}. Each draw resamples seeds jointly for every
policy (the matched-seed bootstrap), so a difference between two policies is
always computed on the same seeds. Differences are in percentage points of the
optimum. The speed-up of a policy over a baseline is the spend at which the
baseline's mean curve first reaches its own \$100k mean, divided by the spend at
which the policy's mean curve first reaches it; it is 0 if the policy's mean
never gets there within \$100k. It is an acceleration factor in the sense used
for self-driving laboratories~\cite{tom2024}, measured in dollars. Wilcoxon signed-rank
$p$-values~\cite{wilcoxon1945} and win counts are reported alongside, without
correction for the number of comparisons. Matching pays where the shared start
varies most: at $\kappa=1$, where the best seed alloy ranges from below zero to
84\% of the optimum, the median matched-seed interval of a difference is
\stat{k1-widthratio-v25} times as wide at \$25k, and \stat{k1-widthratio-auc}
times as wide for the anytime score, as the interval from resampling each
policy's seeds independently (\stat{k1-widthratio-v100} at \$100k; all 28 pairs).

\subsection*{Reproducibility protocol}

Each run writes one JSON file with every decision (stage, spend and scores
after it), every purchase with its inputs, the run's settings (costs, noise,
Monte Carlo sizes, screening parameters), the git commit and whether the
benchmark's tracked files were modified, the versions of Python, PyTorch,
BoTorch, GPyTorch, linear\_operator, NumPy and SciPy, the CPU model, the
command line and the GP-fit log. \code{compare_runs.py} checks a rerun against
a stored run: they match action for action when every decision has the same
stage at the same cumulative spend, the per-stage counts agree, and the two
scores that are true values of purchased designs (the best alloy demonstrated
and the best printed design by the model) agree to within $10^{-9}$ after every
purchase; when both runs log their purchases, the purchased designs must agree
too. The stored MS\&T26 runs predate the purchase log, so for them the identity
of the designs is inferred from the scores.

\section*{Method validation}

\subsection*{The implementation computes what it claims}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{KGFN.} A brute-force check (\code{quality/check_kgfn.py}) redoes
every fantasy by appending the fantasized observation to each node's data with
the same hyperparameters, re-solving the GPs and resampling the network, on a
reduced setting (four designs, 16 fantasies, 32 samples) from the data of seed 0
at $\kappa=1$ and seed 3 at $\kappa=0.5$. It agrees with the closed-form KGFN to
within $10^{-13}$; KG is non-negative, and the KG of re-running a design that has
already been through the whole chain is below $10^{-3}$ of the largest. pKGFN's
single-step KG has no brute-force check; its fantasy update was derived and
checked line by line in the independent review below.
\item \textbf{Monte Carlo noise in pKGFN's knowledge gradient}
(\code{quality/mc_noise.py}). At four decisions of seed 0 ($\kappa=1$), the same
candidates were re-scored with five other draws of 64 base samples and with
1,024 as a reference. The chosen stage was the same at all four decisions and
the chosen design at three. At 64 samples the small KG of atomizing and testing
is underestimated (often exactly zero), which favors printing; the stage choice
was robust where the gap between stages was large. Re-running pKGFN with four
times the base samples (four seeds) or with twice the candidates and twice the
samples (two seeds) left its \$100k result and its mix of purchases within the
seed-to-seed spread, but not its early result: at \$25k the four reruns with four
times the samples averaged 46\% of the optimum against 69\% for the same seeds'
stored runs. pKGFN's early lead is therefore a property of the 20 seeds together,
not of any single run.
\item \textbf{Gaussian processes.} None of the 62,350 GP fits in the 416 runs
compared here failed (\code{paper-stats/allocation.csv}). In the 63 fits of one pKGFN run
whose hyperparameters were recorded (\code{quality/mc_noise_k1_s0.json}), the
lengthscales ranged from 0.10 to 132 and none sat at the lower bound.
\item \textbf{Independent review.} Before the port, two independent analysis
tasks reviewed the code and recomputed every table from the run files without
\code{analyze.py}. The GP posterior matched a NumPy implementation to about
$5\times10^{-15}$ and BoTorch's to $7\times10^{-9}$ (the difference is a
$10^{-8}$ jitter); every table reproduced at its published precision, and the
bootstrap intervals to within 0.1. The two findings that changed code (budget-edge
checks) are fixed, and the fixes change no run of the eight policies compared
here, as the reruns below confirm. \tbd{Cite the review reports in the
repository (\code{edison/results/}).}
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Runs reproduce action for action}

Rerunning the stored runs with the pinned environment reproduced every one
action for action (\cref{tab:rerun}). At $\kappa=1$ the 140 runs of seven
policies (seeds 0--19) were rerun in October 2026 on AMD EPYC 9V45 and 9V74
runners; at $\kappa=0$ and $0.5$ the stored seeds of five policies were rerun in
November 2026 on an AMD EPYC 7763. The largest difference in any recorded
score is $1.1\times10^{-16}$, the last bit of a double, and no difference ever
changed a decision (\code{results-rerun/compare_k*.txt}). The runs with no stored counterpart (KGFN; random and
screen-then-test without reuse at $\kappa=0$ and 0.5; seeds 12--15 or 8--15)
were run from the same clean commit and environment.

\subsection*{The benchmark separates the policies and responds to $\kappa$ as designed}

\Cref{fig:curves} shows the best alloy demonstrated against spend for six of
the policies at each $\kappa$, and \cref{tab:results} the values at \$25k,
\$50k and \$100k with their intervals.

\paragraph{When the screen misses the effect ($\kappa=1$).} Screen-then-test
plateaued at \stat{k1-tiered-v100-mean}\% of the optimum by \$100k (95\% CI
\stat{k1-tiered-v100-lo}--\stat{k1-tiered-v100-hi}), below random designs
(\stat{k1-random-v100-mean}\%), because its shortlist excludes the optimum.
Every model-based policy finished above it at \$100k, by between
\stat{k1-pkgfn-tiered-v100-abs} (pKGFN and standard BO) and
\stat{k1-eifn_reuse-tiered-v100-abs} percentage points (BOFN with powder reuse,
95\% CI \stat{k1-eifn_reuse-tiered-v100-ci}). Measured as
spend to reach screening's own \$100k level, pKGFN and BOFN with powder reuse
were \stat{k1-su-pkgfn-tiered}$\times$ (\stat{k1-su-pkgfn-tiered-ci95}) and
\stat{k1-su-eifn_reuse-tiered}$\times$ (\stat{k1-su-eifn_reuse-tiered-ci95})
faster, full-chain BOFN and KGFN \stat{k1-su-eifn-tiered}$\times$
(\stat{k1-su-eifn-tiered-ci95}) and \stat{k1-su-kgfn-tiered}$\times$
(\stat{k1-su-kgfn-tiered-ci95}), and standard BO
\stat{k1-su-ei-tiered}$\times$ (\stat{k1-su-ei-tiered-ci95};
\cref{fig:speedup}). The ratio depends on the target: screening's mean creeps
up in its last \$5k, which sets its own crossing at the end of the budget.

\paragraph{The $2\times2$ at $\kappa=1$: choosing the step buys an early lead,
not a better finish.} Under the knowledge gradient, partial evaluation was
\stat{k1-c-0-v25-abs}\pp{} ahead of full-chain evaluation at \$25k (95\% CI of
the difference \stat{k1-c-0-v25-ci}), and level by \$100k (difference
\stat{k1-c-0-v100}\pp, \stat{k1-c-0-v100-ci}). Under expected improvement per
dollar the early lead was smaller and not resolved (\stat{k1-c-1-v25}\pp,
\stat{k1-c-1-v25-ci}), and the \$100k difference was \stat{k1-c-1-v100}\pp{}
(\stat{k1-c-1-v100-ci}). Over the whole budget neither contrast was
distinguishable from zero (anytime \stat{k1-c-0-auc}\pp{} and
\stat{k1-c-1-auc}\pp). The knowledge gradient did not beat expected improvement
per dollar: with partial evaluation it finished \stat{k1-c-2-v100-abs}\pp{} behind
(difference \stat{k1-c-2-v100-ci}), and with full chains it was
\stat{k1-c-3-v50-abs}\pp{} behind at \$50k (\stat{k1-c-3-v50-ci}) and level at
\$100k (\cref{fig:contrasts}).
pKGFN spent its budget on prints of a few powders: per run it bought
\stat{k1-pkgfn-nA} powders, \stat{k1-pkgfn-nL} prints and \stat{k1-pkgfn-nT}
tensile tests, against \stat{k1-eifn_reuse-nA}, \stat{k1-eifn_reuse-nL} and
\stat{k1-eifn_reuse-nT} for BOFN with powder reuse (\cref{fig:allocation}).
A one-step knowledge gradient cannot see that a new powder's value shows only
once it is printed and tested.

\paragraph{Against standard BO.} No network policy reached standard BO's own
\$100k level (\stat{k1-su-ei-target}\% of the optimum) reliably sooner: the
speed-ups over it were between \stat{k1-su-pkgfn-ei} and
\stat{k1-su-eifn-ei}$\times$, and every interval reached down to ``not reached''
(Figure~S2). The gain over screening at $\kappa=1$ therefore comes from
searching the whole design space instead of trusting the screen, which standard
BO does too. Modelling the chain and choosing the step help earlier in the budget,
not at the end: full-chain BOFN was \stat{k1-eifn-ei-v50-abs} points ahead of
standard BO at \$50k (difference \stat{k1-eifn-ei-v50-ci}) and level at \$100k.

\paragraph{When the screen is right ($\kappa=0$).} Screen-then-test was the most
reliable policy: \stat{k0-tiered-v100-mean}\% of the optimum at \$100k (95\% CI
\stat{k0-tiered-v100-lo}--\stat{k0-tiered-v100-hi}), the narrowest interval of
any policy, and no other policy's mean curve reached that level within the budget
(\cref{fig:speedup}). The two BOFN policies averaged
\stat{k0-eifn_reuse-v100-mean}\% (with powder reuse) and
\stat{k0-eifn-v100-mean}\%; BOFN with powder reuse finished ahead of
screen-then-test on \stat{k0-eifn_reuse-tiered-v100-wins} of \stat{k0-n} seeds
but far behind on the rest, so its mean was \stat{k0-eifn_reuse-tiered-v100-abs}\pp{}
lower (difference \stat{k0-eifn_reuse-tiered-v100-ci}). The knowledge-gradient policies fell
furthest. pKGFN and KGFN both reached \stat{k0-pkgfn-v100-mean}\%,
\stat{k0-pkgfn-eifn_reuse-v100-abs}\pp{} and \stat{k0-kgfn-eifn-v100-abs}\pp{}
behind their expected-improvement counterparts at \$100k (differences
\stat{k0-pkgfn-eifn_reuse-v100-ci} and \stat{k0-kgfn-eifn-v100-ci}; they finished
ahead on \stat{k0-pkgfn-eifn_reuse-v100-wins} and \stat{k0-kgfn-eifn-v100-wins}
of \stat{k0-n} seeds), and \stat{k0-pkgfn-tiered-v100-abs}\pp{} behind
screen-then-test (\stat{k0-pkgfn-tiered-v100-ci}). The optimum at $\kappa=0$ is a
lean alloy without nucleant, in a corner of composition space that the 32 seed
compositions do not reach. pKGFN atomizes only simulated alloys and bought
\stat{k0-pkgfn-nC} new CALPHAD calls per run, so it kept printing the powders it
had (\stat{k0-pkgfn-nL} prints per run); KGFN ran \stat{k0-kgfn-nC} of its
\stat{k0-kgfn-nA} chains per run on new alloys and the rest on alloys it had
already simulated. A diagnosis of the same pKGFN code in the clocked extension
found the knowledge gradient of a tensile test exactly zero for most candidates at
$\kappa=0$~\tbd{ref once public}. Choosing the step made no resolvable difference
at $\kappa=0$ under either acquisition function (at \$25k, \stat{k0-c-1-v25}\pp{}
under expected improvement, \stat{k0-c-1-v25-ci}; \stat{k0-c-0-v25}\pp{} under
the knowledge gradient, \stat{k0-c-0-v25-ci}).

\paragraph{Half right ($\kappa=0.5$).} Screen-then-test led early (at \$25k,
full-chain BOFN was \stat{k05-eifn-tiered-v25-abs}\pp{} behind it, difference
\stat{k05-eifn-tiered-v25-ci}), but the expected-improvement network policies
finished ahead: BOFN with powder reuse reached \stat{k05-eifn_reuse-v100-mean}\%
of the optimum at \$100k against \stat{k05-tiered-v100-mean}\% (difference
\stat{k05-eifn_reuse-tiered-v100}\pp, \stat{k05-eifn_reuse-tiered-v100-ci};
ahead on \stat{k05-eifn_reuse-tiered-v100-wins} of \stat{k05-n} seeds). The
knowledge-gradient policies again trailed their expected-improvement
counterparts at \$100k: pKGFN by \stat{k05-c-2-v100-abs}\pp{}
(\stat{k05-c-2-v100-ci}) and KGFN by \stat{k05-c-3-v100-abs}\pp{}
(\stat{k05-c-3-v100-ci}).

\paragraph{Across $\kappa$.} Three regularities held at every $\kappa$.
First, choosing the step never bought a better final alloy: no partial-minus-full
contrast at \$100k or over the whole budget excluded zero. Its early lead was
clear only under the knowledge gradient with a wrong screen, and positive but
unresolved under expected improvement at every $\kappa$ (\stat{k0-c-1-v25},
\stat{k05-c-1-v25} and \stat{k1-c-1-v25}\pp{} at \$25k for $\kappa=0$, 0.5
and 1). Second, the knowledge gradient as implemented here never beat expected
improvement per dollar at \$100k: with partial evaluation it trailed at every
$\kappa$, by \stat{k1-c-2-v100-abs}, \stat{k05-c-2-v100-abs} and
\stat{k0-c-2-v100-abs} points for $\kappa=1$, 0.5 and 0 (intervals
\stat{k1-c-2-v100-ci}, \stat{k05-c-2-v100-ci} and \stat{k0-c-2-v100-ci}), most
when the screen was at least half right. Third, BOFN with powder reuse was the most robust
policy: \stat{k0-eifn_reuse-v100-mean}\%, \stat{k05-eifn_reuse-v100-mean}\%
and \stat{k1-eifn_reuse-v100-mean}\% of the optimum at \$100k for $\kappa=0$,
0.5 and 1. Screen-then-test, by contrast, went from the most reliable policy
($\kappa=0$) to one that plateaued below random designs ($\kappa=1$). These are
the regularities a user of the benchmark should expect to reproduce. With 16--20
seeds, the benchmark resolves differences at \$100k of about
\stat{k1-halfwidth-v100} points of the optimum at $\kappa=1$, and of
\stat{k05-halfwidth-v100} and \stat{k0-halfwidth-v100} points at $\kappa=0.5$
and 0, where runs vary more (the median half-width of a pairwise 95\% interval at
\$100k).

\section*{Customization}

The benchmark is meant to be changed. Each of the following is a flag or a
single function, and every change is recorded in the run file, so customized
runs cannot be mistaken for the reference ones.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Costs.} \code{--costs L=1000 T=500} overrides any step's cost.
Which cost ratios favor partial evaluation is what this flag is for; no cost
variant has been run yet, and \code{paper_figures.py}'s spend-per-step figure
assumes the default costs. The companion article measures the real ones.
\item \textbf{Noise.} \code{--noise 0.02}, or per step (\code{--noise A=0.01
L=0.05 T=0.02}), adds Gaussian measurement noise to atomization, printing and
testing, from its own random stream; each node GP is told its noise variance.
CALPHAD stays deterministic, because its error is the bias $\kappa$. The noise
option has only been smoke-tested: downstream GPs take measured parent values as
inputs without modelling their noise, and the primary score then still credits
the tested design with the best true value (an oracle choice), so the honest
score under noise is \code{best_validated_obs}, the true value of the design with
the best measured objective.
\item \textbf{Screen error and budget.} \code{--kappa} takes any value in
$[0,1]$ and \code{--budget} any spend; \code{paper_stats.py} takes the
$\kappa$ values with \code{--kappas}, but its grid stops at \$100k
(\code{analyze.GRID}).
\item \textbf{Monte Carlo settings.} \code{BOFN_SACQ} (base samples, 64),
\code{BOFN_NCAND} (candidates per stage, 16) and \code{BOFN_NFANT} (KGFN
fantasies, 32) set pKGFN and KGFN; the expected-improvement policies use 64
samples per design in the code.
\item \textbf{The truth.} \code{AlloyTruth.node} holds the closed forms of
\cref{tab:nodes}; changing them keeps the workflow. A new workflow is a code
change: the nodes and their parents (\code{SPEC}, \code{GROUPS}), the input
index sets (\code{COMP}, \code{IA}, \code{IPV}), the laboratory's step methods
and costs in \code{bofn_alloy.py}, and the per-step candidate construction in
\code{run_pkgfn}.
\item \textbf{A policy.} A new policy is a new branch in \code{run_full_policy}
(policies that buy whole chains or reprints) or in \code{run_pkgfn}
(knowledge-gradient policies), selected by name in \code{run}. As an example, ``any-alloy'' pKGFN
(\code{pkgfn_free}) lets pKGFN atomize compositions it has never simulated
(CALPHAD runs first, its outputs entering KG at their posterior means) and
values a new powder by atomizing and printing it as one \$4,000 move.
\tbd{Result of pkgfn\_free at $\kappa=0$, 0.5 and 1 from the stored runs (8, 8
and 20 seeds) or from 16-seed reruns if they finish.}
\end{itemize}

A clocked version reuses this truth and adds durations, station capacity,
asynchronous decisions, noise, and failed powders, builds and bars, and reports
days as well as dollars; a digital twin of the laboratory, with its own aluminum
and copper truths, adds people, robots and calendars. \tbd{Cite once public at a
fixed commit (DAL PR 297; byu-vcl twin rounds 3--5).} In both, a pKGFN with fixes
tied full-chain BOFN, and in the clocked version the EI fallback made most of
pKGFN's decisions. That is consistent with what this benchmark shows: on this
network, choosing the step helps early, and the knowledge gradient adds little
over expected improvement per dollar.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Synthetic truth.} The node functions mirror the qualitative
metallurgy but are not fitted to data, and $\kappa$ is the one place the
benchmark is built to make screening wrong. The conclusions are about this
network and these costs.
\item \textbf{Simplifications.} Observations are noise-free by default, there
is one objective, one parameter set per build (real plates carry many coupons,
which would favor partial evaluation further), no time or capacity, and no
failed runs.
\item \textbf{pKGFN as implemented.} It is discrete (candidates come from
EI-ranked pools; the inner maximum runs over an anchored set), one-step myopic,
restricted to simulated alloys, and uses a 2-point-per-output product rule for
CALPHAD's three outputs, which gets the fourth moment wrong. Its results moved
when the candidate rules changed. They are results of this implementation at
these settings, not of p-KGFN in general.
\item \textbf{The $2\times2$ is not a controlled factorial.} The
knowledge-gradient and expected-improvement policies also differ in candidate
pools and in what partial evaluation may buy, so a KG-versus-EI contrast is a
contrast of these implementations, not of the two acquisition functions alone.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} With 16--20 seeds, percentile bootstrap intervals
can under-cover; the speed-up statistic is the first \$250 step a mean curve
crosses, so its bootstrap distribution is lumpy and its interval endpoints move
by about 0.1 between bootstrap seeds. Intervals are not corrected for the
number of comparisons.
\item \textbf{Bit-level reproducibility} has been checked on Linux x86\_64
only, on three AMD EPYC models. Other CPUs and BLAS builds may diverge in
the last digits and, through a different decision, beyond.
\end{itemize}
