\begin{abstract}
\noindent Materials workflows such as composition design, powder making,
printing and mechanical testing are chains of steps whose costs differ by
orders of magnitude. Bayesian optimization of function networks can model such
a chain step by step, and its partial-evaluation variants choose which step to
run next, for which candidate, by expected gain per unit cost. Whether that
choice pays off depends on the workflow, so it should be tested before lab time
is committed. We describe a benchmark for doing so: a synthetic alloy network
(CALPHAD, atomization, laser powder bed fusion, tensile testing) with a known
optimum, physical precedence rules, reusable powder lots, costs of \$10 to
\$2,500 per step, and one knob, $\kappa$, for how badly a cheap
CALPHAD-style crack screen misjudges the alloys that matter. Eight reference
policies share one network model and form a $2\times2$ of
\{knowledge gradient, expected improvement\} per dollar $\times$ \{partial,
full-chain\} evaluation, alongside screen-then-test, standard Bayesian
optimization and random sampling. Every run is seeded and logged, reruns
reproduce stored runs action for action across three CPU models, and every
comparison is reported as a matched-seed bootstrap interval. When the screen
misses the effect ($\kappa=1$, \stat{k1-n} seeds), the partial-evaluation
policies reached screening's \$100k result with \stat{k1-su-pkgfn-tiered}-fold
(95\% CI \stat{k1-su-pkgfn-tiered-ci95}) and
\stat{k1-su-eifn_reuse-tiered}-fold (\stat{k1-su-eifn_reuse-tiered-ci95}) less
spend; their lead over full-chain evaluation was early and gone by \$100k, and
the knowledge gradient was not better than expected improvement per dollar.
\tbd{One sentence on $\kappa=0$ and 0.5 once the 16-seed runs are in.}
\end{abstract}

\section*{Background}

A materials discovery campaign is rarely one experiment. To learn whether an
aluminum alloy is worth making, a laboratory might calculate its phases (minutes
of computing), make powder of it (about a day), print a coupon (a build), and
test the coupon in tension (days). The steps differ in cost by orders of
magnitude, and the later steps need the products of the earlier ones. Campaigns
usually spend in a fixed order. Screen-then-test calculates many compositions
and carries a few through the remaining steps, which is cheap and works when the
screen is right. Carrying every candidate through the whole chain does not
depend on the screen, but it spends most of the budget on candidates a cheap
early step could have ruled out.

Bayesian optimization of function networks (BOFN) models such a chain as a
network of surrogate models, one per step, whose outputs feed the next
step~\cite{astudillo2021}. Its partial-evaluation extension, p-KGFN, goes
further: at each decision it chooses a step and a candidate whose parents are
already available, by the knowledge gradient of that one result per unit
cost~\cite{buathong2024,buathong2025}. On its authors' test problems, p-KGFN
reached better solutions for the same cost than evaluating the whole network
each time~\cite{buathong2024}. Related multi-stage and cost-aware schemes have
been proposed for self-driving laboratories and for alloy
design~\cite{torresi2026,alvi2026}. All of these were tested on synthetic or
retrospective problems, and none on a chain with the physical constraints of
alloy powder: powder must exist before it can be printed, a build before it can
be tested, and one powder lot can be printed many times.

Whether choosing the step pays off is a property of the workflow (its costs,
how informative the cheap steps are, how wrong the screen is), not of the
algorithm alone, and a physical campaign is too expensive to run more than once.
A synthetic benchmark lets the question be asked many times, with the answer
known. Benchmarks of experiment planners exist for
chemistry~\cite{hase2021,felton2021} and across experimental materials
datasets~\cite{liang2021}, but they score single-step objectives and so cannot
test partial evaluation.

This article describes such a benchmark for an alloy powder-to-part workflow.
It makes three customizations of the original methods. (i) It implements
p-KGFN, a full-chain knowledge-gradient counterpart (KGFN), and network
expected improvement per dollar with and without powder reuse on one network
model, so that the policies form a $2\times2$ of
\{knowledge gradient, expected improvement\} per dollar $\times$
\{partial, full-chain\} evaluation, with screen-then-test, standard Bayesian
optimization and random sampling as baselines. (ii) Its synthetic truth has a
known optimum and a single knob, $\kappa$, for the part of the physics that the
cheap screen cannot see: grain refinement by Zr and Sc nucleants, which
suppresses hot cracking in printed high-strength aluminum
alloys~\cite{martin2017} but is invisible to a freezing-range crack
criterion~\cite{kou2015}. (iii) Its protocol makes every comparison checkable:
runs are seeded, every purchase is logged with the code version and the package
versions, reruns are checked action for action, and every comparison between
policies is a matched-seed bootstrap interval.

The benchmark is the synthetic, dollar-budget, sequential base of two larger
simulations by the same group, which reuse its truth: a clocked version that
adds time, asynchronous stations, noise and failures, and a digital twin of the
laboratory. \tbd{Cite once public: bofn-clocked (DAL PR 297) and byu-vcl twin
rounds 3--5.} The physical campaign that motivates it is reported
separately~\tbd{ref.\ companion article}.

\section*{Method details}

\subsection*{The synthetic workflow}

A design $x\in[0,1]^7$ has four composition variables, $c_1$ (Zn+Mg), $c_2$
(Cu), $c_3$ (Zr) and $c_4$ (Sc), and three process variables: an atomizer
setting $a$, laser power $P$ and scan speed $v$. Six hidden nodes connect them
to the objective (\cref{fig:network}; \cref{tab:nodes}). One CALPHAD call
(\$10) returns three outputs from the composition: a strengthening-phase
fraction $s$, a freezing range $f$ and a primary-nucleant (Al$_3$Zr/Al$_3$Sc)
fraction $g$. Atomization (\$1,500) returns a powder quality $q$ from $a$ and
$g$. A print (\$2,500) returns the dense, crack-free fraction $\rho$ of the
build from $P$, $v$, $f$, $g$ and $q$. A tensile test (\$200) returns a
strength $U$ from $s$ and $\rho$. The objective is
$y = U - 0.3\,B(c)$, where $B$ is a known critical-mineral burden that weighs
Sc five times as heavily as Zr~\cite{nassar2020} and costs nothing to compute.
Costs per query are assumptions chosen to span the range of a laboratory with
its own atomizer and printer (the companion article measures them).

\paragraph{The screen-error knob $\kappa$.} Nucleants cut the effective freezing
range that drives cracking by up to 85\% at $\kappa=1$ and not at all at
$\kappa=0$. A screen that keeps compositions with $f\le0.25$ (the
tiered-screening rule below) is therefore conservative but right at $\kappa=0$,
and at $\kappa=1$ it rejects the optimum: a high-solute, fully Zr-inoculated,
Sc-free alloy ($c=(0.65,0.60,1.00,0)$) whose freezing-range index is 0.70 but
which prints 94\% dense because the nucleant brings $f_\mathrm{eff}$ down to
0.25. The best design that passes the screen at $\kappa=1$ scores 65\% of the
optimum. At $\kappa=0$ the optimum is a lean alloy without nucleant
($f=0.27$), and the screen keeps 90\% of it. The optimum $y^*$ at each $\kappa$
is found by random search over 400,000 designs refined locally
(\code{analyze.optimum}); multi-start L-BFGS-B agrees to within 0.1\%.

\paragraph{Rules and shared start.} A powder lot must exist before it is
printed and a build before it is tested; a lot can be printed any number of
times; the first experiment on a new alloy runs its CALPHAD call automatically
and pays for it. Every policy and seed starts from the same data: CALPHAD on 32
scrambled-Sobol compositions~\cite{owen1998} and five random designs carried
through all four steps (seeded by the run seed). This start is sunk; budgets
count only new spend, up to \$100,000, about 24 full make--print--test cycles.
Observations are noise-free by default.

\subsection*{One network model for every network policy}

Every node is an exact Gaussian process (GP) on its own inputs (the design
variables it uses plus its parents' outputs), with an RBF kernel whose
hyperparameters are fitted by maximum marginal likelihood under BoTorch's
default priors~\cite{balandat2020,gardner2018,paszke2019}, and a fixed noise
of $10^{-6}$ as a numerical nugget. Only the nodes whose data changed are
refitted after a purchase, and fit failures are counted and logged with every
run. The network posterior at a design is carried as Monte Carlo samples: 64
standard-normal base samples per node, held fixed across the candidates of one
decision (common random numbers), are pushed through the nodes in topological
order, each node drawing its value from its GP at the sampled values of its
parents~\cite{astudillo2021}. There is no Markov chain; hyperparameters are
point estimates.

\paragraph{pKGFN.} At each decision the candidates are: for CALPHAD, the
compositions of the 12 designs with the largest network EI, of the four with
the largest posterior mean, and four space-filling compositions; for
atomization, the 16 simulated compositions whose best atomizer-and-laser
setting has the largest network EI; for printing, the 16 (lot, laser-setting)
pairs with the largest network EI over 32 settings per existing lot; for
testing, every untested build. The knowledge gradient of one more result at a
candidate is the expected increase in the maximum network posterior mean over
an inner set of designs. The inner set is shared by all candidates and anchored
on the incumbent: the 32 designs with the largest posterior mean, the 16 with
the largest network EI, and the design attached to every candidate. The
fantasized result is integrated by Gauss--Hermite quadrature (10 nodes; a
$2^3$ product rule for CALPHAD's three outputs), the fantasized GP update is the
closed-form rank-one update, and the current value is computed with the same
estimator, so the estimated KG is never negative. pKGFN buys the candidate with
the largest KG per dollar. If KG is numerically zero for every candidate
($<10^{-10}$ per dollar), it buys the atomize, print or test candidate with the
largest network EI per dollar instead, much as fast p-KGFN uses EI to propose
candidates~\cite{buathong2025}. As in the original, pKGFN atomizes only alloys
that have been simulated.

\paragraph{KGFN.} KGFN uses pKGFN's model, pool, candidates and inner set, but
each query runs the whole chain (with CALPHAD if the alloy is new) at the
design attached to one of pKGFN's CALPHAD, atomization or print candidates,
about 52 designs per decision. A fantasy draws one standard normal per node
(32 scrambled-Sobol quasi-Monte Carlo points in six dimensions) and pushes it
down the chain, so that every node gets its fantasized input and value; each
node's GP then takes its own rank-one update. KGFN buys the design with the
largest KG per dollar (\$4,200, or \$4,210 for a new alloy), with the same EI
fallback.

\paragraph{Expected-improvement policies.} BOFN scores 1,024 Sobol designs plus
16 local perturbations around every evaluated design, refines around the eight
best, and runs the whole chain at the design with the largest network EI.
BOFN with powder reuse scores the same new designs at the full-chain cost and
64 laser settings per existing lot at the reprint-and-test cost (\$2,700), and
buys the largest EI per dollar. Standard BO scores 2,048 Sobol designs plus
local perturbations around the five best.

\subsection*{Scoring}

The primary metric is the best alloy demonstrated: the true objective of the
best design that has been printed and tensile-tested so far, divided by $y^*$
at that $\kappa$, recorded after every purchase and read on a \$250 grid of new
spend. A policy gets no credit for designs it has not tested. Per run, we read
it at \$25k, \$50k and \$100k, and average it over the whole \$0--100k grid
(the anytime score, the normalized area under the curve, which needs no choice
of budget). Two secondary metrics are logged: the true value of the printed
design that the policy's own model ranks best, and the argmax of the network
posterior mean over the whole space~\cite{buathong2024}; the latter swings
widely from one \$10 query to the next on this network and is not used for
conclusions.

\subsection*{Statistical protocol}

A seed fixes the shared start, so every policy sees the same five seed alloys
and 32 simulated compositions for a given seed, and comparisons are paired by
seed. All eight policies are run on the same seeds: 20 at $\kappa=1$ and 16 at
$\kappa=0$ and $0.5$. Intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over seeds
(10,000 draws)~\cite{efron1993}. Each draw resamples seeds jointly for every
policy (the matched-seed bootstrap), so a difference between two policies is
always computed on the same seeds. Differences are in percentage points of the
optimum. The speed-up of a policy over a baseline is the spend at which the
baseline's mean curve first reaches its own \$100k mean, divided by the spend at
which the policy's mean curve first reaches it; it is 0 if the policy's mean
never gets there within \$100k~\cite{tom2024}. Wilcoxon signed-rank
$p$-values~\cite{wilcoxon1945} and win counts are reported alongside, without
correction for the number of comparisons.

\subsection*{Reproducibility protocol}

Each run writes one JSON file with every decision (stage, spend and scores
after it), every purchase with its inputs, the run's settings (costs, noise,
Monte Carlo sizes, screening parameters), the git commit and whether the
benchmark's tracked files were modified, the versions of Python, PyTorch,
BoTorch, GPyTorch, linear\_operator, NumPy and SciPy, the CPU model, the
command line and the GP-fit log. \code{compare_runs.py} checks a rerun against
a stored run: they match action for action when every decision has the same
stage at the same cumulative spend, the per-stage counts agree, the purchased
designs agree, and the scores after each purchase agree to within $10^{-9}$.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]

\section*{Method validation}

\subsection*{The implementation computes what it claims}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{KGFN.} A brute-force check (\code{quality/check_kgfn.py}) redoes
every fantasy by appending the fantasized observation to each node's data with
the same hyperparameters, re-solving the GPs and resampling the network. It
agrees with the closed-form KGFN to within $10^{-13}$ (seed 0 at $\kappa=1$ and
seed 3 at $\kappa=0.5$). KG is non-negative, and re-running a design that has
already been through the whole chain is worth zero.
\item \textbf{Monte Carlo noise in pKGFN's knowledge gradient}
(\code{quality/mc_noise.py}). At four decisions of seed 0 ($\kappa=1$), the same
candidates were re-scored with five other draws of 64 base samples and with
1,024 as a reference. The chosen stage was the same at all four decisions and
the chosen design at three. At 64 samples the small KG of atomizing and testing
is underestimated (often exactly zero), which favors printing; the stage choice
was robust where the gap between stages was large. Re-running pKGFN with four
times the base samples (four seeds) or twice the candidates (two seeds) left
its \$100k result and its mix of purchases within the seed-to-seed spread.
\item \textbf{Gaussian processes.} \stat{k1-pkgfn-fitfail} of
\stat{k1-pkgfn-fits} GP fits failed in pKGFN's \stat{k1-n} runs at $\kappa=1$,
and none failed in any policy at any $\kappa$ \tbd{confirm from
paper-stats/allocation.csv after the sweep}. Fitted lengthscales ranged from
0.10 to 132; none sat at the lower bound.
\item \textbf{Independent review.} Before the port, an independent analysis
agent reviewed the code and recomputed every table from the run files without
\code{analyze.py}; the GP posterior matched NumPy and BoTorch to $10^{-15}$
and every table reproduced at its published precision. Its two findings that
changed code (budget-edge checks) are fixed, and the fixes do not change any
stored run. \tbd{Cite the review reports in the repository.}
\end{itemize}

were \stat{k1-su-pkgfn-tiered}$\times$ (\stat{k1-su-pkgfn-tiered-ci95}) and
\stat{k1-su-eifn_reuse-tiered}$\times$ (\stat{k1-su-eifn_reuse-tiered-ci95})
faster, full-chain BOFN and KGFN \stat{k1-su-eifn-tiered}$\times$
(\stat{k1-su-eifn-tiered-ci95}) and \stat{k1-su-kgfn-tiered}$\times$
(\stat{k1-su-kgfn-tiered-ci95}), and standard BO
\stat{k1-su-ei-tiered}$\times$ (\stat{k1-su-ei-tiered-ci95};
\cref{fig:speedup}). The ratio depends on the target: screening's mean creeps
up in its last \$5k, which sets its own crossing at the end of the budget.

\paragraph{The $2\times2$: choosing the stage buys an early lead, not a better
finish.} Under the knowledge gradient, partial evaluation was
\stat{k1-c-0-v25}\pp{} ahead of full-chain evaluation at \$25k (95\% CI
\stat{k1-c-0-v25-ci}), and level by \$100k (\stat{k1-c-0-v100}\pp,
\stat{k1-c-0-v100-ci}). Under expected improvement per dollar the early lead was
\stat{k1-c-1-v25}\pp{} (\stat{k1-c-1-v25-ci}) and the \$100k difference
\stat{k1-c-1-v100}\pp{} (\stat{k1-c-1-v100-ci}). Over the whole budget neither
contrast was distinguishable from zero (anytime \stat{k1-c-0-auc}\pp{} and
\stat{k1-c-1-auc}\pp). The knowledge gradient did not beat expected improvement
per dollar: with partial evaluation it finished \stat{k1-c-2-v100}\pp{} behind
(\stat{k1-c-2-v100-ci}), and with full chains it was \stat{k1-c-3-v50}\pp{}
behind at \$50k (\stat{k1-c-3-v50-ci}) and level at \$100k (\cref{fig:contrasts}).
pKGFN spent its budget on prints of a few powders: per run it bought
\stat{k1-pkgfn-nA} powders, \stat{k1-pkgfn-nL} prints and \stat{k1-pkgfn-nT}
tensile tests, against \stat{k1-eifn_reuse-nA}, \stat{k1-eifn_reuse-nL} and
\stat{k1-eifn_reuse-nT} for BOFN with powder reuse (\cref{fig:allocation}).
A one-step knowledge gradient cannot see that a new powder's value shows only
once it is printed and tested.

\paragraph{Against standard BO.} No network policy reached standard BO's own
\$100k level (\stat{k1-su-ei-target}\% of the optimum) reliably sooner: the
speed-ups over it were between \stat{k1-su-pkgfn-ei} and
\stat{k1-su-eifn-ei}$\times$, and every interval reached down to ``not reached''
(Figure~S2). The gain over screening at $\kappa=1$ is therefore a gain of
modelling the whole chain over trusting the screen; partial evaluation adds an
early lead on top.

\section*{Customization}

The benchmark is meant to be changed. Each of the following is a flag or a
single function, and every change is recorded in the run file, so customized
runs cannot be mistaken for the reference ones.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Costs.} \code{--costs L=1000 T=500} overrides any step's cost.
The case for partial evaluation should grow with the ratio of print to
tensile cost and shrink as printing gets cheaper; the companion article
measures the real ratios.
\item \textbf{Noise.} \code{--noise 0.02}, or per step (\code{--noise A=0.01
L=0.05 T=0.02}), adds Gaussian measurement noise to atomization, printing and
testing, from its own random stream; each node GP is told its noise variance.
CALPHAD stays deterministic, because its error is the bias $\kappa$.
\item \textbf{Screen error and budget.} \code{--kappa} takes any value in
$[0,1]$; \code{--budget} any spend.
\item \textbf{Monte Carlo settings.} \code{BOFN_SACQ} (base samples, 64),
\code{BOFN_NCAND} (candidates per stage, 16) and \code{BOFN_NFANT} (KGFN
fantasies, 32).
\item \textbf{The truth.} \code{AlloyTruth.node} holds the closed forms of
\cref{tab:nodes}; a new workflow needs its nodes, their parents
(\code{SPEC}) and costs.
\item \textbf{A policy.} A policy is a function from the current data and network
model to one purchase. As an example, ``any-alloy'' pKGFN
(\code{pkgfn_free}) lets pKGFN atomize compositions it has never simulated
(CALPHAD runs first, its outputs entering KG at their posterior means) and
values a new powder by atomizing and printing it as one \$4,000 move.
\tbd{Result of pkgfn\_free at $\kappa=0$, 0.5 and 1 from the stored runs (8, 8
and 20 seeds) or from 16-seed reruns if they finish.}
\end{itemize}

Two extensions reuse this truth. A clocked version adds durations, station
capacity, asynchronous decisions, noise, failed powders, builds and bars, and
reports days as well as dollars; a digital twin of the laboratory adds people,
robots and calendars. \tbd{Cite once public (DAL PR 297; byu-vcl twin rounds
3--5).} In both, a fixed pKGFN tied full-chain BOFN, and in the clocked version
the EI fallback made most of pKGFN's decisions, which is consistent with what
this benchmark shows: on this network, choosing the stage helps early, and the
knowledge gradient adds little over expected improvement per dollar.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Synthetic truth.} The node functions mirror the qualitative
metallurgy but are not fitted to data, and $\kappa$ is the one place the
benchmark is built to make screening wrong. The conclusions are about this
network and these costs.
\item \textbf{Simplifications.} Observations are noise-free by default, there
is one objective, one parameter set per build (real plates carry many coupons,
which would favor partial evaluation further), no time or capacity, and no
failed runs.
\item \textbf{pKGFN as implemented.} It is discrete (candidates come from
EI-ranked pools; the inner maximum runs over an anchored set), one-step myopic,
restricted to simulated alloys, and uses a 2-point-per-output product rule for
CALPHAD's three outputs, which gets the fourth moment wrong. Its results moved
when the candidate rules changed. They are results of this implementation at
these settings, not of p-KGFN in general.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} With 16--20 seeds, percentile bootstrap intervals
can under-cover; the speed-up statistic is the first \$250 step a mean curve
crosses, so its bootstrap distribution is lumpy and its interval endpoints move
by about 0.1 between bootstrap seeds. Intervals are not corrected for the
number of comparisons.
\item \textbf{Bit-level reproducibility} has been checked on Linux x86\_64
only, on three AMD EPYC models. Other CPUs and BLAS builds may diverge in
the last digits and, through a different decision, beyond.
\end{itemize}
